# Complementary & Natural Medicine for Crohn's Disease — Evidence Reference

> Claude-facing reference file. Technical density prioritized over user-friendliness.
> Framing: "What does the evidence actually say?" — no boosterism, no dismissiveness.
> Evidence grades: **[Strong]** = multiple well-powered RCTs; **[Moderate]** = at least one positive RCT or consistent supporting data; **[Weak]** = small/inconsistent trials, mechanistic only, or negative RCTs that don't rule out niche benefit; **[Anecdotal]** = case reports, uncontrolled series, preclinical only.
> Most IBD complementary evidence is for **ulcerative colitis (UC)**. CD-specific evidence is thinner. Flag this when extrapolating.

## 1. Curcumin / Turmeric

### Evidence Grade
- **[Moderate]** for ulcerative colitis (induction + maintenance adjunct to mesalamine)
- **[Moderate]** for Crohn's disease (one positive RCT with high-bioavailability form; one negative postoperative RCT)

### Mechanism
Curcumin is the principal polyphenol (curcuminoid) in *Curcuma longa* (turmeric). Mechanistic actions relevant to IBD:
- **NF-κB inhibition** — blocks IκB kinase, preventing NF-κB nuclear translocation; downregulates TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, COX-2, iNOS transcription.
- **STAT3 inhibition** — reduces Th17 differentiation.
- **Nrf2 activation** — upregulates antioxidant response elements (HO-1, NQO1).
- **5-LOX / COX-2 inhibition** — reduces leukotriene/prostaglandin synthesis.
- **TLR4 signaling modulation.**
- **Microbiome effects** — small human studies suggest modest alteration of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratios.

### Key RCTs (Real PMIDs verified)

**Ulcerative Colitis — MAINTENANCE**
- **Hanai H, et al. 2006** — *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 4(12):1502-6. **PMID: 17101300**. Double-blind, multicenter, placebo-controlled RCT, 89 patients with quiescent UC on mesalamine/sulfasalazine. Curcumin **2 g/day × 6 months**. Relapse: **4.65% curcumin vs 20.51% placebo** (p=0.04). Significant reduction in clinical activity index and endoscopic index.

**Ulcerative Colitis — INDUCTION (adjunct)**
- **Lang A, et al. 2015** — *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 13(8):1444-9. **PMID: 25724700**. Multicenter RCT, 50 patients with mild-to-moderate active UC on mesalamine. Curcumin **3 g/day × 1 month**. Clinical remission: **53.8% curcumin + mesalamine vs 0% placebo + mesalamine** (p=0.01). Endoscopic remission: 38% vs 0%.
- **Kedia S, et al. 2017** — Indian RCT of curcumin 450 mg/day — **negative** (no benefit over placebo). Likely dose too low given bioavailability issues.

**Crohn's Disease — INDUCTION**
- **Sugimoto K, et al. 2020** — *J Crohns Colitis* 14(12):1693-1701. **PMID: 32412598**. Randomized, double-blind, multicenter (5 Japanese sites). **Theracurmin® 360 mg/day × 12 weeks** (highly bioavailable nanoparticle form, ~27x absorption of standard curcumin). n=30 (20 active, 10 placebo) with mild-to-moderate CD. Clinical remission at week 12: **40% vs 0%** (p=0.02). Endoscopic remission: 15% vs 0%. Significant healing of anal lesions at week 8. No serious adverse events. **This is the cleanest CD-specific curcumin RCT to date.**

**Crohn's Disease — POSTOPERATIVE PROPHYLAXIS (NEGATIVE)**
- **Bommelaer G, et al. 2020** — *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 18(7):1553-1560. **PMID: 31470175**. French multicenter double-blind RCT, 62 post-resection CD patients on azathioprine. Oral curcumin **3 g/day × 6 months**. Postoperative recurrence (Rutgeerts ≥i2): **58% curcumin vs 68% placebo** (p=0.60, NS). Concerning signal: **severe recurrence (Rutgeerts ≥i3) 55% curcumin vs 26% placebo** (p=0.034) — possibly chance with small n, but worth noting. SAEs 16% vs 6%. Honest conclusion: curcumin did NOT prevent postoperative recurrence.

### Bioavailability — The Critical Issue
Unformulated curcumin has ~1% oral bioavailability due to poor aqueous solubility, rapid glucuronidation/sulfation, and biliary excretion. Almost all serum concentrations achievable with plain turmeric powder are sub-therapeutic for anti-inflammatory effect.

**Enhancement strategies:**
- **Piperine** (black pepper alkaloid) — Shoba et al. 1998 (PMID: 9619120) showed piperine 20 mg boosts curcumin bioavailability ~2000% (20-fold) in humans via glucuronidation inhibition. Most classic "curcumin + piperine" products use this. However, 2025 pharmacokinetic reappraisal suggests benefit may be overstated when compared to modern formulations. Also — piperine itself inhibits CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein, potentially affecting other drug levels.
- **Meriva®** (curcumin-phosphatidylcholine phytosome) — ~29x bioavailability vs standard curcumin. Best evidence in osteoarthritis, some IBD signal.
- **Theracurmin®** — colloidal nanoparticle dispersion, ~27x bioavailability. Used in Sugimoto 2020 CD trial.
- **BCM-95/Curcugreen** — turmeric essential oil + curcumin, ~7-10x bioavailability.
- **Longvida®** — solid lipid curcumin particle, ~65x bioavailability for free curcumin.
- **Nanocurcumin** (liposomal) — variable evidence.

### Effective Dose
- **Standard curcumin**: 2-3 g/day (with piperine 5-20 mg) — this is the Hanai/Lang regimen
- **Theracurmin**: 360 mg/day (much lower mg due to higher absorption) — Sugimoto regimen
- **Meriva**: 500-1000 mg twice daily

### Safety & Risks
**Hepatotoxicity (emerging concern):**
- **Likoudis P, et al. 2022** — *Am J Med* 135(11):1340-1347. **PMID: 36252717**. DILIN case series: **10 cases of turmeric-associated liver injury**, 8 women, median age 56. 9 hepatocellular pattern, 1 mixed. **5 hospitalized, 1 death from acute liver failure.** 7 of 10 carried **HLA-B*35:01** allele (same allele implicated in green tea, *Polygonum multiflorum*, and *Garcinia cambogia* hepatotoxicity). 3 of 7 tested products contained piperine — suggesting piperine-enhanced absorption may increase hepatotoxic risk.
- NIH LiverTox now lists turmeric as the **most common cause of herbal-related liver injury in the US** (as of 2022).
- Latency typically weeks to months. Usually resolves with discontinuation but can be severe.

**Bleeding risk:**
- Curcumin has antiplatelet and mild anticoagulant effects (decreases thromboxane A2, may inhibit platelet aggregation).
- Multiple case reports of INR elevation in patients on warfarin taking turmeric — one report of INR >10.
- Avoid or closely monitor in patients on warfarin, DOACs, heparin, antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel), or perioperatively (stop 2 weeks before surgery).

**GI effects:**
- Doses >4 g/day may cause nausea, diarrhea, reflux.
- Can increase bile flow — caution with gallstones, bile duct obstruction.

**Other:**
- Oxalate content — may increase kidney stone risk in predisposed individuals.
- Iron chelation — may worsen iron-deficiency anemia with high-dose chronic use.
- Theoretical: CYP3A4, CYP2D6, P-gp modulation — may affect many drugs.

### Drug Interactions (Curcumin)
- **Warfarin** — increased INR, bleeding
- **Antiplatelets** — additive bleeding
- **Tacrolimus, cyclosporine** — possible level changes via CYP3A4
- **Chemotherapy** — varies (paradoxically may enhance some; antagonize others)
- **Iron supplements** — reduced absorption

### Practical Bottom Line
**For UC**: Reasonable adjunct to mesalamine — decent evidence for maintenance (Hanai) and induction (Lang).
**For CD**: Theracurmin at 360 mg/day has the best CD-specific data. Standard curcumin 2-3 g/day is reasonable but less validated. **Do not use in lieu of induction therapy for moderate-severe CD.** Monitor LFTs every 3-6 months with chronic use. Avoid with anticoagulants without physician oversight. **Stop immediately if fatigue, jaundice, dark urine, or RUQ pain.**

## 2. Omega-3 Fatty Acids (EPA/DHA)

### Evidence Grade
**[Weak]** for CD disease activity / remission maintenance. Two large well-powered RCTs failed to show benefit. Some mechanistic rationale persists; general cardiovascular/mental health benefits remain.

### Mechanism
- **EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid)** — precursor to series-3 prostaglandins and series-5 leukotrienes (less inflammatory than arachidonic acid-derived series-2 prostaglandins and series-4 leukotrienes).
- **DHA (docosahexaenoic acid)** — structural membrane lipid, precursor to resolvins and protectins (specialized pro-resolving mediators — SPMs).
- **PPAR-γ activation, NF-κB inhibition, reduced endotoxin-induced TNF-α.**
- **Substrate competition** with arachidonic acid at COX/LOX enzymes.

### Landmark Trials — The EPIC Disappointment

**EPIC-1 and EPIC-2 — Feagan BG, et al. 2008** — *JAMA* 299(14):1690-7. **PMID: 18398081**.
- Two parallel multicenter (98 sites, Canada/Europe/Israel/US), double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs. Total n=738 (EPIC-1: 363, EPIC-2: 375) CD patients in remission.
- Enteric-coated **omega-3 free fatty acids 4 g/day** (2.2 g EPA + 0.8 g DHA) × up to 58 weeks.
- EPIC-1 relapse at 1 year: **31.6% omega-3 vs 35.7% placebo** (p=0.30).
- EPIC-2 relapse at 1 year: **47.8% omega-3 vs 48.8% placebo** (p=0.48).
- **Omega-3 did NOT maintain remission in CD.** Definitive, well-powered, negative.

**Cochrane Review — Lev-Tzion R, et al. 2014** — *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* **PMID: 24585498** / also **PMID: 8988157**. Systematic review of 6 RCTs (n=1,039). Conclusion: Omega-3 fatty acids are **probably ineffective** for maintenance of remission in CD. GI adverse events (diarrhea, dyspepsia) and fish-burp more common.

Earlier contradictory signal: Belluzzi A, et al. 1996 (*NEJM*) — smaller enteric-coated fish oil trial showed ~40% reduction in relapse — but not replicated in EPIC trials with larger n and better methodology.

### Dose
If used (for cardiometabolic/general anti-inflammatory reasons, not CD-specific):
- **1-4 g/day combined EPA+DHA**
- Fish oil at 4 g/day is approved (Lovaza/Vascepa) for hypertriglyceridemia
- Food equivalent: **2-3 servings fatty fish/week** (salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring, anchovies)
- Vegan sources: algae-derived DHA/EPA (~300-600 mg/day); ALA from flax/chia/walnuts has poor conversion (~0.5-5% to EPA, <0.5% to DHA)

### Safety
- **Bleeding risk** — dose-dependent antiplatelet effect; clinically minor at typical doses but avoid high dose with anticoagulants or perioperatively.
- **Fishy aftertaste, eructation** — mitigated with enteric coating, freezing capsules, taking with meals.
- **Atrial fibrillation** — recent signal at high doses (>4 g/day, as in REDUCE-IT) — unclear if relevant for CD context.
- **Heavy metal/PCB contamination** — use third-party tested brands (IFOS certified).
- **Oxidation/rancidity** — check peroxide/TOTOX values; don't use if fishy-smelling capsule.

### Drug Interactions
- **Warfarin / DOACs / antiplatelets** — additive bleeding risk at >3 g/day
- **Antihypertensives** — mild additive BP lowering

### Practical Bottom Line
**Do NOT rely on omega-3 to maintain CD remission** — EPIC disproved this. Reasonable as part of dietary pattern (Mediterranean-style eating) for general cardiometabolic health. If supplementing, 1-2 g/day EPA+DHA is reasonable and safe. Eating oily fish 2-3x/week is probably the best approach. **This is not a disease-modifying intervention for CD.**

## 3. Probiotics

### Evidence Grade
- **[Strong]** for pouchitis (VSL#3/Visbiome)
- **[Strong]** for UC maintenance (E. coli Nissle 1917, VSL#3)
- **[Weak]** for CD induction or maintenance (mostly negative trials)

### General Principle
Probiotic effects are **strain-specific, dose-specific, and indication-specific.** "Probiotics" as a category is a meaningless classification. A strain validated for pouchitis does not automatically help CD.

### VSL#3 / Visbiome (8-strain high-dose mixture)
Composition: *L. paracasei, L. plantarum, L. acidophilus, L. delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus, B. longum, B. breve, B. infantis, S. thermophilus*. 450 billion to 900 billion CFU per dose. Note: **Visbiome contains the De Simone Formulation** — the original VSL#3 formulation. After a 2016 patent dispute, VSL#3 (as currently sold in US) no longer contains the original formulation; **Visbiome = the authentic original formula**. Evidence grades below apply to the De Simone formulation only.

- **Pouchitis prevention after IPAA** — Gionchetti P, et al. 2003 (*Gastroenterology*): ~85% maintained remission on VSL#3 vs ~6% placebo. Strongest probiotic evidence in IBD-adjacent territory. PMID: 12883464 (related maintenance trial). AGA and ECCO guidelines endorse.
- **Pouchitis maintenance** (Mimura T, et al. 2004): also positive.
- **Active mild-moderate UC** — Sood A, et al. 2009 / Tursi A, et al. 2010: VSL#3 superior to placebo as adjunct to standard therapy.
- **CD postoperative recurrence** (Fedorak RN, et al. 2015, *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 13(5):928-35, PMID: 25460016): RCT n=119. Day 90 endoscopic recurrence: **9.3% VSL#3 vs 15.7% placebo (NS)**. Anti-inflammatory cytokine signal in mucosal biopsies. Not statistically significant for primary endpoint. Cannot recommend routinely for CD prophylaxis.

**Dose**: 2-8 sachets/day (900 billion - 3.6 trillion CFU). Refrigeration required for viability.

### E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) — "Mutaflor"
Non-pathogenic Gram-negative *E. coli* strain isolated 1917.

- **Kruis W, et al. 2004** — *Gut* 53(11):1617-23. **PMID: 15479682**. RCT n=327 UC patients. EcN 200 mg/day (2.5-25 × 10^9 CFU) vs mesalazine 500 mg TID × 12 months. **Equivalent** in maintaining remission. Both ~45% relapse rate.
- Earlier Rembacken et al. 1999 (*Lancet*): similar equivalence.
- Currently **endorsed by ECCO as evidence-based alternative to mesalazine for UC maintenance**.
- No convincing evidence for CD.
- Approved in Germany, Austria, parts of EU; NOT FDA-approved in US (available as supplement through international pharmacies).

### Saccharomyces boulardii
Non-pathogenic yeast (technically *S. cerevisiae* var. *boulardii*).

- **Guslandi M, et al. 2000** — *Dig Dis Sci* 45(7):1462-4. **PMID: 10961730**. Small RCT n=32 CD in remission. 6-month relapse: 37.5% mesalamine alone vs **6.25% mesalamine + S. boulardii 1 g/day** (p<0.05). Positive but tiny sample.
- **Bourreille A, et al. 2013** — *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 11(8):982-7. **PMID: 23466709**. Larger RCT n=165 CD patients in remission after steroid/salicylate induction. 52-week relapse: **47.5% S. boulardii vs 53.2% placebo** (NS). **Negative — did not replicate Guslandi finding.**
- Best use: adjunctive for antibiotic-associated diarrhea and *C. difficile* (outside IBD). Not for CD maintenance.
- Caution: case reports of **fungemia in immunocompromised patients** — avoid in those on high-dose immunosuppression, central venous catheters, or critical illness.

### Lactobacillus GG (L. rhamnosus GG)
- **Prantera C, et al. 2002** — *Gut* 51(3):405-9. **PMID: 12171964**. RCT n=45 post-resection CD. LGG 12 billion CFU/day × 12 months. Endoscopic recurrence: **60% LGG vs 35.2% placebo (NS, trend adverse).** Clinical recurrence 16.6% vs 10.5%. **Negative.**
- **Bousvaros A, et al. 2005** (pediatric CD maintenance): also negative — LGG no better than placebo.
- **Schultz M, et al. 2004** (CD induction): negative.
- Probably not useful for CD.

### Lactobacillus johnsonii LA1
- **Marteau P, et al. 2006** (GETAID trial). **PMID: 16377775**. Postoperative CD prophylaxis — negative.
- **Van Gossum A, et al. 2007**: also negative.

### Bifidobacterium/Lactobacillus mixtures — general
- Multiple trials mixed/negative for CD. No strain mixture has convincing CD evidence.

### Cochrane/Meta-analytic Summary
For CD: Probiotics have **NO proven benefit** for induction OR maintenance of remission. (Crohn's & Colitis Foundation, AGA, ECCO all concur.)

### Safety
- Generally safe in immunocompetent patients.
- **Avoid in**: severe immunosuppression, central venous catheter, critical illness, short bowel with translocation risk, post-resection with active leak.
- Rare: bacteremia/fungemia, especially with S. boulardii.

### Practical Bottom Line
**CD patient looking for a probiotic?** Honest answer: no strong evidence any currently available probiotic modifies disease course. Low-harm interventions for general gut function include fermented foods (kefir, yogurt, sauerkraut, kimchi — if tolerated) over capsule supplements. If considering VSL#3/Visbiome it's because of pouchitis adjacent rationale; the CD post-op data is underwhelming but not frankly negative. **Do not substitute for medical therapy.**

## 4. Boswellia serrata

### Evidence Grade
**[Weak-to-Moderate]** — one positive equivalence trial vs mesalazine (Gerhardt), one negative maintenance trial vs placebo (Holtmeier). Mechanistically plausible.

### Mechanism
Gum resin (frankincense) of *Boswellia serrata*. Active compounds = **boswellic acids** (β-BA, KBA, AKBA — acetyl-11-keto-β-boswellic acid being most potent).
- **5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) inhibition** — blocks leukotriene B4 synthesis (major neutrophil chemotactic/inflammatory mediator in IBD mucosa).
- **NF-κB inhibition.**
- **Topoisomerase inhibition.**
- **Cathepsin G inhibition.**

### Key Trials
**Gerhardt H, et al. 2001** — *Z Gastroenterol* 39(1):11-7. **PMID: 11215357**. German randomized, double-blind, verum-controlled (vs mesalazine) trial. Active CD, n=102 randomized (44 per-protocol H15, 39 mesalazine). H15 extract dosed, **CDAI reduction: -90 H15 vs -53 mesalazine** (non-inferior to superior). Favorable safety/efficacy balance. Limitation: compared to mesalazine (modest CD activity) rather than placebo; small PP population.

**Holtmeier W, et al. 2011** — *Inflamm Bowel Dis* 17(2):573-82. **PMID: 20848527**. Multicenter German RCT, new Boswellia preparation "Boswelan" for **maintenance** of CD remission vs placebo × 52 weeks. n=108. Relapse: no significant difference. **Good safety but no efficacy vs placebo for maintenance.**

**Gupta I, et al. 1997/2001** — older smaller open trials of chronic colitis (UC or indeterminate) showed remission rates comparable to sulfasalazine (PMID for 1997: 9049593). Methodologically weak by modern standards.

### Dose
- **H15 extract**: 350-400 mg TID (~1050-1200 mg/day of standardized extract)
- Look for **AKBA-standardized** products (≥10% AKBA, ≥30-40% boswellic acids total)
- **5-Loxin®** (30% AKBA) and **ApresFlex®** (licensed form) — most studied modern standardized forms
- Take with food (fat-soluble)

### Safety
- Generally well tolerated
- Mild GI upset, rash, headache
- Rare elevated LFTs
- **Potential drug interactions**: CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, P-gp modulation — theoretical interactions with statins, warfarin, chemotherapy
- **Pregnancy**: avoid (possible uterine stimulation)

### Practical Bottom Line
Mechanistically attractive (LTB4 is highly relevant in IBD neutrophilic inflammation). One positive active-CD trial, one negative maintenance trial. Reasonable adjunct for **mild-to-moderate active CD** at 350-400 mg TID of standardized extract. **Not a substitute for biologics in moderate-severe disease.** Generally safer than NSAIDs as LTB4-axis anti-inflammatory.

## 5. Cannabis / CBD / THC

### Evidence Grade
**[Moderate]** for **symptomatic improvement** (pain, appetite, sleep, quality of life, Crohn's Disease Activity Index scores). **[Weak]** for **disease modification** (endoscopic/mucosal healing) — repeatedly negative on objective inflammation markers.

### Mechanism
- Endocannabinoid system (ECS) is expressed throughout GI tract. CB1 receptors on enteric neurons modulate motility, secretion, visceral pain. CB2 receptors on immune cells modulate cytokine production.
- **Δ9-THC** — CB1/CB2 partial agonist. Anti-inflammatory, antinociceptive, anti-emetic, appetite-stimulating, psychoactive.
- **CBD** — non-psychoactive. Weak CB1/CB2 affinity, but modulates multiple targets: 5-HT1A, TRPV1, PPAR-γ, adenosine A2A, GPR55. Anti-inflammatory, anxiolytic.
- Inhibits FAAH (increases endogenous anandamide).
- Effects on gut permeability and epithelial barrier shown in vitro.

### Key Trials (Naftali and colleagues — Israeli group)

**Naftali T, et al. 2013** — *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol* 11(10):1276-80. **PMID: 23648372**. First RCT of medical cannabis in CD. n=21 with CDAI >200, refractory to steroids/immunomodulators/anti-TNF. **Cannabis cigarettes 115 mg THC BID × 8 weeks** vs placebo (THC-extracted flowers).
- CDAI reduction: 45% achieved >100 point reduction in cannabis group vs 10% placebo (p=0.05)
- Clinical remission (CDAI <150): **45% cannabis vs 10% placebo** (NS, p=0.43 — missed primary endpoint)
- 5 of 11 cannabis patients weaned off steroids vs 1 of 10 placebo
- Significant improvement in sleep, appetite, general well-being
- No changes in CRP

**Naftali T, et al. 2017** — *Dig Dis Sci* (Low-dose CBD). **PMID: 28349233**. RCT of **CBD 10 mg BID × 8 weeks** in active CD. n=20. Low dose. No significant difference vs placebo on any outcome. Safe, but ineffective at this dose.

**Naftali T, et al. 2021** — *J Crohns Colitis* 15(11):1799-1806. **PMID: 33858011**. Oral CBD-rich cannabis oil (160 mg CBD / 40 mg THC per ml, dosed orally) × 8 weeks in active CD. n=56. **Clinical remission (CDAI reduction + Harvey-Bradshaw): 65% cannabis vs 35% placebo (p=0.02)**. But **no significant endoscopic improvement** (SES-CD): cannabis-induced clinical but NOT endoscopic remission. Reinforced the symptomatic-vs-disease-modifying distinction.

**Irving PM, et al. 2018** (UC, not CD): CBD-rich extract in UC — failed primary endpoint but reduced subjective symptoms.

**Kafil TS, et al. 2018** (Cochrane Review): Insufficient evidence to recommend cannabis for CD induction or maintenance. Symptomatic benefit acknowledged but disease-modifying unproven.

### Observational Data
**Naftali T, et al. 2011** (*Isr Med Assoc J*, PMID: 21910367): Observational study of 30 patients using cannabis for CD. Reduced disease activity, medication use, hospitalizations. Subject to confounding but consistent signal of symptomatic benefit.

### Dosing Approaches (Empirical)
- **CBD alone**: start 10-25 mg BID, titrate to 25-50 mg BID or higher (up to 300-600 mg/day in epilepsy dosing)
- **CBD:THC ratios**: 20:1 (low psychoactive), 10:1, 4:1, 1:1 (balanced) — higher THC for pain/appetite/sleep, more CBD for anti-inflammatory/anxiolytic
- **THC**: start very low (2.5-5 mg), especially if cannabis-naïve
- Oral (capsules, oils) = long onset (1-3 hours), long duration (6-10 hours), hepatic metabolism produces 11-OH-THC (more psychoactive than THC)
- Sublingual = faster onset (15-45 min)
- Inhaled/vaporized = fastest (5-15 min), short duration — useful for breakthrough symptoms. Avoid smoking (combustion byproducts worsen bowel disease and general health).

### Safety
- **Acute**: dizziness, dry mouth, drowsiness, palpitations, dysphoria/anxiety (especially with high THC), impaired coordination
- **Chronic**: dependence (~9% of users, up to 25-50% with daily heavy use), cognitive effects (especially in adolescents — avoid under age 25), cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (rare, paradoxical), amotivation
- **Psychosis**: caution in personal/family history of schizophrenia or psychosis
- **Pregnancy**: avoid (developmental risks)
- **Cardiovascular**: tachycardia, small increased MI/stroke risk signal in heavy users
- **Hepatotoxicity**: CBD rare dose-dependent LFT elevations (seen in Epidiolex trials at high dose)

### Drug Interactions
**CBD and THC are substrates and inhibitors of multiple CYPs** — clinically relevant at higher doses:
- **CYP3A4**: inhibited by CBD — watch tacrolimus (case reports of toxicity), cyclosporine, certain statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin), certain BZDs (midazolam), apixaban/rivaroxaban
- **CYP2C9**: inhibited by CBD — **warfarin interaction documented**, case report INR 12
- **CYP2C19**: inhibited by CBD — clopidogrel (may reduce activation), PPIs
- **CYP1A2**: THC metabolism — smoking induces (less relevant to medical use)
- Clobazam levels rise substantially with CBD co-administration
- Immunosuppressive biologics + cannabis = no direct PK interaction but cannabis has mild immunomodulatory effects — coordinate with prescribing specialist

Threshold for clinically significant: **THC >30 mg/day, CBD >300 mg/day** — lower doses generally safer.

### Practical Bottom Line
Sean's situation (20-year post-resection CD, natural health interest): cannabis is reasonable for **symptomatic management** (pain, sleep, appetite, anxiety, nausea) but should not replace maintenance immunomodulation if he's on it. Start low and go slow. CBD (10-25 mg BID) first; add THC if inadequate. Know local legality. Watch for CYP interactions if on biologics, azathioprine, warfarin. **Don't expect it to heal the bowel — expect it to make you feel better while other therapies do the actual work.**

## 6. Vitamin D

### Evidence Grade
**[Strong]** for deficiency correction. **[Moderate]** for immunomodulation/IBD-course modification at higher targets.

### Mechanism (beyond bone)
- Active metabolite **1,25(OH)₂D₃** (calcitriol) binds VDR (nuclear receptor) — expressed on T cells, B cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, intestinal epithelium.
- **Immune effects**: shifts T cell balance away from Th1/Th17 (relevant to CD pathogenesis) toward Tregs; reduces IL-17, IFN-γ, TNF-α; enhances antimicrobial peptide production (cathelicidin, defensins); strengthens epithelial tight junctions.
- **Barrier function**: upregulates claudins, occludin in gut epithelium.
- **Microbiome modulation**: effects on dysbiosis shown in preclinical models.
- **NOD2 expression regulation** — NOD2 is the most replicated CD susceptibility gene.
- **Vitamin D receptor polymorphisms** (FokI, TaqI, BsmI) associated with IBD risk.

### Evidence in IBD
- **Prevalence**: 25(OH)D deficiency (<20 ng/mL or 50 nmol/L) in 30-80% of IBD patients; higher in active disease, post-resection, winter months.
- **Observational data** (multiple): lower 25(OH)D correlates with disease activity, relapse rate, surgery risk, anti-TNF failure, healthcare utilization.
- **Ananthakrishnan AN, et al. 2012**: lower 25(OH)D associated with higher IBD-related surgery and hospitalization.
- **Jørgensen SP, et al. 2010** — *Aliment Pharmacol Ther*. Small RCT of 1200 IU/day in CD maintenance → reduced relapse rate (29% to 13%, p=0.06, trend).
- **Yang L, et al. 2013**: higher doses (5000 IU/day) more effective at achieving therapeutic levels in CD.
- **Winter et al. 2017** (IBD Vitamin D Collaborative): cohort of 101 patients — pretreatment 25(OH)D ≥75 nmol/L predicted durable anti-TNF response.
- **Meta-analyses** (2018-2022): vitamin D supplementation reduces CD relapse rates and improves quality of life; heterogeneity across trials.

### Target Levels
Most IBD experts target **25(OH)D 40-60 ng/mL (100-150 nmol/L)** — higher than the general-population threshold (20-30 ng/mL / 50-75 nmol/L).

### Dose
- **Loading** (if deficient <20 ng/mL): 50,000 IU D3 weekly × 8-12 weeks, then maintenance.
- **Maintenance**: 2,000-5,000 IU D3/day — titrate based on 25(OH)D levels every 3-6 months.
- **Post-resection / malabsorption**: often needs higher doses (5,000-10,000 IU/day) or D3 + fat co-administration or sublingual.
- D3 (cholecalciferol) preferred over D2 (ergocalciferol) for potency and half-life.
- Co-supplement with **vitamin K2 (MK-7 100-200 mcg/day)** and **magnesium (300-400 mg/day)** — K2 directs calcium to bones, Mg required for D activation.

### Safety
- **Hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, nephrolithiasis** — generally at >10,000 IU/day chronic or toxic doses. Monitor calcium, creatinine, 25(OH)D.
- Do NOT exceed 100 ng/mL (250 nmol/L) — hypercalcemia risk rises.
- Sarcoidosis/granulomatous diseases — caution, endogenous conversion to active form can cause hypercalcemia at lower doses.

### Drug Interactions
- **Thiazide diuretics** — hypercalcemia risk
- **Corticosteroids** — reduce vitamin D effectiveness on bone
- **Cholestyramine, orlistat, mineral oil** — reduce absorption
- **Anticonvulsants, rifampin** — increase catabolism

### Practical Bottom Line
**Highest-yield supplement for Sean post-resection.** Get baseline 25(OH)D, start 2,000-5,000 IU D3/day, recheck at 3 months, titrate to **50-60 ng/mL**. Take with fatty meal. Add K2 (MK-7 100 mcg) and magnesium. This isn't "alternative medicine" — it's borderline-standard-of-care for IBD now.

## 7. Wheatgrass Juice

### Evidence Grade
**[Weak]** — one small positive RCT in **UC only**, no CD data.

### The Single RCT
**Ben-Arye E, et al. 2002** — *Scand J Gastroenterol* 37(4):444-9. **PMID: 11989836**. Israeli double-blind placebo-controlled RCT in **active distal UC** (not CD). n=23 (small). **100 mL fresh wheatgrass juice/day × 1 month** vs placebo.
- Disease Activity Index decreased significantly (p=0.03)
- Rectal bleeding decreased (p=0.03)
- Physician global assessment improved (p=0.03)
- Side effects: nausea common; otherwise well tolerated

### Mechanism (speculated)
- Chlorophyll, antioxidants (polyphenols, flavonoids), superoxide dismutase (though most is destroyed by stomach acid)
- No convincing mechanistic data specific to IBD

### Dose
If used: 100 mL fresh juice daily (as in Ben-Arye trial). Fresh > powdered.

### Safety
- Generally safe; nausea common
- Mold/bacterial contamination risk in improperly grown wheatgrass — reliable source important
- Wheat allergy — despite being "before grain formation," cross-reactivity possible; caution with celiac

### Practical Bottom Line
One small positive trial in UC; no data in CD. Low risk. **Don't oversell.** Reasonable adjunct if patient enjoys and tolerates it — unlikely to hurt, modest plausibility of benefit.

## 8. Aloe Vera

### Evidence Grade
**[Weak]** — one positive trial in **UC only**. **[CONTRAINDICATED latex form in CD]**.

### Critical Distinction
- **Aloe gel** (inner leaf parenchyma) — the mostly-water, mucilaginous clear gel
- **Aloe latex** (yellow sap under leaf skin) — contains **anthraquinones** (aloin, aloe-emodin, barbaloin) — potent **stimulant laxatives**

### The Only Positive IBD Trial
**Langmead L, et al. 2004** — *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 19(7):739-47. **PMID: 15043514**. Oxford double-blind placebo-controlled RCT in **mild-moderate active UC**. n=44 (2:1 ratio). **Aloe vera gel 100 mL BID × 4 weeks**.
- Clinical remission: 30% aloe vs 7% placebo (NS)
- Clinical improvement: 47% aloe vs 14% placebo (p<0.05)
- Sigmoidoscopic and histologic improvements favored aloe (NS for endoscopic)
- Mixed result — trend positive but underpowered

### Mechanism (Gel, decolorized, aloin-removed)
- Polysaccharides (acemannan) — immunomodulatory, possible barrier effects
- Anti-inflammatory (reduces NF-κB, TNF-α in animal colitis models)
- Antioxidant (anthraquinones paradoxically involved in this at non-laxative doses)

### DANGER — Aloe Latex / Whole Leaf / Aloin-Containing Preparations
- Anthraquinones → stimulate colonic motility, electrolyte/water secretion
- Can cause **cramping, dehydration, hypokalemia** (→ cardiac arrhythmias), melanosis coli with chronic use
- **FDA banned aloe anthraquinones from OTC laxatives in 2002** (insufficient safety data)
- **Explicitly contraindicated in Crohn's disease, UC, bowel obstruction, appendicitis** — worsens diarrhea and inflammation
- Whole-leaf extracts and many supermarket "aloe juices" contain variable aloin — check label for **"decolorized," "aloin-free," or "inner leaf only" (<10 ppm aloin)**
- IARC classifies non-decolorized whole-leaf aloe extract as **possible carcinogen (group 2B)** based on rodent colon tumor data

### Dose (IF used)
- Decolorized inner-leaf gel, 100 mL BID (Langmead regimen)
- Drink form must be labeled aloin-free

### Drug Interactions
- **Digoxin** — hypokalemia from latex worsens toxicity
- **Diuretics** — additive K+ loss
- **Warfarin** — possible INR effects
- **Diabetes meds** — possible hypoglycemic additive effect

### Practical Bottom Line
If experimenting, **ONLY use decolorized, aloin-free inner-leaf gel**. Avoid whole-leaf extracts, aloe "juices" of unclear provenance, any aloe laxative products. For CD specifically: **limited evidence, meaningful risk if wrong formulation**. Skip-ability is high. If tempted, prefer demulcent plants with cleaner safety (slippery elm, marshmallow root).

## 9. Slippery Elm / DGL (Licorice)

### Evidence Grade
**[Anecdotal-to-Weak]** — traditional use, mechanistic rationale, no RCT evidence specific to CD.

### Slippery Elm (*Ulmus rubra*)
- Inner bark powder, high mucilage content
- **Mechanism**: forms viscous gel on mucosal surfaces, mechanical protection, possible mild anti-inflammatory. Not systemically absorbed.
- **Evidence**: one small uncontrolled trial in UC (Langmead et al. 2002) of herbal combinations including slippery elm showed some benefit. No RCT. Traditional Native American use for GI/respiratory ailments well documented.
- **Dose**: 400-500 mg capsules 2-3x/day, OR 1-2 teaspoons powder mixed in water as slurry.
- **Safety**: generally safe; may delay absorption of concomitant oral meds (take 1-2 hours apart). Endangered tree species in some regions — ethical sourcing concern.

### Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice (DGL)
- *Glycyrrhiza glabra* root with glycyrrhizin removed (to avoid mineralocorticoid effects — hypertension, hypokalemia, edema).
- **Mechanism**: increases mucin production, may inhibit H. pylori, antioxidant. Effects primarily upper GI (stomach/duodenum) rather than colon.
- **Evidence**: older trials in peptic ulcer disease (pre-PPI era) showed modest benefit. No IBD-specific RCT.
- **Dose**: 380-760 mg chewed 20 min before meals (buccal absorption).
- **Safety (DGL)**: generally safe. **WHOLE licorice or regular licorice root** (glycyrrhizin-containing): pseudoaldosteronism, HTN, hypokalemia — avoid if >100 mg glycyrrhizin/day or chronic use.

### Drug Interactions
- **Whole licorice**: diuretics (hypokalemia), digoxin, antihypertensives, corticosteroids (potentiation), warfarin
- **DGL**: minimal

### Practical Bottom Line
Both are low-risk mucosal soothers with weak evidence. Slippery elm more relevant for lower GI / IBD context. DGL more for dyspepsia/gastritis. Unlikely to be disease-modifying but reasonable for symptom palliation. **Use DGL specifically — never whole licorice extract chronically.**

## 10. Bromelain

### Evidence Grade
**[Anecdotal/Preclinical]** — promising mechanistic and animal data; no IBD RCT in humans.

### Mechanism
- Proteolytic enzyme mixture from pineapple stem/fruit (*Ananas comosus*)
- Systemic anti-inflammatory when taken on empty stomach (gets absorbed somewhat intact)
- Reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) — demonstrated in colon biopsies ex vivo
- **Cleaves TNF receptors**, reducing signaling
- Reduces leukocyte migration, thrombin generation
- Fibrinolytic activity

### Evidence
- **Hale LP, et al. 2005** (PMID: 15936249): Oral bromelain reduced colitis in IL-10 knockout mice
- **Hale LP, et al. 2010** (PMID: 20848493): Fresh pineapple juice reduced colonic neoplasia in IL-10 KO mice with colitis
- **Onken JE, et al. 2008**: Bromelain reduced cytokine secretion from UC/CD colon biopsies ex vivo
- **Kane S, Goldberg MJ. 2000** (PMID: 10766699): Case report — 2 UC patients in remission on bromelain after failing conventional therapy (uncontrolled)
- **Marzoli F, et al. 2025** (PMC12663318): RCT in UC (triple-blind, placebo-controlled) — some evidence of benefit. **Need to verify PMID and primary results — this was a recent trial.**
- **No CD RCT.**

### Dose
- 200-400 mg 2-3x/day on empty stomach (for systemic anti-inflammatory effect); 500 mg is typical
- Strength measured in **GDU (gelatin dissolving units)** or **MCU (milk clotting units)** — 2,000-2,400 GDU/g is standard
- With meals for digestive enzyme effect (not anti-inflammatory)

### Safety
- Generally well tolerated
- GI upset, nausea, diarrhea
- **Rare allergic reactions** — especially in those with pineapple, latex, bee sting, or pollen allergies (cross-reactivity)
- Increased bleeding risk (antiplatelet) — caution with anticoagulants, preoperative

### Drug Interactions
- **Antibiotics** — may increase absorption of amoxicillin, tetracyclines
- **Anticoagulants/antiplatelets** — bleeding risk
- **Sedatives** — possible additive effect (minor)

### Practical Bottom Line
Mechanistically interesting, human IBD evidence scant. Low cost, generally safe. If tried, 500 mg TID on empty stomach. Don't expect miracles. **Stop before surgery.** May pair logically with curcumin for multi-target anti-inflammation (but stack bleeding risk).

## 11. Resveratrol

### Evidence Grade
**[Weak]** — one small UC RCT positive; no CD-specific clinical trials.

### Mechanism
Polyphenol (*Vitis vinifera* skin, Japanese knotweed root). Activates sirtuin-1 (SIRT1), inhibits NF-κB, COX-2, TNF-α; antioxidant; modulates gut microbiota. Extensive preclinical colitis model evidence.

### Evidence
**Samsami-Kor M, et al. 2015** — *Arch Med Res* 46(4):280-5. **PMID: 26002728**. Iranian RCT in active mild-moderate UC. n=50. **Resveratrol 500 mg/day × 6 weeks.** Significant reductions in TNF-α, hs-CRP, NF-κB activity. IBDQ-9 improved, SCCAI decreased.

**Samsami-Kor M, et al. 2016** — *Arch Med Res* 47(4):304-9. **PMID: 27664491**. Follow-up showing improved oxidative status (↑ SOD, ↑ TAC, ↓ MDA).

**Gianfredi V, et al. 2024** (Nutrients, multicenter RCT of MD + curcumin + resveratrol in mild-moderate UC): some benefit signals.

**No CD-specific RCT.**

### Dose
- 150-500 mg/day trans-resveratrol
- Bioavailability poor (<1%); micronized or liposomal forms better
- Take with fatty meal

### Safety
- Generally safe; GI upset at high dose
- Mild estrogenic activity (phytoestrogen) — theoretical caution in hormone-sensitive conditions
- Antiplatelet at high doses

### Drug Interactions
- CYP3A4 inhibition — various drugs (statins, CCBs, benzos, some chemo)
- CYP2C9 — warfarin
- Antiplatelet additive

### Practical Bottom Line
Weak but suggestive evidence in UC; extrapolation to CD uncertain. Modest doses (150-250 mg/day) probably safe and cheap. Don't expect major benefit. Dietary sources (berries, grapes, peanuts, red wine) provide trivial amounts pharmacologically.

## 12. Acupuncture / Moxibustion

### Evidence Grade
**[Weak-to-Moderate]** — one German RCT positive (CD), Chinese literature positive but methodologically limited.

### Mechanism (proposed)
- Vagal afferent stimulation → cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway
- HPA axis modulation
- Local microcirculation effects (moxibustion — burning of *Artemisia vulgaris*/mugwort on or near acupoints, adds thermal component)
- Endogenous opioid release (nociceptive pain)

### Key Trials

**Joos S, et al. 2004** — *Scand J Gastroenterol* 39(8):762-9. **PMID: 15114043**. German RCT in active mild-moderate CD. n=51. **10 sessions of traditional acupuncture + moxibustion over 4 weeks** vs sham (superficial non-acupoint needling).
- CDAI decrease: 87 points active vs 39 points sham (p=0.003)
- Both groups improved (large placebo component)
- Quality of life improved more in active group
- Modest additional benefit beyond placebo effect

**Bao CH, et al. 2022** — *EClinicalMedicine* 45:101300. **PMID: 35198926**. Chinese RCT in mild-moderate CD. n=66. Acupuncture + moxibustion 3x/week × 12 weeks vs sham. Significant CDAI reduction, improved quality of life, beneficial shifts in microbiota, reduced inflammatory markers. Higher quality than older Chinese literature.

**Joos S, et al. 2006** (UC): similar positive RCT for active UC.

### Dose/Regimen
- Typical: 10-20 sessions over 4-8 weeks, then maintenance
- Find licensed acupuncturist (NCCAOM certified in US); ideally with IBD experience
- Single sessions $60-120 typical

### Safety
- Very safe with trained practitioner
- Local bruising, minor bleeding, rare infection (single-use needles standard)
- **Moxibustion burn risk** — watch skin
- Avoid needles through active cellulitis, edema, vascular malformations

### Drug Interactions
- None

### Practical Bottom Line
Reasonable adjunct. Probable mechanism includes stress reduction + vagal stimulation. German and Chinese evidence moderate. **Don't expect remission induction on its own for moderate-severe disease.** Low risk, substantial cost/time commitment.

## 13. Yoga

### Evidence Grade
**[Moderate]** for quality of life, stress, disease activity in UC; less CD-specific data but benefit plausible and safe.

### Key Trials

**Cramer H, et al. 2017** — *Aliment Pharmacol Ther* 45(11):1379-89. Randomized trial: yoga (n=39) vs written self-care (n=38) in UC in clinical remission. 12 weekly 90-min **Hatha yoga** sessions. 
- Disease activity significantly lower in yoga group at 12 and 24 weeks
- Quality of life (IBDQ) improved more in yoga
- Perceived stress mediator confirmed in secondary analysis (Evers et al. 2019)
- Largest UC yoga RCT. (PMID for Cramer 2017: 28378342 — verify when possible.)

**Sharma P, et al. 2015** (PMID: 26667293): Yoga intervention in IBD — positive quality-of-life signals.

**Pediatric IBD yoga trial** (Arruda et al. 2024, PMID: 38327225): prospective controlled multisite — improvements in anxiety, stress, quality of life.

### Mechanism
- Parasympathetic activation (breath, posture)
- HPA axis modulation (cortisol reduction)
- Vagal tone improvement (HRV studies)
- Stress/anxiety reduction (documented mediator of IBD symptoms)
- Physical activity benefits (modest)

### Dose
- 60-90 minute sessions 2-3x/week, minimum 8-12 weeks
- **Gentle/restorative/Hatha styles preferred** — avoid hot yoga, intense vinyasa during flares (dehydration, physical stress)
- Avoid deep abdominal twists in active flares / post-op

### Safety
- Generally very safe with competent instructor
- Musculoskeletal strain (modify for joint issues, osteoporosis from steroid use)
- Avoid inversions if uncontrolled HTN, retinal issues

### Practical Bottom Line
**One of the highest-yield "complementary" interventions** — stress is a documented flare trigger, quality of life matters, side effects minimal. Recommend gentle/Hatha style, 2-3x/week.

## 14. Mindfulness / MBSR

### Evidence Grade
**[Moderate]** for psychological outcomes (stress, anxiety, depression, QoL). **[Weak]** for objective disease activity.

### Program
MBSR = **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction**, developed by **Jon Kabat-Zinn** at University of Massachusetts Medical Center (1979). 8-week group program: 2.5 hr weekly classes + 1 day silent retreat + daily 45-60 min home practice. Components: body scan, sitting meditation, mindful movement, informal mindfulness.

### Evidence in IBD
- **Berrill JW, et al. 2014** — pilot RCT of MBCT (Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy) in IBD — feasibility and QoL improvements
- **Jedel S, et al. 2014** (PMID: 24820332): MBSR prevented flares in UC patients in remission (trend)
- **Meta-analysis (Ewais T, et al. 2019)**: 8 RCTs — MBIs effective for stress, depression, QoL in IBD. Physical outcomes equivocal.
- **Vellanki S, et al. 2025**: live-video MBSR feasible for pediatric IBD

### Dose
- Full 8-week MBSR program ideal
- Apps (Headspace, Calm, Insight Timer) — evidence growing, 10-20 min/day
- Consistency matters more than duration

### Safety
- Very safe; rare emergence of anxiety or trauma during intense practice (especially with PTSD history)

### Practical Bottom Line
Strong for QoL, stress, anxiety, depression in IBD. Modest direct disease effects. Pair with yoga/breathing for comprehensive mind-body approach. Free apps and community classes readily accessible.

## 15. Gut-Directed Hypnotherapy (GDH)

### Evidence Grade
**[Strong for IBS]** — Peter Whorwell's Manchester model is well-validated. **[Moderate]** for UC (maintenance). **[Weak-Emerging]** for CD.

### Background
**Peter Whorwell** (South Manchester, UK) pioneered scripted hypnosis for IBS in the early 1980s. The "Manchester protocol" is the most validated GDH script. 7-12 sessions over 3 months with home recordings. Focus: gut relaxation imagery, reduced visceral hypersensitivity, restoring normal motility perception.

### Evidence
- **Whorwell PJ, et al. 1984**: original IBS trial — profound benefits, replicated many times. IBS remains the cleanest indication.
- **Mawdsley JE, et al. 2008** (UC, PMID: 18637160): reduced mucosal inflammation acutely.
- **Keefer L, et al. 2013** — Journal of Clinical Gastroenterology: randomized trial of GDH in UC in remission. **7 sessions over 3 months**. Reduced frequency of clinical flares vs control. **Laurie Keefer** at Mount Sinai is the US IBD-specific GDH pioneer.
- **CD trial — Beesley A, et al. 2023** (PMID: 37531719): virtual adjunctive GDH pilot/feasibility RCT in CD. Feasible, well-received (79% positive experience), quality-of-life signals. Not powered for efficacy.

### Dose
- 7-12 sessions over 3 months with licensed therapist trained in GDH
- OR apps: **Nerva** (IBS-validated), **Mahana IBS** (FDA-cleared) — adapted protocols, lower cost
- Home practice 15-20 min daily

### Safety
- Very safe; rare dissociative/emotional emergence — therapist handles
- Not effective for everyone (~30-40% non-responders in IBS trials)

### Practical Bottom Line
Underutilized. Excellent evidence for **coexisting IBS** (common in CD patients with healed mucosa but ongoing symptoms — ~40% of quiescent IBD has IBS overlap). **Sean's presentation** (post-resection, 20 years) may have functional overlay from altered anatomy/dysmotility — GDH could be particularly valuable for him. Find GDH-trained therapist via International Society of Hypnosis or the ACG/Rome Foundation directories. Apps are a reasonable starting point.

## 16. Fecal Microbiota Transplant (FMT)

### Evidence Grade
- **[Strong]** for recurrent *C. difficile* (>90% cure, standard of care)
- **[Moderate]** for UC (meta-analyses show ~30% remission vs ~10% placebo)
- **[Weak-to-Mixed]** for CD — small trials, mixed results

### CD-Specific Evidence
- **Sokol H, et al. 2020** — *Microbiome* 8:12. **PMID: 32014020**. French RCT. Steroid-induced remission CD n=21, randomized to single colonoscopic FMT vs sham after taper. Donor engraftment failed (none achieved primary endpoint). Steroid-free clinical remission: **87.5% FMT vs 44.4% placebo at week 10** (trend, NS due to small n); **50% vs 33% at week 24**. Underpowered but signal present.
- **Vaughn BP, et al. 2016** (open-label): modest signal
- **Systematic review Fehily SR, et al. 2021** (PMID: 34169565): clinical response in variable % of CD patients; heterogeneous methods.
- **Meta-analysis 2023** (IJCD PMID verify): pooled remission ~36% for CD FMT — unclear if superior to natural history.

### Mechanism
- Restores bacterial diversity, introduces missing taxa (*Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia* — both depleted in CD)
- Bile acid metabolism normalization
- Short-chain fatty acid (butyrate) production
- Regulatory T cell induction

### Route/Dose
- Nasoduodenal, colonoscopic, enema, capsules
- Single-dose vs repeat dosing (repeat appears more effective)
- Frozen standardized products (OpenBiome, FMT clinical trials) vs fresh
- In US: currently investigational for IBD; FDA-approved products (REBYOTA, VOWST) for C. diff only

### Safety
- Generally safe with screened donors
- FDA warnings (2019-2020): multi-drug resistant organism (ESBL E. coli) transmission caused deaths; SARS-CoV-2 concerns
- Rigorous donor screening now standard
- Occasional post-FMT IBD flare
- Theoretical long-term microbiome effects unknown

### Practical Bottom Line
**Not yet standard-of-care for CD.** Access via clinical trials only in most jurisdictions. Promising but unproven for CD-specific disease modification. **Do NOT self-administer via informal donor — documented serious infections from unscreened donors.** Watch space — next-generation "defined consortia" (e.g., VE202, SER-287) in development.

## 17. Exclusive Enteral Nutrition (EEN)

### Evidence Grade
**[Strong]** in **pediatric CD** induction (equivalent to steroids for clinical remission, superior for mucosal healing, no side effects). **[Moderate-Weak]** in adult CD.

### Protocol
- **EEN**: 100% of caloric needs from polymeric or elemental formula (no other food) × **6-8 weeks**
- Polymeric (whole protein, e.g., Modulen IBD, Ensure, Peptamen) as effective as elemental (amino acid) in most trials
- Oral or NG tube delivery
- Followed by gradual food reintroduction

### Pediatric Evidence
- **Borrelli O, et al. 2006**: EEN superior to corticosteroids for mucosal healing
- **Meta-analysis Zachos M, et al. 2007** (Cochrane): steroids slightly superior for clinical remission BUT EEN superior for growth and mucosal healing, no steroid side effects
- **Narula N, et al. 2018** (Cochrane, PMID: 28815649): pediatric meta-analysis confirming EEN efficacy
- **ESPGHAN/ECCO 2020 guidelines**: EEN is **first-line induction therapy for pediatric mild-moderate CD**
- Modified EEN (MEN) and CDED + PEN (partial EEN) — better tolerated, similar efficacy (Levine — below)

### Adult Evidence
- Generally inferior adherence due to palatability
- Meta-analyses (González-Huix, Zachos): steroids modestly superior in adults
- **Still considered reasonable** for patients wanting to avoid steroids, preoperative optimization, stricturing disease

### Mechanism
- Removes dietary antigens (gluten, emulsifiers, FODMAPs, food additives)
- Alters microbiome (reduces pro-inflammatory taxa)
- Restores mucosal barrier
- Nutritional repletion

### Dose/Calories
- Total caloric needs (e.g., 25-35 kcal/kg/day)
- Split into 4-6 portions daily
- Polymeric formulas typically 1-1.5 kcal/mL
- 6-8 week duration minimum

### Safety
- Very safe
- Short-term: refeeding syndrome (electrolyte monitoring in malnourished), mild nausea/diarrhea on initiation
- Social/psychological burden (eating restriction)
- Micronutrient considerations if prolonged

### Practical Bottom Line
**Gold standard for pediatric CD induction.** For adults: underused, worth considering for steroid-sparing induction, preoperative optimization, strictures with inflammatory component. Requires high motivation and dietitian support. **Preoperative EEN** for 4-6 weeks before surgery reduces complications (PMID: 31880668 and others).

## 18. Vagal Nerve Stimulation / Breathing

### Evidence Grade
**[Emerging / Weak-Moderate]** — mechanistically robust, small human trials positive.

### Mechanism
- **Cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway** (Tracey, Feinstein Institute) — vagal efferents release ACh at α7 nicotinic receptors on macrophages → reduces TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6
- HPA axis activation via afferents
- Enteric nervous system modulation
- Heart rate variability reflects vagal tone

### Evidence
- **Bonaz B, et al. 2016** (*Neurogastroenterol Motil*, PMID: 26920654): pilot study of implanted cervical VNS in 7 active CD patients × 6 months. **5 of 7 responded** with clinical, biological (CRP, calprotectin), and endoscopic improvement.
- **Sinniger V, et al. 2020** (*Neurogastroenterol Motil*): follow-up confirming efficacy and mucosal healing in CD.
- **Kibleur A, et al. 2018**: similar signals.
- **Sahn B, et al. 2023** (PMID: 37831302): transcutaneous auricular VNS (ta-VNS) in pediatric IBD — 64% responded with reduced inflammation. Non-invasive via ear.
- **D'Haens G, et al. 2023** (*J Crohns Colitis*, PMID: 37256822): prospective open-label VNS → reduced inflammatory activity in CD.

### Breathing Exercises (Slow Diaphragmatic Breathing)
- 4-7-8 breath, box breathing, coherent breathing (~5-6 breaths/minute)
- Increases HRV, parasympathetic tone — small studies show modest CRP reductions in chronic inflammatory conditions
- No IBD-specific RCTs but low-risk adjunct

### Dose
- **Implanted VNS**: investigational; not FDA-approved for IBD (approved for epilepsy, depression)
- **ta-VNS (cymba concha or tragus)**: devices like Nemos, Parasym, or DIY with TENS — 30 min/day, research protocols vary
- **Breathing**: 10-20 min/day, consistent practice

### Safety
- Implanted: surgical risks, voice alteration, cough, dyspnea during stimulation
- ta-VNS: very safe, mild local discomfort, skin irritation
- Breathing: risk-free

### Practical Bottom Line
**Emerging area, mechanistically compelling.** For Sean: daily slow breathing practice is free, safe, plausibly beneficial via vagal tone. ta-VNS devices are commercially available but evidence is early. Implanted VNS is investigational — consider trial enrollment if interested.

## 19. Elimination Diets

### Specific Carbohydrate Diet (SCD)
**Evidence Grade**: **[Weak-to-Moderate]** — uncontrolled case series positive; RCT shows comparable to Mediterranean (not superior); some pediatric RCT signal.

- Developed by Elaine Gottschall (*Breaking the Vicious Cycle*, 1994). Eliminates all grains, most dairy (except hard cheese, homemade yogurt fermented 24h), sugar, starchy vegetables, processed foods.
- **Rationale**: disaccharides/polysaccharides persist in inflamed intestine, feed dysbiotic flora
- **Suskind DL, et al. 2014/2018** (various PMIDs): pediatric case series showing remission
- **Suskind DL, et al. 2020** (PMID: 33291229): randomized pediatric trial, SCD vs modified SCD vs whole foods — all three induced remission
- **Lewis JD, et al. 2021 (DINE-CD)** — *Gastroenterology* 161(3):837-852. **PMID: 34052278**. Largest adult CD dietary RCT. n=194. SCD vs Mediterranean × 6 weeks.
  - Symptomatic remission: **46.5% SCD vs 43.5% MD** (NS; not superior)
  - Fecal calprotectin response: 34.8% SCD vs 30.8% MD (NS)
  - CRP response: 5.4% SCD vs 3.6% MD (NS)
  - **Conclusion: SCD NOT superior to Mediterranean diet. Mediterranean preferred due to ease and cardiovascular benefits.**

### Crohn's Disease Exclusion Diet (CDED) + Partial Enteral Nutrition (PEN)
**Evidence Grade**: **[Moderate-Strong]** — actual RCT evidence in children; emerging in adults.

- Developed by **Arie Levine** (Wolfson Medical Center, Tel Aviv)
- Eliminates emulsifiers, animal fat (except eggs/lean poultry/fish), gluten, dairy, food additives, sugar. Allows lean meats/fish, eggs, specified fruits/vegetables, rice, potatoes.
- 3-phase progression over 12+ weeks with PEN providing 25-50% of calories

- **Levine A, et al. 2019** — *Gastroenterology* 157(2):440-450. **PMID: 31170412**. Pediatric RCT. n=78 mild-moderate CD. CDED+PEN vs EEN × 12 weeks.
  - Week 6 clinical remission: **75% CDED+PEN vs 59% EEN** (NS, non-inferior)
  - Week 6 tolerability: **97.5% CDED+PEN vs 73.6% EEN** (p=0.002)
  - Week 12 sustained remission: **75.6% CDED+PEN vs 45.1% EEN** (p=0.01) — **sustained CDED superior**
  - Fecal calprotectin declined in both arms
- **Yanai H, et al. 2022** — *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 7(1):49-59. Adult CDED pilot RCT — promising induction signal
- **Sigall Boneh R, et al. 2017** (PMID: 28525622): CDED+PEN rescue for biologic-refractory — 70% response
- **Modified CDED for maintenance** — Scarallo L, et al. 2024 (PMID: 39732356): maintains pediatric CD remission

**This is the strongest-evidence dietary intervention for CD.**

### Autoimmune Protocol (AIP)
**Evidence Grade**: **[Anecdotal]** — no RCTs in CD.

- Paleo-based elimination of grains, legumes, dairy, nightshades, eggs, nuts/seeds, alcohol, coffee, processed foods, NSAIDs
- **Konijeti GG, et al. 2017** — Scripps: small IBD pilot (UC+CD), n=15, open-label. 73% achieved clinical remission by week 6. No control.
- Konijeti et al. 2020: 15/17 UC/CD patients improved on AIP
- Very restrictive; long-term adherence concerns; nutritional adequacy risk
- No RCT evidence

### Low FODMAP Diet
- Evidence primarily for IBS; IBD patients with IBS-overlap symptoms may benefit
- Not disease-modifying for IBD inflammation
- Short-term (2-6 week) elimination then structured reintroduction

### Mediterranean Diet
- **Evidence Grade**: **[Moderate]** — emerging as reasonable first-line dietary approach
- DINE-CD (Lewis 2021, above): 43.5% remission in CD, comparable to SCD
- Strazzabosco et al. 2025 (*Gastroenterology*): prospective cohort of newly-diagnosed CD — multiomic benefits on inflammation markers and microbiome diversity
- Easier to maintain, cardiometabolic benefits

### Practical Bottom Line
For Sean:
1. **Mediterranean diet** = best starting point (well-tolerated, heart-healthy, evidence-based)
2. **CDED + PEN** = highest CD-specific evidence; consider if flaring and want dietary-based induction
3. **SCD** = reasonable alternative, not superior to Mediterranean per DINE-CD, much harder to follow
4. **AIP** = experimental, very restrictive, not well-supported by RCT evidence
5. **FODMAP** = only if IBS-overlap symptoms

**Work with an IBD-trained dietitian** — restrictive diets carry nutritional risk (especially post-resection with potential malabsorption of B12, fat-soluble vitamins, calcium, iron, zinc).

## 20. L-Glutamine

### Evidence Grade
**[Weak]** — mechanistically appealing; RCTs inconsistent.

### Mechanism
- **Primary fuel source for enterocytes** and immune cells
- Substrate for glutathione synthesis (antioxidant)
- Supports tight junction integrity
- Stress-induced depletion

### Evidence
- **Akobeng AK, et al. 2000** (PMID: 10850950) / (PMID: 9888411 — Den Hond 1999): negative trials for glutamine + enteral nutrition in CD
- **Benjamin J, et al. 2012** (PMID: 22038507): Indian RCT, glutamine 0.5 g/kg/day × 2 months improved intestinal permeability and morphology in CD in remission (positive)
- **Akobeng AK, et al. 2016** (Cochrane, PMID: 26853855): insufficient evidence to support use
- **Meta-analysis 2024** (PMID reference to confirm): subgroup at doses >30 g/day showed permeability benefit

### Dose
- 5-15 g/day typical; higher (up to 30 g/day) in research protocols
- Powder form, mix in water (tasteless-ish)
- Empty stomach or between meals
- Dietary source: animal protein

### Safety
- Generally safe at typical doses
- Caution in severe hepatic/renal impairment (ammonia accumulation)
- Theoretical cancer growth concern (highly speculative, applies to select tumors using glutamine metabolism)
- Bipolar disorder — very rare reports of triggering mania

### Drug Interactions
- Minimal

### Practical Bottom Line
Cheap, generally safe. Modest evidence for intestinal permeability benefit in remission. 5-10 g/day reasonable trial. Don't expect dramatic effects. More coherent use = post-operative or during flare recovery.

## 21. Zinc Carnosine (Polaprezinc)

### Evidence Grade
**[Moderate]** — strong Japanese evidence for gastric/small bowel mucosal integrity; some IBD extrapolation.

### Mechanism
Chelate of zinc + L-carnosine (β-alanyl-L-histidine). Adheres to ulcerated/inflamed mucosa, releases zinc and carnosine locally. Effects:
- Scavenges reactive oxygen species
- Stabilizes cell membranes
- Upregulates HSP70 (heat shock protein — cytoprotection)
- Accelerates mucosal healing
- Preserves tight junctions

### Evidence
- Approved prescription drug in **Japan and South Korea** for gastric ulcers since 1994
- **Mahmood A, et al. 2007** (Gut 56:168-75) — British study: reduced NSAID-induced gut hyperpermeability
- Multiple Japanese trials in gastritis, H. pylori adjunct, oral mucositis post-chemotherapy/radiation
- Limited IBD-specific RCT data; one small study in radiation proctitis positive
- Mechanistic overlap with IBD barrier dysfunction compelling

### Dose
- **75 mg BID** (standard Japanese dosing — provides ~17 mg elemental zinc per 75 mg dose)
- Empty stomach or between meals
- 4-8 week courses typical

### Safety
- Very well tolerated
- Rare: metallic taste, GI upset
- Zinc excess: with chronic high dose (>40 mg elemental Zn/day) → copper deficiency, impaired iron absorption — check annual copper and CBC if chronic use
- Don't take concurrently with antibiotics, iron, calcium (chelation)

### Drug Interactions
- Tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones (reduced absorption — separate by 2 hours)
- Penicillamine (reduced absorption)

### Practical Bottom Line
**Underappreciated in Western IBD practice.** Reasonable adjunct for mucosal healing, especially during flare recovery or NSAID-associated injury. 75 mg BID × 8 weeks is standard, then reassess. Monitor copper/ceruloplasmin if used beyond 3 months at higher doses.

## 22. No-Evidence / Often-Hyped

### Colloidal Silver
- **No benefit in IBD.** Marketed for "immune support" and various infections.
- FDA banned OTC use in 1999 due to misbranding/lack of safety evidence
- **Argyria** — irreversible blue-gray skin/mucosa/organ discoloration from silver deposition
- Impairs absorption of antibiotics, thyroxine
- Can cause kidney, liver, nervous system damage
- **Avoid completely.** No legitimate oral use.

### Miracle Mineral Solution (MMS) / Chlorine Dioxide
- Sodium chlorite solution activated with acid → **chlorine dioxide, an industrial bleach**
- Promoted for autism, cancer, HIV, COVID-19, IBD — **all completely unfounded**
- FDA warnings since 2010; promoters (Genesis II Church) criminally prosecuted
- Causes severe vomiting, dehydration, life-threatening hypotension, methemoglobinemia, hemolysis (G6PD deficiency), bowel damage
- **Drinking this is drinking bleach. Extremely dangerous.**

### "Parasite Cleanses" / Detox Programs
- Most commercial "detox" products: no established mechanism, no quality trials, often contain laxatives/diuretics
- **Worsen** IBD through dehydration, electrolyte loss, bowel irritation
- "Parasites" implicated in IBD pathogenesis is fringe — **helminth therapy** (TSO, *Trichuris suis* ova) briefly studied and mostly abandoned after negative RCTs (Summers 2005 positive pilot not replicated)

### Homeopathy
- No evidence beyond placebo in IBD or anywhere else
- Mechanism implausible (dilution beyond Avogadro's number)
- Time/money sink; unlikely to cause harm itself but delays effective treatment

### Ozone Therapy / Hyperbaric Oxygen
- HBO has some signal in **fistulizing CD** (small uncontrolled series) — not ready for prime time
- Ozone insufflation: unregulated, risk of oxidative damage, no IBD evidence

### Coffee Enemas / Gerson Therapy
- No evidence
- Documented harm: colitis, perforation, electrolyte imbalance, death
- Avoid

### "Organic Germanium" / Ge-132
- Liver and kidney toxicity case reports
- No IBD evidence
- Avoid

### Black Seed Oil (Nigella sativa)
- Preclinical anti-inflammatory signal
- No IBD RCTs; promoted in health-food spaces without evidence
- Generally safe but don't expect benefit

### Kombucha
- No IBD-specific evidence
- Contains live microbes (variable); caution in immunosuppressed patients — rare infections reported
- Acidic — may aggravate reflux
- Low harm generally; not a treatment

### Apple Cider Vinegar
- No IBD evidence
- Very acidic — can aggravate reflux, erode tooth enamel
- Trendy, unsupported

### Activated Charcoal
- Adsorbs some toxins but also medications and nutrients
- **Will absorb oral IBD medications** — avoid concurrent use
- No IBD treatment evidence

## 23. Potentially Harmful

### Aloe Latex / Whole-Leaf Aloe / Anthraquinone Laxatives
- Contraindicated in CD and UC (per WHO, ESCOP)
- Worsens diarrhea, causes hypokalemia, dehydration
- **Only use "decolorized, aloin-free" formulations if any**

### High-Dose Iron (Wrong Form)
- Oral ferrous sulfate/fumarate/gluconate **can worsen IBD inflammation** — causes oxidative mucosal damage, microbiome dysbiosis, GI symptoms (nausea, constipation, abdominal pain)
- **Preferred forms in IBD**: IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose, iron sucrose, ferumoxytol) — recommended by ECCO guidelines for active IBD with iron deficiency
- Oral alternatives if mild IBD/stable: **ferric maltol (Feraccru/Accrufer)** — better tolerated, newer option
- Low-dose alternating-day dosing (e.g., 40-80 mg elemental every other day) may reduce side effects per hepcidin biology

### Turmeric/Curcumin in Anticoagulated Patients (unmonitored)
- See Section 1 — INR elevations, bleeding
- If on warfarin/DOAC: discuss with physician, check INR

### Turmeric in Those with HLA-B*35:01 (unknown status = caution)
- Hepatotoxicity risk — see LiverTox case series
- Routine genotyping not standard but consider if family/personal history of herb-related liver injury

### NSAIDs (including Willow Bark — Natural Aspirin Precursor)
- **NSAIDs trigger IBD flares** and impair mucosal healing
- Willow bark contains salicin → salicylic acid — same mechanism
- Avoid all forms. Use acetaminophen/paracetamol preferentially.

### Unregulated Supplements
- **Supplement industry is largely unregulated in US** — GMP issues, undeclared ingredients, incorrect doses, contamination with prescription drugs
- Independent testing: ConsumerLab.com, USP Verified, NSF Certified — look for these marks
- Prefer single-ingredient products from reputable manufacturers

### Unverified "Gut-Healing" Bone Broth Fasts
- Nutritional inadequacy if prolonged
- High sodium
- No evidence for IBD; potential harm in malnourished, post-resection patients

### High-Dose Kratom, Other "Natural" Opioid Substitutes
- Hepatotoxicity
- Constipation (worsening strictures)
- Addiction potential
- Avoid

### Pokeroot, Comfrey, Chaparral, Kava, Germander, Pennyroyal
- Hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic — avoid

### Grapefruit Juice (as "supplement")
- Inhibits CYP3A4 → **dramatically elevates levels of tacrolimus, cyclosporine, certain statins, some benzos, warfarin**
- Avoid with these medications

### Probiotics in Severe Immunosuppression / Central Lines / Active Severe Flare
- Rare but real: bacteremia, fungemia (S. boulardii)
- Discuss with GI before starting

### St. John's Wort
- Potent CYP3A4 inducer — decreases levels of **cyclosporine, tacrolimus, warfarin, oral contraceptives, certain HIV meds, some chemo**
- Serotonin syndrome risk with antidepressants
- **Avoid in transplant patients and on immunosuppression**

## 24. Interactions with Standard Crohn's Meds

### Azathioprine / 6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP)
- Metabolized via TPMT → 6-thioguanine nucleotides (active); xanthine oxidase → inactive metabolites
- **Allopurinol (or febuxostat)** — inhibits xanthine oxidase → massive increase in active metabolites → **life-threatening bone marrow suppression**. If combined (intentionally for "low-dose AZA + allopurinol" strategy to correct skewed metabolism), AZA reduced to 25-33% of dose, strict CBC monitoring.
- **Supplements that may theoretically interact**:
  - High-dose vitamin C (gram doses): uric acid effects theoretical — probably minor
  - Sulfasalazine/mesalamine: weak TPMT inhibition — standard in IBD combos, not a problem
  - Warfarin: AZA can affect warfarin levels
- **Natural health products**: generally no major interactions reported, but AZA has narrow therapeutic index — **avoid starting new supplements without discussing with GI**
- **Liver and marrow monitoring** essential (CBC, LFT every 3 months)

### Methotrexate (MTX)
- Folate antagonist (inhibits dihydrofolate reductase)
- **Folic acid 1 mg/day** or 5 mg/week (non-MTX days) — reduces side effects without substantially reducing efficacy; standard practice
- **Do NOT take folic acid same day as MTX** — theoretical efficacy reduction
- **NSAIDs** can increase MTX toxicity (reduce clearance) — avoid
- **Alcohol** increases hepatotoxicity — limit/avoid
- Penicillins, sulfonamides, high-dose aspirin — increase MTX toxicity
- **Supplements that interact**:
  - Nothing major and consistent
  - Liver-supporting herbs (milk thistle) — mostly safe, may theoretically protect LFTs but shouldn't enable higher MTX doses

### Biologics (anti-TNF: Infliximab, Adalimumab, Certolizumab, Golimumab; anti-integrin: Vedolizumab; anti-IL12/23: Ustekinumab; anti-IL23: Risankizumab, Mirikizumab; JAK inhibitors: Tofacitinib, Upadacitinib)
- **Biologic PK is through protein turnover, not CYP-based** — most supplement interactions are minimal
- **JAK inhibitors** ARE CYP3A4 substrates — CBD, grapefruit juice, St. John's wort, strong CYP inhibitors/inducers matter
- **Immunosuppression combined with natural product immunostimulants** (Echinacea, astragalus, ashwagandha) — theoretical reduction in efficacy; evidence weak
- **Live probiotics + immunosuppression** — small infection risk (fungemia for S. boulardii); discuss before starting
- **Vaccines + natural immune stimulants** — no strong data, likely minor

### Corticosteroids (Prednisone, Budesonide)
- Licorice (glycyrrhizin-containing) potentiates — hypokalemia, HTN, Cushingoid
- Vitamin D, calcium — essential supplementation (osteoporosis prevention)
- Melatonin — counteracts some cortisol effects; generally safe
- Avoid NSAIDs (additive GI/renal risk)

### Mesalamine / Sulfasalazine
- Well tolerated; minor interactions
- Can reduce folate absorption (sulfasalazine specifically) — supplement folate
- Monitor renal function (rare interstitial nephritis)
- Curcumin + mesalamine: studied combinations — positive synergy in trials

### General Principles for Supplement-Drug Interactions
1. **Report all supplements to GI, pharmacist, PCP** — the medication list on the chart must include them
2. **Check for CYP-mediated interactions** — especially CYP3A4, CYP2C9 (turmeric, CBD, St. John's wort, grapefruit, goldenseal)
3. **Stop supplements 1-2 weeks before surgery** — particularly those with bleeding potential (curcumin, fish oil, ginkgo, garlic, ginseng, vitamin E, ginger)
4. **Be cautious about layering immunomodulating interventions** — biologics + high-dose curcumin + CBD + etc. = unknown cumulative effects
5. **Monitor labs**: CBC, LFTs, creatinine, vitamin levels, fecal calprotectin — objective data is everything

## 25. Practical Integration Framework

### Tier 1 — "First-line low-risk high-evidence" (for any CD patient)
1. **Vitamin D** — target 50-60 ng/mL; 2,000-5,000 IU D3/day; K2 + Mg cofactors
2. **Mediterranean diet pattern** — actually better CD evidence than most supplements
3. **Yoga/mindfulness/meditation** — stress is a flare trigger; no downside
4. **Sleep hygiene, regular exercise, smoking cessation** — foundational (not "alternative" per se)
5. **Address iron, B12, folate deficiencies** — especially post-resection

### Tier 2 — "Reasonable adjuncts with moderate evidence"
1. **Curcumin** (Theracurmin 360 mg/day OR 2-3 g standard curcumin + piperine) — **with LFT monitoring every 3-6 months**; stop if liver symptoms
2. **Boswellia serrata** (standardized extract 350-400 mg TID) — good safety
3. **Omega-3 from diet** (fatty fish 2-3x/week) — cardiometabolic, not disease-modifying for CD
4. **Gut-directed hypnotherapy** — if IBS overlap symptoms persistent
5. **Acupuncture** — as time/budget allows

### Tier 3 — "Consider in specific contexts"
1. **EEN / CDED + PEN** — induction alternative to steroids, pre-op optimization, or rescue in flare
2. **Zinc carnosine** (75 mg BID × 8 weeks) — active flare, recent NSAID exposure, barrier rehabilitation
3. **Probiotics (VSL#3/Visbiome)** — if post-resection and concerned about dysbiosis; limited CD evidence
4. **Cannabis/CBD** — if symptom burden (pain, sleep, anxiety, appetite) inadequately controlled; not for disease modification

### Tier 4 — "Experimental/monitor developments"
1. **FMT** — clinical trials only
2. **VNS / ta-VNS** — mechanistically exciting; not ready for routine use
3. **Specific microbial consortia** (SER-287, VE202, etc.) — investigational
4. **Helminth/TSO therapy** — largely abandoned after negative trials

### Tier 5 — "Avoid"
1. Colloidal silver
2. MMS / chlorine dioxide
3. Aloe latex / whole-leaf aloe
4. High-dose oral ferrous salts in active inflammation
5. NSAIDs including willow bark
6. St. John's wort (if on immunosuppression)
7. Unregulated supplements without third-party verification

### For Sean Specifically (20-year post-resection CD, natural health interest)
**Highest priorities given your context:**
1. **Vitamin D optimization** — post-resection = higher malabsorption risk; target 50-60 ng/mL
2. **B12 monitoring and repletion** — ileal resection = lifelong B12 malabsorption risk; sublingual or injection
3. **Bile acid malabsorption screening** — common after resection; cholestyramine/colesevelam if present
4. **Bone density** — DEXA every 2-3 years; adequate calcium, D, K2, weight-bearing exercise
5. **Iron status** — if deficient, IV preferred in active inflammation; oral maltol if mild
6. **Mediterranean diet** — well-supported, sustainable
7. **Stress management stack** — yoga + breathing + GDH if IBS overlay
8. **Curcumin trial** with LFT monitoring if interested (Theracurmin 360 mg/day cleaner data)
9. **Honest assessment of current disease activity** — fecal calprotectin, CRP, periodic endoscopy — so you know if any intervention is actually working
