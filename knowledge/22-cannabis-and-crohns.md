# Cannabis and Cannabinoids in Crohn's Disease — Claude Reference

> Technical, greppable, citation-heavy reference. Not patient-facing prose. Claude reads this when Sean asks a cannabis-related question.
>
> **Central finding**: Cannabis/cannabinoids consistently improve **symptoms** (pain, sleep, appetite, abdominal cramping, quality of life, Crohn's Disease Activity Index score) in Crohn's disease. Cannabis/cannabinoids do **not consistently improve objective inflammation** (CRP, fecal calprotectin, endoscopic SES-CD score, mucosal histology). This symptom-vs-disease-activity gap is the defining feature of the evidence base (Naftali 2013, PMID:23648372; Naftali 2017, PMID:28349233; Naftali 2021, PMID:33858011; Kafil 2018 Cochrane, PMID:30407616). Cannabis is not a substitute for guideline-directed induction/maintenance therapy.
>
> **Safety non-negotiables**: (1) Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS) can mimic a CD flare and is a distinct, dangerous, and treatable entity. (2) CBD inhibits multiple CYPs — real interactions with tacrolimus, warfarin, clobazam, and theoretical with tofacitinib. (3) Cannabis use in CD patients has been associated in observational data with increased surgery risk (Storr 2014, PMID:24407485) — this remains unresolved.

## Overview (prose, brief)

The endocannabinoid system (ECS) is an endogenous lipid-signaling network present throughout the GI tract. In inflamed bowel, the ECS is upregulated — anandamide and 2-AG levels rise, CB2 receptor expression increases on immune cells, and this appears to be a protective/compensatory response. This provides a plausible mechanistic basis for exogenous cannabinoids modulating GI inflammation (D'Argenio 2006, PMID:16403786; Wright 2005, PMID:16083701; Di Marzo 2011, PMID:21481098).

Preclinical animal models of colitis (DSS, DNBS, TNBS) show consistent anti-inflammatory effects from CB1 agonism, CB2 agonism, FAAH inhibition (raising anandamide), CBD, and CBG (Borrelli 2013, PMID:23415610; Esposito 2013; D'Argenio 2006, PMID:16403786).

Human RCTs in Crohn's disease have been small (n=20–56), short (8 weeks), and conducted by essentially one Israeli group (Naftali and colleagues). The three formal CD RCTs diverge in a revealing way: inhaled THC-rich cannabis and oral CBD-rich cannabis oil produce clinical symptom benefit; low-dose pure CBD does nothing. Across all three, inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, calprotectin) and where measured, endoscopy (SES-CD) are unchanged. The Cochrane review concludes "effects are uncertain; no firm conclusions" (Kafil 2018, PMID:30407616).

Observational cohorts show that ~10–30% of IBD patients use cannabis and overwhelmingly report symptomatic benefit (Lal 2011, PMID:21795981; Ravikoff Allegretti 2013, PMID:24185313). A concerning observational signal from Storr 2014 (PMID:24407485) linked cannabis use >6 months with increased CD surgery risk (OR 5.03); subsequent larger cohorts show mixed results and the causal direction is uncertain (surgery-needing patients may seek more cannabis as much as vice versa).

Clinical bottom line for Sean: if using cannabis, use it **as adjunctive symptom management**, not as a substitute for maintenance therapy. Start low, go slow. Oral/sublingual over smoked. Watch for CHS if vomiting pattern shifts. Disclose CBD use to any prescriber because of CYP3A4/CYP2C9/CYP2C19 inhibition, especially if on JAK inhibitors or tacrolimus.

# SECTION 1 — The endocannabinoid system (ECS) and IBD

## ECS-COMPONENT: CB1-receptor
### full_name: cannabinoid receptor type 1
### class: Gi/o-coupled GPCR
### distribution_gut: myenteric plexus (neurons), submucosal plexus, enteric cholinergic motor neurons, epithelium (sparse), smooth muscle, plasma cells in lamina propria
### distribution_cns: high density in cortex, hippocampus, basal ganglia, cerebellum
### function_gut: reduces acetylcholine release from cholinergic motor neurons; slows gastric emptying and intestinal transit; reduces visceral nociception; epithelial wound closure (via heteromer with LPA1)
### function_other: psychoactive effects of THC mediated primarily here
### expression_IBD: upregulated in inflamed gut tissue
### evidence_grade: [Strong]
### key_sources: PMID:16083701 (Wright 2005), PMID:21471961 (Di Marzo/Izzo 2011 mucosal immunology review), PMID:21481098 (Di Marzo 2011 review)

## ECS-COMPONENT: CB2-receptor
### full_name: cannabinoid receptor type 2
### class: Gi/o-coupled GPCR
### distribution_gut: immune cells (macrophages, T cells, plasma cells), enteric neurons (induced in inflammation), colonic epithelium in IBD
### distribution_cns: low under baseline; induced in microglia during neuroinflammation
### function_gut: modulates cytokine production (reduces TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6); reduces LPS-induced hypermotility without affecting normal transit
### psychoactivity: no direct psychoactive effect
### expression_IBD: upregulated in inflamed mucosa
### evidence_grade: [Strong]
### key_sources: PMID:16083701 (Wright 2005), PMID:18483180 (Duncan 2008 CB2 motility), PMID:23415610 (Borrelli 2013 CBG)

## ECS-COMPONENT: anandamide-AEA
### full_name: N-arachidonoylethanolamine
### class: endogenous partial agonist at CB1 > CB2; also TRPV1, PPAR-alpha/gamma agonist
### synthesis: NAPE-PLD primarily
### degradation: FAAH (primary), also MAGL minor
### levels_IBD: elevated in inflamed colon of UC patients and DSS/DNBS mice — interpreted as protective compensatory response
### pharmacology_relevance: FAAH inhibitors raise AEA levels and reduce colitis in animal models
### evidence_grade: [Moderate]
### key_sources: PMID:16403786 (D'Argenio 2006), PMID:21471961 (mucosal immunology 2011)

## ECS-COMPONENT: 2-AG
### full_name: 2-arachidonoylglycerol
### class: endogenous full agonist at CB1 and CB2
### synthesis: diacylglycerol lipase (DAGL-alpha, DAGL-beta)
### degradation: MAGL (monoacylglycerol lipase) ~85%, ABHD6/ABHD12 minor
### levels_IBD: variable changes in inflamed gut; some studies show increase, others no change
### pharmacology_relevance: MAGL inhibitors raise 2-AG and reduce colitis in preclinical models
### evidence_grade: [Moderate]
### key_sources: PMID:16403786 (D'Argenio 2006), PMID:21481098 (Di Marzo review)

## ECS-COMPONENT: FAAH
### full_name: fatty acid amide hydrolase
### function: hydrolyzes anandamide, palmitoylethanolamide (PEA), oleoylethanolamide (OEA)
### therapeutic_target: FAAH inhibitors raise endogenous AEA/PEA/OEA, produce anti-inflammatory effect in colitis models without CB1-mediated psychoactivity
### clinical_status: no FAAH inhibitors FDA-approved for IBD; URB597 investigational tool compound; PF-04457845 advanced in other indications
### evidence_grade: [Emerging] for IBD
### key_sources: PMID:24641009 (Alhouayek 2014 review), PMID:21481098 (Di Marzo 2011)

## ECS-COMPONENT: MAGL
### full_name: monoacylglycerol lipase
### function: hydrolyzes 2-AG
### therapeutic_target: MAGL inhibition elevates 2-AG; preclinical anti-colitis effect
### clinical_status: investigational only
### evidence_grade: [Emerging] for IBD
### key_sources: PMID:21481098 (Di Marzo 2011)

## ECS-COMPONENT: TRPV1
### full_name: transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (capsaicin receptor)
### relevance: activated by anandamide at high concentrations; target of CBD; mediates visceral pain and thermosensation
### expression_IBD: upregulated in inflamed gut, contributes to visceral hypersensitivity
### evidence_grade: [Moderate]
### key_sources: multiple preclinical

## ECS-COMPONENT: GPR55
### full_name: G-protein-coupled receptor 55 ("orphan cannabinoid receptor")
### relevance: CBD acts as antagonist; THC is agonist; proposed CB3 role in some literature
### evidence_grade: [Emerging]

## ECS-COMPONENT: PPAR-gamma
### full_name: peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma
### relevance: nuclear receptor; activated by CBD and some endocannabinoids; mediates anti-inflammatory gene transcription
### evidence_grade: [Moderate]

### ECS summary — IBD-specific findings
- Anandamide is elevated in inflamed colon from UC patients and animal colitis models (D'Argenio 2006, PMID:16403786). [Strong]
- CB1 and CB2 immunoreactivity is increased in inflamed mucosa (Wright 2005, PMID:16083701). [Strong]
- CB2 induction on enteric neurons during inflammation modulates hypermotility (Duncan 2008, PMID:18483180). [Moderate]
- Pharmacologic raising of endogenous cannabinoids (FAAH/MAGL inhibition) reduces colitis in mouse models (D'Argenio 2006, PMID:16403786; Di Marzo 2011, PMID:21481098). [Moderate] — no human trials yet.
- ECS upregulation in IBD is interpreted as protective/compensatory response limiting inflammation. [Consensus-based]

# SECTION 2 — Phytocannabinoids (individual compounds)

## CANNABINOID: THC
### full_name: delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol
### aliases: dronabinol (when synthetic/isolated, USAN), Delta-9-THC, trans-THC
### class: phytocannabinoid, partial agonist at CB1 and CB2
### psychoactive: yes (primary psychoactive constituent of cannabis)
### mechanism_cd: CB2-mediated anti-inflammatory effect on immune cells; CB1-mediated reduction of visceral pain, appetite stimulation, antiemesis, slowed motility
### metabolism: primary CYP2C9 > CYP3A4; active metabolite 11-OH-THC (more psychoactive); secondary metabolite 11-COOH-THC (inactive, basis of drug screens); CYP2C9 poor metabolizers have ~3x higher THC AUC
### cyp_interactions: CYP2C9 substrate (major), CYP3A4 substrate, modest inhibitor of CYP1A1/CYP1A2 and CYP2B6 at high concentrations
### evidence_grade_cd: [Moderate] for symptomatic benefit in inhaled form (Naftali 2013); [Weak] for oral/disease modification
### key_cd_trials: PMID:23648372 (Naftali 2013, 115 mg THC BID), PMID:33858011 (Naftali 2021, oral 40 mg THC + 160 mg CBD/ml), PMID:21910367 (Naftali 2011 obs)
### dose_inhaled_studied: 115 mg THC BID via cigarettes
### dose_oral_studied: 40 mg/ml combined with 160 mg/ml CBD in oil, titrated
### response_clinical: 90% CDAI response in Naftali 2013 (>100-point reduction); 65% remission in Naftali 2021 (strict CDAI + HBI criterion)
### response_remission_formal_endpoint: Naftali 2013 missed formal CDAI <150 primary endpoint (45% vs 10%, p=0.43); Naftali 2021 met clinical endpoint but not endoscopic
### adverse_events: psychoactivity, dry mouth, tachycardia, dizziness, anxiety/dysphoria (especially at high doses), dependence risk, cognitive impairment, cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome at high chronic dose, rare psychosis in predisposed
### contraindications: pregnancy (Category X effectively), severe cardiovascular disease (unstable angina, recent MI), history of psychosis or strong family history schizophrenia, under age 25 except under strict medical supervision (brain development concerns)
### legal_status_us: federal Schedule I; state-by-state variable; FDA-approved synthetic THC (dronabinol) is Schedule II/III
### sources: PMID:23648372, PMID:33858011, PMID:21910367, FDA Marinol label (Schedule III), FDA Syndros label

## CANNABINOID: CBD
### full_name: cannabidiol
### class: phytocannabinoid, non-euphoric
### psychoactive: no direct euphoria (some anxiolytic/sedative effects noted)
### mechanism_cd: complex multi-target pharmacology — negative allosteric modulator at CB1; low-affinity CB2 partial agonist; TRPV1 agonist; 5-HT1A partial agonist; PPAR-gamma agonist; GPR55 antagonist; FAAH indirect inhibitor (via competitive binding to FABP); adenosine reuptake inhibition; anti-inflammatory via NF-kB suppression
### metabolism: CYP3A4 (major) and CYP2C19 (major); minor CYP1A1/CYP1A2/CYP2C9/CYP2D6; glucuronidation UGT1A7/1A9/2B7; active metabolite 7-OH-CBD
### cyp_inhibition: potent in vitro inhibitor of CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4; clinically relevant at doses used in Epidiolex (>10 mg/kg/day); lower doses (<300 mg/day) less likely clinically significant but remain relevant with narrow-TI drugs
### evidence_grade_cd: [Moderate] for CBD-rich/THC combination oil (Naftali 2021); [Weak/negative] for low-dose pure CBD (Naftali 2017); [Negative] for UC pure CBD extract (Irving 2018)
### key_cd_trials: PMID:28349233 (Naftali 2017, NEGATIVE, 10 mg BID); PMID:33858011 (Naftali 2021, 160 mg CBD + 40 mg THC per ml)
### dose_oral_studied_cd: 10 mg BID (insufficient); 160 mg/ml CBD + 40 mg/ml THC oil titrated in Naftali 2021
### dose_oral_epidiolex: 2.5 mg/kg BID titrated to 10 mg/kg BID (~700 mg/day in 70 kg adult) — for epilepsy
### adverse_events: somnolence, diarrhea, decreased appetite (paradoxically — not the GI-cramp mechanism), fatigue, hepatotoxicity (ALT elevation 12-17% at high doses), drug interactions
### contraindications: hypersensitivity, severe hepatic impairment; caution in moderate hepatic impairment
### hepatotoxicity_details: ALT >3x ULN in 12% at 20 mg/kg/day, 17% at 25 mg/kg/day; most resolve on discontinuation; risk higher with concomitant valproate, clobazam (Epidiolex label, FDA)
### sources: PMID:28349233, PMID:33858011, PMID:29538683 (Irving UC), FDA Epidiolex label 210365; PMC8246741 (CBD hepatotoxicity phase I)

## CANNABINOID: CBG
### full_name: cannabigerol
### class: non-psychoactive phytocannabinoid; precursor to THC and CBD biosynthetically
### mechanism_preclinical: CB1 partial agonist (low affinity), CB2 partial agonist, TRPA1/TRPV1 modulation, 5-HT1A antagonist, alpha-2 adrenergic agonist, PPAR-gamma agonist
### evidence_grade_cd: [Emerging] — preclinical only, no human IBD RCTs
### key_preclinical_data: Borrelli 2013 (PMID:23415610) — CBG reduced DNBS colitis in mice; decreased colon weight/length ratio, MPO activity, iNOS; effect partially CB2-mediated
### dose_preclinical: 1-30 mg/kg in mouse
### human_data: none in IBD; regulatory status unclear; hemp-derived CBG legal under 2018 Farm Bill if <0.3% THC
### sources: PMID:23415610

## CANNABINOID: CBN
### full_name: cannabinol
### class: oxidative degradation product of THC (forms in aged cannabis); minor phytocannabinoid
### mechanism: weak CB1 agonist (~10% potency of THC); CB2 agonist
### evidence_grade_cd: [UNVERIFIED] — no CD-specific trials; marketed for sleep despite weak evidence
### sources: consumer claims of sedation sedation largely unsupported by trials [UNVERIFIED]

## CANNABINOID: CBC
### full_name: cannabichromene
### class: non-psychoactive phytocannabinoid
### mechanism: TRPA1 agonist; minimal CB1/CB2 binding
### evidence_grade_cd: [UNVERIFIED] — no IBD clinical trials
### preclinical: limited anti-inflammatory data

## CANNABINOID: THCV
### full_name: tetrahydrocannabivarin
### mechanism: CB1 antagonist at low dose, agonist at high dose; CB2 partial agonist
### relevance: appetite suppression (opposite of THC); not studied in CD
### evidence_grade_cd: [UNVERIFIED]

## CANNABINOID: THCA
### full_name: tetrahydrocannabinolic acid (raw THC)
### class: non-psychoactive acid form in fresh/unheated cannabis; decarboxylates to THC with heat
### mechanism: low CB1 binding; anti-inflammatory data preclinical
### evidence_grade_cd: [Emerging]; no human IBD RCTs

## CANNABINOID: CBDA
### full_name: cannabidiolic acid (raw CBD)
### class: non-psychoactive acid form; decarboxylates to CBD
### mechanism: COX-2 inhibitor, 5-HT1A agonist; higher bioavailability than CBD in some models
### evidence_grade_cd: [Emerging]; no human IBD RCTs

# SECTION 3 — Key human clinical trials (Crohn's disease)

## TRIAL: Naftali-2011-observational
### design: retrospective observational, single center, Israel
### authors: Naftali T, Bar Lev L, Yablecovitch D, Half E, Konikoff FM
### journal_year: Isr Med Assoc J 2011;13(8):455-8
### pmid: 21910367
### flag_regulatory_context: Israeli medical cannabis program — government-sanctioned; this is not a regulatory-body source being cited as efficacy evidence, but patients sampled were enrolled in the IL program
### n: 30 (26 male) with CD
### intervention: cannabis use for any indication, inhaled primarily
### duration: median 3 years cannabis use post-initiation; compared to 9 years pre-initiation (within-patient)
### outcomes_reported: Harvey-Bradshaw Index (HBI), medication use, surgeries, hospitalizations
### results: HBI improved 14 ± 6.7 → 7 ± 4.7 (p<0.001); decreased steroid, immunomodulator, and biologic use reported; 19 surgeries over 9 years pre-cannabis vs 2 over 3 years post
### limitations: selection bias (patients who continued cannabis are those who felt it helped), no placebo, no blinding, within-patient comparison vulnerable to regression to mean and changing disease course, small n
### evidence_grade: [Weak] — hypothesis-generating only
### sources: PMID:21910367

## TRIAL: Naftali-2013-THC-RCT
### design: prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled, single-blind (placebo cigarettes were THC-extracted flowers, visually similar)
### authors: Naftali T, Bar-Lev Schleider L, Dotan I, Lansky EP, Sklerovsky Benjaminov F, Konikoff FM
### journal_year: Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol 2013;11(10):1276-1280.e1
### pmid: 23648372
### n: 21 (13 male, mean age 40 ± 14)
### population: active CD with CDAI >200, refractory to steroids, immunomodulators, and anti-TNFs
### intervention: cannabis cigarettes containing 115 mg Delta-9-THC twice daily vs THC-extracted flower placebo, 8 weeks
### primary_endpoint: complete remission CDAI <150
### results_primary: cannabis 5/11 (45%) vs placebo 1/10 (10%), p=0.43 (MISSED primary endpoint due to small n)
### results_secondary: clinical response (>100 point CDAI drop) 10/11 (91%) vs 4/10 (40%), p=0.028; improved appetite and sleep; 3 patients weaned off steroids; no change in CRP
### safety: no serious adverse events; subjective mild side effects expected with cannabis
### limitations: very small n, short duration, not double-blind, refractory-only population, no endoscopy, no calprotectin
### evidence_grade: [Moderate] for clinical response, [Weak] for formal remission
### interpretation: clinical symptom benefit without demonstrated inflammatory modification
### sources: PMID:23648372

## TRIAL: Naftali-2017-CBD-low-dose
### design: prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled
### authors: Naftali T, Mechulam R, Marii A, et al.
### journal_year: Dig Dis Sci 2017;62(6):1615-1620
### pmid: 28349233
### n: 20 (19 completed); mean age 39 ± 15; 11 male
### population: active CD
### intervention: oral CBD 10 mg twice daily (20 mg/day total) vs placebo, 8 weeks
### primary_endpoint: CDAI change
### results_primary: CBD 337 → 220; placebo 308 → 216; no significant difference between groups — NEGATIVE
### safety: no side effects; lab parameters (Hgb, albumin, liver/kidney function) unchanged
### limitations: DOSE TOO LOW (20 mg/day vs therapeutic CBD doses 300-700 mg/day in epilepsy); pure CBD without THC; small n
### evidence_grade: [Weak negative] — does not rule out higher-dose pure CBD; does rule out 20 mg/day
### interpretation: pure CBD at 20 mg/day is ineffective in CD; this is a dose-finding, not pharmacology-level, negative result
### sources: PMID:28349233

## TRIAL: Naftali-2021-oral-CBD-rich-oil
### design: prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single-center (Israel)
### authors: Naftali T, Bar-Lev Schleider L, Almog S, Meiri D, Konikoff FM
### journal_year: J Crohns Colitis 2021;15(11):1799-1806
### pmid: 33858011
### n: 56 (30 cannabis, 26 placebo); mean age 34.5 ± 11; 54% male
### population: active CD
### intervention: oral cannabis oil 160 mg CBD/ml + 40 mg THC/ml, titrated, 8 weeks
### primary_endpoint: CDAI and SES-CD (Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn's Disease)
### results_primary_cdai: significant decrease cannabis 282 → 166 vs placebo 264 → 237; clinical remission (CDAI <150) achieved in 65% cannabis vs 35% placebo (p=0.02)
### results_primary_sescd: NO SIGNIFICANT DIFFERENCE between cannabis and placebo in SES-CD endoscopic score
### results_secondary: QoL (SIBDQ) improved cannabis 91 vs placebo 75; CRP and calprotectin unchanged
### safety: well tolerated
### limitations: small n, short duration, endoscopy timing may have been too early to capture healing
### evidence_grade: [Moderate] for symptomatic benefit; [Negative] for endoscopic healing
### interpretation: LANDMARK demonstration of clinical/QoL benefit with cannabis-oil combination, BUT dissociation of symptom improvement from mucosal/endoscopic/biomarker healing — consistent with cannabis as symptomatic therapy, not disease-modifying
### sources: PMID:33858011

## TRIAL: Irving-2018-CBD-UC
### note: UC, not CD — provided for context only
### design: randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group pilot
### authors: Irving PM, Iqbal T, Nwokolo C, et al.
### journal_year: Inflamm Bowel Dis 2018;24(4):714-724
### pmid: 29538683
### n: 60 (planned), multicenter UK
### population: left-sided or extensive UC, Mayo 4-10, on stable 5-ASA
### intervention: CBD-rich botanical extract capsules (up to ~500 mg CBD + <2% THC) vs placebo, 10 weeks
### primary_endpoint: clinical remission
### results_primary: CBD 28% vs placebo 26% — NO DIFFERENCE (primary endpoint not met)
### results_secondary: PP analysis of total and partial Mayo score favored CBD; physician QoL assessments improved with CBD
### tolerability: poor — active-arm patients took on average 1/3 fewer capsules than placebo due to CNS side effects
### evidence_grade: [Weak/Negative]
### interpretation: suggests tolerability-limited dosing in UC; symptomatic signal but primary endpoint missed
### sources: PMID:29538683

# SECTION 5 — Symptoms cannabis MAY help (evidence by symptom)

## SYMPTOM: abdominal-pain
### cd_context: visceral pain, inflammatory pain, post-surgical adhesion pain, chronic functional component
### cannabis_evidence: [Moderate]
### key_data: 84% pain relief self-report in Storr 2014 (PMID:24407485); 91% clinical response in Naftali 2013 (PMID:23648372); Naftali 2021 oral oil reduced CDAI (which weights pain heavily, PMID:33858011)
### mechanism: CB1-mediated visceral afferent modulation; TRPV1 desensitization (CBD); descending inhibitory modulation
### caveats: chronic cannabis can paradoxically worsen pain (opioid-like hyperalgesia), especially high-THC daily use
### sources: PMID:23648372, PMID:33858011, PMID:24407485, PMID:21795981

## SYMPTOM: nausea-vomiting
### cd_context: flares, post-op adhesions, gastroparesis (rare in CD), chemotherapy (if coexisting malignancy)
### cannabis_evidence: [Strong] for chemotherapy-induced nausea; [Weak/Moderate] extrapolated to CD
### key_data: FDA approval of dronabinol and nabilone for chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting is based on multiple RCTs
### mechanism: CB1 in dorsal vagal complex; area postrema modulation
### caveat: chronic high-dose cannabis can CAUSE cyclic vomiting (CHS) — see ADVERSE section
### sources: FDA Marinol label, FDA Cesamet label; Rock 2016 (cannabinoids antiemesis review)

## SYMPTOM: appetite-weight-loss
### cd_context: common in active disease, post-resection malabsorption
### cannabis_evidence: [Strong] for short-term appetite stimulation
### key_data: dronabinol FDA-approved for AIDS wasting/anorexia; Lahat 2012 (PMID:22095142) showed weight gain; Naftali 2013 (PMID:23648372) reported improved appetite
### mechanism: CB1 in hypothalamic nuclei and mesolimbic reward pathway; increased ghrelin
### sources: PMID:22095142, PMID:23648372, FDA Marinol label

## SYMPTOM: sleep
### cd_context: sleep is commonly disrupted in active CD; sleep disruption drives flares (cortisol dysregulation)
### cannabis_evidence: [Moderate] short-term; [Weak/uncertain] long-term
### key_data: Naftali 2013 reported sleep improvement; UK registry showed SQS improvement
### mechanism: CB1 modulation of sleep architecture (reduces REM, increases slow-wave initially); receptor tolerance develops
### caveat: chronic cannabis use disrupts sleep architecture when ceased (withdrawal vivid dreams, insomnia)
### sources: PMID:23648372, PMID:39689344

## SYMPTOM: anxiety-depression
### cd_context: highly comorbid in IBD; affects disease course and treatment adherence
### cannabis_evidence: [Mixed/Contested]
### positive_evidence: CBD at higher doses anxiolytic; Naftali 2013 improved general well-being
### negative_evidence: Cannabis (THC-heavy) can acutely trigger anxiety/panic in susceptible individuals; chronic use associated with depression in some studies
### sources: Marconi 2016 (PMID:26884547) psychosis dose-response; Volkow 2014 NEJM review (DOI:10.1056/NEJMra1402309, PMID:24897085)

## SYMPTOM: diarrhea
### cd_context: hallmark symptom, often post-resection bile-acid diarrhea
### cannabis_evidence: [Weak]
### key_data: 29% reported diarrhea improvement in Storr 2014 (PMID:24407485)
### mechanism: CB1-mediated slowed transit; CB2 induced during inflammation modulates hypermotility (Duncan 2008, PMID:18483180)
### caveats: cannabis does not treat bile-acid diarrhea mechanism; colestyramine remains first-line for that
### sources: PMID:24407485, PMID:18483180

## SYMPTOM: arthralgia-EIM
### cd_context: inflammatory arthropathy is most common EIM; axial or peripheral
### cannabis_evidence: [Weak]
### key_data: 48% joint pain improvement self-report in Storr 2014 (PMID:24407485)
### mechanism: CB2 on synoviocytes; CBD/PEA anti-inflammatory peripheral action
### sources: PMID:24407485

# SECTION 6 — Symptoms cannabis does NOT reliably improve

### Key negative findings to emphasize

- **CRP**: unchanged in Naftali 2013 (PMID:23648372), Naftali 2017 (PMID:28349233), Naftali 2021 CD oral (PMID:33858011), Naftali UC 2021 (PMID:33571293). [Strong negative]
- **Fecal calprotectin**: unchanged in Naftali 2021 CD oral (PMID:33858011). [Moderate negative]
- **Endoscopic SES-CD score**: no significant improvement in Naftali 2021 (PMID:33858011). [Moderate negative]
- **Mucosal histology**: not reliably improved in any formal human trial to date
- **Clinical remission strictly defined CDAI<150 as primary**: missed in Naftali 2013 (p=0.43); met in Naftali 2021 as a composite CDAI+HBI criterion (65% vs 35%, p=0.02) but caveat remains regarding mucosal healing
- **Postoperative recurrence prevention**: not studied in CD
- **Sustained remission beyond 8 weeks**: not studied in CD (all trials 8-10 weeks)

### Implication
Cannabis at doses studied to date produces clinical symptom improvement and quality-of-life benefit, but does not reliably reduce the objective biology of intestinal inflammation. A patient could feel better on cannabis while the underlying ulceration progresses. This is the core reason cannabis is not a substitute for guideline-directed induction/maintenance.

# SECTION 7 — Routes of administration

## ROUTE: smoked
### onset: 5-10 min
### peak: 30 min
### duration: 2-4 hours
### bioavailability_thc: 10-35% (highly variable)
### advantages: fastest titration, useful breakthrough
### disadvantages: combustion tar and carcinogens (benzo[a]pyrene, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons); respiratory irritation; chronic bronchitis risk (Tashkin 2019, PMID:31298945); bystander exposure; stigma; variable dosing; inconsistent supply content
### ibd_specific_caveat: smoking in general is harmful to CD (smoking tobacco is a known CD risk factor and modifier); though cannabis smoke differs, combustion byproducts should be presumed harmful
### evidence_grade: [Consensus-based] to avoid if alternatives available
### sources: PMID:31298945 (Tashkin pulmonary effects); PMID:24897085 (Volkow NEJM)

## ROUTE: vaporized
### onset: 5-15 min
### peak: 30 min
### duration: 2-4 hours
### bioavailability_thc: 10-30%
### advantages: no combustion (temperature 180-220°C vaporizes cannabinoids without pyrolysis of plant material); fast titration; fewer combustion byproducts
### disadvantages: some respiratory irritation remains; vape-associated lung injury (EVALI 2019) was primarily from vitamin E acetate in illicit THC cartridges (CDC MMWR 2020) — regulated medical products safer; dependence on device quality
### evidence_grade: [Moderate] preferred over smoked if inhalation chosen
### sources: CDC EVALI reports

## ROUTE: oral-oil-capsule
### onset: 30-120 min
### peak: 2-4 hours
### duration: 6-10 hours
### bioavailability_thc: 4-20% (highly variable; food, bile, fat content affect)
### advantages: long duration; avoids lung; precise dosing possible
### disadvantages: slow onset leads to over-dosing ("I don't feel it yet, take more"); variable first-pass metabolism generates 11-OH-THC (more psychoactive than THC); food affects absorption
### ibd_specific_caveat: post-resection patients may have altered bile flow and intestinal transit affecting absorption; oil formulation helps; fat-malabsorption states reduce bioavailability
### evidence_grade: [Moderate] generally preferred for sustained symptom management
### sources: Naftali 2017 (PMID:28349233), Naftali 2021 (PMID:33858011) used oral route

## ROUTE: sublingual
### onset: 15-45 min
### peak: 1-2 hours
### duration: 4-8 hours
### bioavailability: intermediate (some buccal absorption bypasses first-pass)
### advantages: faster than oral, no combustion
### disadvantages: variable depending on formulation; swallowed portion undergoes first-pass
### evidence_grade: [Moderate]

## ROUTE: rectal-suppository
### onset: 15-60 min
### bioavailability: debated; limited CB1 CNS penetration has been claimed but PK data inconsistent
### advantages: hypothetically local delivery to rectum/sigmoid (relevant for distal inflammation); bypasses some first-pass
### disadvantages: compounded product variability; adherence; no RCT data in IBD
### evidence_grade: [Emerging/UNVERIFIED for IBD]
### sources: no Tier-1 IBD RCT data

## ROUTE: topical
### relevance_cd: minimal (systemic absorption low); could be used for perianal skin tags/fissures symptomatically
### evidence_grade: [UNVERIFIED] for CD

# SECTION 8 — THC:CBD ratios, product types, and FDA-approved cannabinoid medicines

### Hemp-derived vs cannabis-derived
- **Hemp-derived CBD** (<0.3% THC by dry weight, per 2018 Farm Bill): federally legal in US; available OTC; quality unregulated by FDA outside Epidiolex
- **Cannabis-derived CBD**: state medical/recreational program only; federally Schedule I
- CBD itself chemically identical from either source; plant matrix, contaminants (heavy metals, pesticides, mycotoxins), minor cannabinoids, terpenes differ

### Ratio products (empirical, not from IBD RCTs)
- **1:20 THC:CBD** (high-CBD): anxiolytic, minimal psychoactivity
- **1:10**: mild psychoactivity
- **1:4 to 1:1**: balanced; Naftali 2021 CD used approximately 1:4 (40 mg THC : 160 mg CBD per ml)
- **4:1 to 10:1 THC:CBD**: psychoactive-dominant; Naftali 2013 used THC-rich cigarettes (essentially THC-dominant)
- **20:1 THC:CBD**: sleep, pain; high psychoactivity

### Isolate vs full-spectrum
- **Isolate**: >99% pure compound (typically CBD); no terpenes, no minor cannabinoids
- **Full-spectrum**: whole plant extract including THC, CBN, CBG, terpenes
- **Broad-spectrum**: full-spectrum minus THC
- Entourage effect hypothesis claims full-spectrum > isolate — evidence is mixed and marketing-driven (see SECTION 17)

## DRUG: dronabinol
### aliases: Marinol (capsules, Schedule III), Syndros (oral solution, Schedule II)
### active_ingredient: synthetic delta-9-THC in sesame oil (Marinol) or ethanol/water (Syndros)
### fda_approved_indications: anorexia associated with AIDS weight loss; chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting refractory to conventional antiemetics
### not_fda_approved_for_cd: correct — no CD indication
### dose_aids: start 2.5 mg BID (lunch and dinner) — can increase to 20 mg/day max
### dose_cinv: 5 mg/m² 1-3 h before chemo, then q2-4h up to 4-6 doses/day; max 15 mg/m² per dose
### metabolism: CYP2C9 (major), CYP3A4 (minor)
### interactions: CYP2C9 inhibitors (fluconazole, amiodarone) and CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole, ritonavir) increase exposure; inducers (rifampin) decrease
### adverse_events: somnolence, dizziness, euphoria, confusion, anxiety, dysphoria; tachycardia; GI effects
### contraindications: hypersensitivity to dronabinol/sesame (Marinol) or alcohol-contraindicated (Syndros disulfiram interaction), history of psychosis
### schedule: Marinol Schedule III; Syndros Schedule II
### sources: FDA Marinol label, FDA Syndros label (accessdata.fda.gov)

## DRUG: nabilone
### aliases: Cesamet
### active_ingredient: synthetic THC analog (structurally similar but distinct)
### fda_approved_indications: chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting refractory to conventional antiemetics
### not_fda_approved_for_cd: correct
### dose: 1-2 mg BID; max 6 mg/day divided
### schedule: Schedule II
### adverse_events: similar to THC — dizziness, drowsiness, dry mouth, euphoria/dysphoria
### sources: FDA Cesamet label (accessdata.fda.gov)

## DRUG: nabiximols
### aliases: Sativex
### active_ingredient: standardized cannabis plant extract 2.7 mg THC + 2.5 mg CBD per 100 microliter oromucosal spray
### fda_approved_indications: NONE (not FDA-approved in US — phase 3 failed to meet MS spasticity primary endpoint in US trials)
### approved_indications_elsewhere: MS spasticity (Canada, UK, EU 29 countries); neuropathic pain in MS (Canada, conditional); cancer pain (Canada, conditional)
### not_approved_for_cd: correct, anywhere
### dose: titrated up to 12 sprays/day (max 32.4 mg THC + 30 mg CBD)
### evidence_grade_cd: no CD data; a pilot CD study (GWP42003 extract, not identical to nabiximols) reported in IACM bulletin but not peer-reviewed as full paper [UNVERIFIED as published Tier-1 IBD evidence]

## DRUG: cannabidiol-Epidiolex
### aliases: Epidiolex (US), Epidyolex (EU)
### active_ingredient: purified plant-derived CBD 100 mg/ml oral solution
### fda_approved_indications: seizures associated with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome, tuberous sclerosis complex (ages 1+)

# SECTION 9 — Drug interactions with Crohn's medications (CRITICAL)

### General principle
CBD is a clinically relevant inhibitor of CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4 at doses ≥300 mg/day; weaker but potentially relevant at 100-300 mg/day; minor below 100 mg/day. THC is primarily a CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 SUBSTRATE, with minor inhibitory effects on CYP1A2/CYP2B6. The interactions relevant to CD drugs are almost entirely CBD-mediated for inhibition, and bidirectional metabolism for THC/CBD themselves.

### Threshold for clinical concern
- **CBD <50 mg/day**: minimal interaction risk; likely safe with most CD drugs but monitor narrow-TI drugs (warfarin, tacrolimus)
- **CBD 50-300 mg/day**: moderate risk; real-world interactions reported
- **CBD >300 mg/day** (Epidiolex-range): substantial risk; require dose adjustments
- **THC any dose**: interactions mostly from concurrent drugs inhibiting/inducing THC metabolism

## INTERACTION: cannabis-tacrolimus
### direction: CBD raises tacrolimus levels substantially
### mechanism: CBD and 7-OH-CBD inhibit CYP3A4-mediated tacrolimus metabolism
### magnitude: Cmax 4.2-fold, AUC 3.1-fold increase in Phase I study (So et al. 2025); case reports of 3-fold tacrolimus increase with 2000-2900 mg/day CBD
### clinical_relevance_cd: tacrolimus occasionally used for refractory fistulizing CD or as steroid-sparing agent; narrow therapeutic index
### recommendation: dose reduction of tacrolimus and frequent trough monitoring if co-prescribed; caution with CBD-rich products
### evidence_grade: [Strong]
### sources: PMID:31012522 (Leino 2019 case report AJT), PMID:39921332 (2025 in vitro), PMID:27595035 (Hauser 2016 cannabis + calcineurin warning)

## INTERACTION: cannabis-warfarin
### direction: CBD (and possibly THC) raise warfarin levels via CYP2C9 inhibition
### mechanism: CYP2C9 metabolizes S-warfarin (more potent enantiomer); CBD inhibits
### magnitude: case report of INR >10 with warfarin + CBD co-administration; multiple INR elevation reports
### clinical_relevance_cd: warfarin is less commonly used in CD patients but may be needed for VTE or atrial fibrillation
### recommendation: close INR monitoring on initiation/change of cannabis; consider DOAC alternative if CBD anticipated long-term (but DOACs also CYP-dependent)
### evidence_grade: [Strong]
### sources: FDA Epidiolex label; case reports

## INTERACTION: cannabis-clobazam
### direction: bidirectional — CBD raises N-desmethylclobazam; clobazam raises 7-OH-CBD
### mechanism: CBD inhibits CYP3A4 metabolism of clobazam; clobazam inhibits metabolism of 7-OH-CBD
### magnitude: N-desmethylclobazam AUC ~3-fold increase; 7-OH-CBD Cmax 73%, AUC 47% increase
### clinical_relevance_cd: clobazam rarely used in CD, but relevant if concurrent seizure disorder
### sources: FDA Epidiolex label

## INTERACTION: cannabis-tofacitinib
### direction: CBD may raise tofacitinib levels
### mechanism: CBD inhibits CYP3A4 (major tofacitinib metabolism) and CYP2C19 (minor tofacitinib metabolism)
### magnitude: [UNVERIFIED in specific PK study] — predicted from mechanism; no dedicated human PK study in patients
### clinical_relevance_cd: tofacitinib FDA-approved for UC; off-label for refractory CD; interacts with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors requiring dose reduction
### recommendation: caution with high-dose CBD (>300 mg/day); monitor for tofacitinib toxicity (cytopenias, infection, VTE); consider dose reduction per label for moderate/strong CYP3A4 inhibitor co-administration
### evidence_grade: [Emerging/theoretical]
### sources: Xeljanz FDA label; secondary reports [no dedicated PMID for the specific interaction study]

## INTERACTION: cannabis-upadacitinib
### direction: CBD may raise upadacitinib levels
### mechanism: CYP3A4 substrate upadacitinib; CBD is CYP3A4 inhibitor
### clinical_relevance_cd: upadacitinib FDA-approved for CD and UC; strong CYP3A4 inhibitors require dose attention per label (though modest effect expected)
### recommendation: monitor with high-dose CBD co-administration
### evidence_grade: [Emerging/theoretical]
### sources: Rinvoq FDA label

## INTERACTION: cannabis-methotrexate
### direction: minimal direct metabolic interaction
### mechanism: methotrexate cleared primarily renally and by hepatic AOX; little CYP involvement; CBD not known to affect renal clearance of MTX substantially
### clinical_relevance_cd: cannabis and methotrexate both potentially hepatotoxic — additive hepatic risk

## INTERACTION: cannabis-biologics-antiTNF

### recommendation: no routine concern
### evidence_grade: [Weak]

### clinical_relevance: theoretically raises PPI levels; probably clinically insignificant
### sources: mechanism

# SECTION 10 — Safety and adverse effects

## ADVERSE: cannabinoid-hyperemesis-syndrome-CHS
### description: paradoxical cyclic vomiting syndrome caused by chronic cannabis use (typically >1 year daily use); characterized by hyperemesis, abdominal pain, compulsive hot bathing, resolution with cannabis cessation
### diagnostic_criteria_simonetto_2012: essential — chronic cannabis use (>weekly); major features — severe cyclic nausea/vomiting, resolution with cannabis cessation, relief with hot showers/baths; supportive — male predominance (~73%), abdominal pain (~85%), at least weekly cannabis use
### cd_specific_concern: CHS can mimic a CD flare — cyclic vomiting, abdominal pain, weight loss. Sean (post-resection CD) could be particularly confused by this overlap. If a daily cannabis user presents with new or worsening vomiting, CHS must be considered alongside CD flare, obstruction, adhesion, SIBO.
### differentiation: CHS has characteristic hot-bathing behavior (relieves symptoms), absence of inflammation markers elevation expected in CD flare, absence of anatomic finding, rapid resolution on cannabis cessation (days to weeks)
### treatment_acute: IV fluids; topical capsaicin (0.075-0.1%) on abdomen (TRPV1 mechanism); haloperidol (0.05 mg/kg IV) or droperidol; benzodiazepines adjunct; traditional antiemetics often ineffective
### treatment_definitive: stop cannabis
### prevalence: 2.75-6% of ED presentations with cyclic vomiting in legalized-cannabis jurisdictions
### evidence_grade: [Strong] — well-characterized entity; AGA 2024 Clinical Practice Update published
### sources: Simonetto 2012 (Mayo Clin Proc 87:114-119); AGA Clinical Practice Update 2024 (Gastroenterology); PMC3847982; PMID:32564375

## ADVERSE: cannabis-use-disorder-CUD
### dsm5_criteria: 2+ of 11 criteria over 12 months (tolerance, withdrawal, use more than intended, unsuccessful control attempts, time spent, craving, failure in role obligations, social problems, activities given up, physical/psychological problems, hazardous use)
### severity: mild (2-3 criteria), moderate (4-5), severe (6+)
### lifetime_prevalence_us: 6.3% (Hasin 2016, PMID:26940807)
### past_year_prevalence_us: 2.5% (Hasin 2016)
### risk_in_daily_users: 25-50% develop CUD
### risk_of_dependency_lifetime: ~9% of any-use initiators
### risk_of_dependency_daily_users: ~25-50%
### risk_factors: male sex, Native American ethnicity, unmarried, younger, low income, psychiatric comorbidity, early initiation (<18)
### evidence_grade: [Strong]
### sources: PMID:26940807 (Hasin 2016 AJP), PMID:28445557 (Hasin 2017 JAMA Psychiatry)

## ADVERSE: withdrawal-syndrome
### dsm5_cannabis_withdrawal_criteria: >3 symptoms within 1 week of cessation after heavy prolonged use — irritability, anxiety, sleep disturbance, decreased appetite, restlessness, depressed mood, physical symptoms (abdominal pain, sweating, chills, headache)
### onset: 24-72 hours
### peak: days 2-6
### duration: 1-3 weeks (sleep disturbance can persist months)
### clinical_relevance_cd: withdrawal abdominal pain may overlap with CD flare symptoms; irritability and sleep disturbance can worsen CD quality of life transiently
### evidence_grade: [Strong]
### sources: DSM-5; multiple epidemiologic studies

## ADVERSE: psychosis-risk
### relationship: dose-response association between cannabis use frequency/potency and psychosis incidence
### magnitude: heaviest users OR ~3.9 for schizophrenia/psychotic disorders (Marconi 2016, PMID:26884547)
### causality: cannabis is a component cause in genetically predisposed individuals; not a sole cause
### risk_factors: personal/family history of schizophrenia, early initiation (<18), high-potency cannabis (>10% THC), frequent use
### evidence_grade: [Strong] for association; [Moderate] for causation
### ibd_relevance: psychosis history is a relative contraindication to THC-containing cannabis
### sources: PMID:26884547 (Marconi 2016 Schizophr Bull)

## ADVERSE: cognitive-effects
### acute: attention, short-term memory, working memory, executive function, psychomotor speed impaired at peak THC effect; lasts hours
### chronic_heavy_users: persistent impairments in learning and memory; variable reversibility; adolescent onset users show greater persistent deficits
### cd_relevance: post-resection patients (Sean's context) may have subtle cognitive effects from B12/thiamine/folate deficiencies; added cannabis cognitive burden may compound
### evidence_grade: [Strong] acute; [Moderate] chronic
### sources: Volkow 2014 (PMID:24897085)

## ADVERSE: cardiovascular-risk
### acute: tachycardia (20-100% heart rate increase), BP effects (biphasic — rise then drop), increased myocardial oxygen demand
### myocardial_infarction: 4.8-fold increased risk in hour after cannabis use (Mittleman 2001); observational data showed higher MI in recent users
### stroke: observational association, causality uncertain
### arrhythmia: case reports of AF, SVT, VT
### cd_relevance: relatively low concern absent CV disease; concerning for older patients with CAD risk factors
### evidence_grade: [Moderate]
### contraindications: unstable angina, recent MI (<6 weeks), severe arrhythmia, severe HF

## ADVERSE: pulmonary-risk
### smoked_cannabis: chronic bronchitis (cough, sputum, wheeze); alveolar macrophage dysfunction; goblet cell hyperplasia
### vs_tobacco: less clear link to COPD and emphysema than tobacco; less clear lung cancer link
### cd_relevance: smoking tobacco worsens CD; cannabis smoke byproducts presumed similar regarding combustion toxins; vaporized/oral forms preferable
### evidence_grade: [Moderate]
### sources: PMID:31298945 (Tashkin 2019)

## ADVERSE: CBD-hepatotoxicity
### magnitude: ALT >3x ULN in 12% at 20 mg/kg/day Epidiolex, 17% at 25 mg/kg/day
### onset: typically within first 2 months
### reversibility: most resolve on discontinuation within 1-2 weeks
### risk_factors: concomitant valproate (strongest), clobazam, underlying liver disease
### low_dose_relevance: CBD <50 mg/day appears very low risk; typical consumer CBD doses in 10-50 mg range have rare reports
### clinical_relevance_cd: consider LFTs if taking >100 mg/day CBD chronically, especially with methotrexate or azathioprine co-administration
### evidence_grade: [Strong]
### sources: FDA Epidiolex label; PMC8246741 (CBD Phase I hepatotoxicity trial in healthy adults)

## ADVERSE: pregnancy-lactation
### pregnancy: CONTRAINDICATED
### effects_on_fetus: lower birth weight (MD -177g); small for gestational age (RR ~1.79); decreased head circumference; increased NICU admission; possible association with childhood ADHD, ASD (hazard ratios ~1.5); neurodevelopmental concerns
### lactation: CONTRAINDICATED — THC and CBD excreted in breast milk; persist for days; fetal/infant brain has high CB1 expression during development
### evidence_grade: [Strong] for avoidance
### sources: PMID:37547668 (systematic review 2023); PMID:37968824 (meta-analysis 2023); ACOG committee opinion

# SECTION 11 — Special populations

### Post-resection CD (Sean's context) — key considerations
- **Absorption**: ileal resection reduces bile acid circulation and may affect fat-soluble cannabinoid (THC/CBD) absorption from oral formulations. Take with fatty meal to boost absorption, or consider sublingual/inhaled to bypass this variable.
- **Transit time**: shorter transit post-resection may reduce oral bioavailability; inhaled route not affected
- **B12 and other deficiencies**: cognitive effects of cannabis may compound any cognitive effects of B12 deficiency — ensure B12 repleted
- **Bile acid diarrhea**: cannabis does not treat this mechanism; colestyramine/colesevelam is first-line
- **Medication load**: high-resection patients often on loperamide, PPIs, antibiotics cyclically — low PK interaction concern but polypharmacy increases monitoring needs
- **Kidney stone risk** (oxalate post-ileal-resection): cannabis does not affect oxalate; unrelated
- **CHS particular concern**: vomiting pattern should be evaluated carefully — differential includes CD flare, obstruction, adhesion, SIBO, gallstone, cholangiopathy, CHS

### Older adults (>65)
- Increased falls risk (cannabis orthostasis + cognitive effects)
- Slower metabolism means prolonged effects
- Polypharmacy increases interaction risk
- Cardiovascular risk higher

### Adolescents (<18) and young adults (<25)
- Brain development ongoing to age 25
- Psychosis risk higher with early initiation
- Persistent cognitive deficits with adolescent-onset heavy use
- CONTRAINDICATED except under strict pediatric GI + psychiatry supervision

### Pregnant/lactating
- CONTRAINDICATED — see ADVERSE block above

# SECTION 12 — Practical dosing protocols (evidence-graded)

### "Start low, go slow" — canonical titration approach

### CBD (oral, hemp-derived OTC or medical)
- **Week 1**: 5-10 mg once daily, evening
- **Week 2**: 5-10 mg BID if tolerated
- **Week 3-4**: 10-25 mg BID
- **Target for symptomatic benefit**: 25-100 mg/day (though Naftali 2021 used much higher doses in cannabis-oil format)
- **Ceiling for OTC use without monitoring**: ~100 mg/day; above this, consider LFTs
- **Not equivalent to Epidiolex doses**: Epidiolex indication doses (700-1750 mg/day) require FDA supervision and are for epilepsy, not IBD

### THC (if cannabis-naive)
- **First dose**: 2.5 mg oral, evening, with a trusted person present
- **Titrate**: every 3-5 days by 2.5 mg
- **Typical therapeutic range**: 5-10 mg oral per dose, 2-3 times daily
- **Inhaled**: start with one small inhalation; wait 15 minutes; repeat only if needed
- **Ceiling in CD trials**: 115 mg/day THC inhaled (Naftali 2013) or 80-120 mg/day oral (Naftali 2021) — these are high, study-controlled doses

### Combination (CBD-rich with THC)
- Ratio 1:4 to 1:1 (THC:CBD) for CD-like applications
- Start with the CBD component dominant (higher CBD), titrate THC up as needed
- Naftali 2021 used 160 CBD + 40 THC per ml — effectively 4:1 CBD:THC (inverse of how typically expressed)

### Monitoring
- **Baseline**: LFTs (ALT, AST, bilirubin), CBC, CRP, fecal calprotectin (if used to assess IBD activity)
- **Month 1-2 on moderate-dose CBD**: repeat LFTs
- **Ongoing**: track symptoms in a diary; reassess every 3 months
- **If on warfarin/tacrolimus/clobazam**: close drug-specific monitoring per specialist

### Expected time to effect
- **Symptoms** (pain, sleep, appetite, nausea): hours (inhaled), days (oral)
- **Persistent quality of life**: 2-4 weeks
- **Inflammatory markers**: NOT EXPECTED to change (consistent with evidence)
- **Endoscopic change**: NOT EXPECTED at 8 weeks (Naftali 2021)

# SECTION 15 — Patient-reported outcomes and surveys

### Prevalence of cannabis use in IBD cohorts
- Current/active use: 10-30% across surveys
- Lifetime use: ~50%
- Representative surveys:
  - Lal 2011 Toronto (PMID:21795981): lifetime 50%, active for symptoms 33% UC, 50% CD
  - Ravikoff Allegretti 2013 MGH (PMID:24185313): 12.3% active, 39% past
  - Storr 2014 Calgary (PMID:24407485): 17.6% active for IBD symptoms
  - Coates 2022 (PMID:33998892): symptoms and EIMs in active users

### Reasons reported
In rough order of frequency across surveys:
1. Abdominal pain
2. Poor appetite / weight loss
3. Nausea
4. Sleep difficulty
5. Anxiety/mood
6. Diarrhea
7. "Just helps overall"
8. Joint pain (EIM)

### Subjective benefit vs objective disease activity — the central paradox
- Subjective: 75-90% of users in symptom surveys report improvement
- Objective: trials show no change in CRP, calprotectin, endoscopy, or histology
- **Why this matters**: cannabis is genuine symptomatic therapy, but it can "mask" ongoing inflammation. A patient may feel fine while mucosal healing fails or disease progresses, leading to delayed recognition of flare and potentially surgery.
- Storr 2014 (PMID:24407485) surgery signal may reflect this — patients self-treating symptoms rather than escalating anti-inflammatory therapy, presenting later.
- Counterargument: sicker patients may seek cannabis more, biasing surgery association. Causality unresolved.

# SECTION 16 — CDED and cannabis — nutrition interactions

### CDED (Crohn's Disease Exclusion Diet) — brief context
CDED is a whole-food elimination diet used alone or with partial enteral nutrition (PEN); validated in pediatric and adult CD for induction of remission (Levine 2019 PMID:31030707, Yanai 2022 PMID:35643092).

### Cannabis interactions with CDED
- **Appetite stimulation**: if a patient struggles to consume the required caloric load of CDED (especially with PEN supplementation), THC-stimulated appetite could support adherence. Benefit unverified in trials but mechanistically plausible.
- **Edibles carry sugar and fat**: commercial cannabis edibles often have ingredients excluded in CDED (high sugar, processed emulsifiers, certain oils). If using edibles, prefer medical oil/tincture or capsule without excipients incompatible with CDED.
- **Smoking/vaping do not carry nutrition content concern**: appropriate for CDED adherence if patient chooses that route
- **Munchies and phase 1 rigidity**: THC-stimulated cravings may be for excluded foods (ice cream, chips) — important practical concern
### evidence_grade: [Emerging/UNVERIFIED in trials]

# SECTION 17 — Cannabis vs CBD isolate — the "entourage effect"

### The hypothesis
"Full-spectrum" cannabis (with all cannabinoids and terpenes) is more therapeutic than isolated compounds due to synergistic interactions among cannabinoids, terpenes, and flavonoids — the "entourage effect" (Russo 2011 and subsequent writings).

### What the evidence shows
- **Preclinical (in vitro/animal)**: some synergistic effects documented; for example, CBD can blunt some THC adverse effects; terpenes may modulate receptor activity at high concentrations
- **Human**: limited controlled comparisons of full-spectrum vs isolate; Naftali 2017 pure CBD negative trial (PMID:28349233) vs Naftali 2021 cannabis oil positive trial (PMID:33858011) is the most direct test in CD, and supports full-spectrum benefit over low-dose pure CBD, but the dose difference confounds
- **Industry framing**: "entourage effect" is heavily promoted by cannabis industry to justify premium full-spectrum products; marketing has outpaced evidence
- **Practical**: full-spectrum products cannot be produced without at least some THC content, which carries legal and cognitive implications

### Terpenes (entity blocks)

## TERPENE: beta-caryophyllene
### class: sesquiterpene; uniquely among common plant terpenes, it is a full selective CB2 agonist (Ki ~155 nM)
### mechanism_cd: CB2-mediated anti-inflammatory; preclinical anti-colitis
### dietary_sources: black pepper, cloves, hops, oregano, rosemary, cannabis
### safety: generally regarded as safe (GRAS) as food additive
### evidence_grade: [Emerging] for IBD-specific; [Moderate] for general anti-inflammatory preclinical
### sources: Gertsch 2008 (PMID: 18574142, Proc Natl Acad Sci), secondary reviews

## TERPENE: myrcene
### class: monoterpene; sedative claims ("couch lock")
### evidence: limited human data; mostly lay marketing
### evidence_grade: [UNVERIFIED for cannabis synergy]

## TERPENE: limonene
### class: monoterpene; fruity citrus aroma
### claims: mood elevation, anxiety reduction
### evidence_grade: [UNVERIFIED for cannabis synergy]

## TERPENE: linalool
### class: monoterpene (also in lavender)
### claims: sedation, anxiolysis
### evidence_grade: [Weak] preclinical

## TERPENE: pinene
### class: monoterpene
### claims: cognitive-enhancing, bronchodilatory
### evidence_grade: [UNVERIFIED for cannabis synergy]

### Bottom line on entourage effect
Plausible mechanism; limited rigorous human trials; commercial overclaim. A well-dosed full-spectrum product may outperform an underdosed isolate, but that's not the same as demonstrating true synergy. For CD, the Naftali 2021 positive trial with combination oil is the best human signal, but doesn't formally test synergy.

# SECTION 19 — When cannabis is NOT appropriate (CD-specific)

- **Active psychosis or schizophrenia personal history**: absolute contraindication
- **Strong family history schizophrenia**: relative; avoid THC, pure CBD lower-risk
- **Severe cardiovascular disease**: unstable angina, recent MI, severe arrhythmia, severe HF
- **Pregnancy and lactation**: contraindicated
- **Age <18 (outside strict medical supervision)**: not appropriate
- **Safety-sensitive occupations**: transportation (DOT), aviation, maritime, law enforcement, direct patient care, heavy machinery — drug screening and safety rules apply
- **Concurrent opioid use disorder in recovery**: adds cannabis exposure to recovery framework; coordinate with addiction specialist
- **Concurrent cannabis use disorder**: obvious contraindication for continued use
- **Severe liver disease** (cirrhosis Child-Pugh C): hepatic metabolism compromised; CBD hepatotoxicity risk
- **On tacrolimus or warfarin without close monitoring**: relative contraindication until plan in place
- **Active flare with obstructive symptoms**: vomiting from a stricture or CHS is a difficult differential; better to stabilize flare first
- **Post-operative period**: perioperative cannabis may affect anesthesia dosing (higher propofol requirements, altered volatile anesthetic requirements); disclose to anesthesiologist

# SECTION 20 — How to discuss with a GI doctor

### Current reality
- Many GIs have limited formal education on medical cannabis
- Attitudes range from supportive to skeptical to actively discouraging
- GI professional societies (AGA, ACG, ECCO) do not recommend cannabis for CD induction or maintenance (per current guidelines through 2024-2025)
- AGA 2024 published a Clinical Practice Update on Cannabinoid Hyperemesis Syndrome — this is the main cannabis-related GI society document of practical relevance

### How Sean can have the conversation
1. Frame it as disclosure, not permission-seeking
2. Be specific: product, dose, frequency, route
3. State purpose: "I use X mg CBD at night for sleep and abdominal pain, not as a substitute for Humira"
4. Ask about drug interactions based on current regimen
5. Ask for it to be documented in the chart (useful if adverse events, employment disputes, anesthesia)
6. Ask about monitoring: LFTs, troughs (if on tacrolimus), INR (if on warfarin)

### Medical cannabis certification in legal states
- Qualifying conditions: CD typically qualifies directly or via chronic pain in ~30 US states
- Evaluation: typically 30-minute visit with an MD/DO authorized to certify
- Cost: $50-200 evaluation + state registration $50-300
- Duration: typically 1-2 year renewals

# SECTION 22 — Safety quick reference

### Immediate red flags requiring medical evaluation
- **Cyclic vomiting with hot-bath relief** → consider CHS
- **Chest pain, palpitations, syncope** → cardiac evaluation
- **New psychotic symptoms** → psychiatric emergency
- **Vivid GI bleeding, severe abdominal pain, fever** → rule out CD flare or complication independent of cannabis
- **INR elevation on warfarin** → emergency vitamin K/FFP as needed
- **Tacrolimus levels rising, tremor, renal dysfunction** → immediate dose adjustment

### Overdose management
- Cannabis overdose is not lethal (no documented LD50 in humans)
- Severe anxiety/panic from acute THC: reassurance, quiet environment, benzodiazepine if severe, CBD can blunt THC effect
- Pediatric/accidental edible ingestion: supportive care; observe in ED; rare severe sedation or respiratory depression in small children

### When to stop cannabis
- Pregnancy confirmed or planned
- New psychotic symptoms
- Pattern of cyclic vomiting suggesting CHS
- Tacrolimus or warfarin levels unstable
- Liver enzyme elevation unexplained by other cause
- Worsening CD despite use (means it's not working for the underlying disease)

# SECTION 25 — Practical bottom line for Sean (decision support)

### For Sean, post-resection CD ~20 years, natural-health interested

**If Sean asks "Should I try cannabis/CBD for my Crohn's?"**
- Legitimate candidate for **symptomatic** management: pain, sleep, appetite, anxiety
- NOT a substitute for any guideline-directed CD maintenance
- NOT expected to heal mucosa or reduce CRP/calprotectin

**Recommended approach (if pursuing):**
1. Disclose to GI physician first
2. Start with CBD alone, 10-25 mg oral in evening
3. Titrate over 2-4 weeks to 25-50 mg BID if tolerated and beneficial
4. Track symptoms in a diary weekly
5. Baseline + 8-week LFTs
6. Add low-dose THC (2.5-5 mg oral evening) only if CBD alone insufficient and legally available
7. Prefer oral oil or sublingual tincture; avoid smoking
8. Do not discontinue any IBD maintenance therapy based on feeling better

**Red flags that should prompt medical evaluation:**
- New cyclic vomiting pattern → consider CHS
- Worsening abdominal pain despite improved subjective symptoms → CD flare hiding under symptomatic relief
- New anxiety, panic, dissociation → reduce THC, consult
- New chest pain, palpitations → cardiac workup
- Elevated LFTs → stop CBD

**Drug interactions to watch:**
- If Sean is on warfarin, tacrolimus, tofacitinib, upadacitinib, clobazam: high-stakes; monitor
- If on methotrexate or azathioprine: monitor LFTs, avoid alcohol
- Biologics (adalimumab, ustekinumab, vedolizumab, infliximab, risankizumab): no meaningful PK concern
- Mesalamine, budesonide, loperamide: no meaningful concern

**Context for natural-health orientation:**
- The ECS is a legitimate physiologic system; phytocannabinoids legitimately modulate it
- Dose matters more than "natural"; low-dose consumer CBD often underwhelms because doses are sub-therapeutic
- "Full spectrum" marketing outpaces evidence
- Quality control is a real concern; third-party COA essential
- Tobacco smoking is harmful in CD; cannabis smoking should be presumed harmful also; vaporized or oral preferred
