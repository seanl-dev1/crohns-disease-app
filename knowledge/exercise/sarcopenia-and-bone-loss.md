# Sarcopenia, Bone Loss, and Muscle Wasting in Crohn's Disease

**Scope:** Pathophysiology of how Crohn's affects the musculoskeletal system — sarcopenia prevalence, osteopenia/osteoporosis, muscle wasting mechanisms, deconditioning, and fatigue pathophysiology. Foundation context for exercise prescription.

**Evidence grading shorthand (used throughout):**
- **[A]** — Multiple RCTs / systematic review of RCTs, consistent findings
- **[B]** — At least one RCT or several well-designed non-randomized studies
- **[C]** — Small uncontrolled studies, case series, or extrapolation from adjacent fields (general IBD or non-IBD populations)
- **[D]** — Expert opinion / mechanistic reasoning only
- **[Weak]** — Marked inline where the evidence base is particularly thin

## 1. How Crohn's Affects the Musculoskeletal System

### 1.1 Sarcopenia prevalence

Sarcopenia (loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength) is one of the most under-recognized complications of IBD. Prevalence estimates depend heavily on the diagnostic criteria used (EWGSOP1/EWGSOP2, AWGS, or simple cross-sectional CT-based skeletal muscle index).

- **Crohn's disease prevalence: ~42–52%** across systematic reviews. Ulcerative colitis lower at ~28–37%. **[A]**
  - Systematic review (Ryan et al., *Clin Nutr*, 2018; PMID 29889230) — over one-third of adult IBD patients have myopenia or pre-sarcopenia; ~20% meet sarcopenia criteria.
  - More recent follow-up data (PMID 39848333) — sarcopenia in 40% of IBD patients; one-fifth with severe sarcopenia.
  - Dedola et al. systematic review (PMID 37352818) — confirmed high prevalence across disease subtypes.
- **Clinical consequences of sarcopenia in IBD:**
  - Increased therapy failure (OR ~2.0, 95% CI 1.43–2.79)
  - Higher surgical risk
  - More postoperative complications
  - Lower bone mineral density (BMD)
  - Possible association with NAFLD
  - Reference: Nardone et al. systematic review and meta-analysis (PMID 37706492); Springer IJCD meta-analysis 2025.
- **Pediatric IBD sarcopenia** (PMID 37739718) — documented but data sparse; relevant for growth impairment and steroid exposure.

### 1.2 Osteopenia and osteoporosis

- **Osteopenia: 22–77%** of IBD patients; **osteoporosis: 17–41%**.
- **Crohn's-specific:** ~32–38% with osteopenia; 30–60% with low BMD overall in Crohn's/UC. **[A]**
- Etiology is multifactorial:
  - **Corticosteroids** — 30–50% of long-term users develop osteoporosis; dose- and duration-dependent.
  - **Malabsorption** of calcium, vitamin D, vitamin K, magnesium (particularly post-ileal resection).
  - **Chronic inflammation** — TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β directly upregulate osteoclast activity (RANKL pathway) and suppress osteoblast function.
  - **Hypogonadism** from chronic illness and steroid use.
  - **Low BMI, reduced weight-bearing activity, vitamin D insufficiency.**
- References: Lima et al. (PMC 8772394); Lewis et al. (PMC 7504246); Bernstein et al. *Gastroenterology* IBD bone loss review (PMID 15785432).
- **Relevance to exercise:** Mechanical loading is one of the few modifiable levers; progressive resistance and impact training are essential adjuncts to vitamin D, calcium, and bisphosphonates where indicated.

### 1.3 Muscle wasting and protein malabsorption

- **Mechanistic drivers:**
  - Chronic inflammation elevates systemic TNF-α and IL-6 (pathological, non-exercise–induced), activating the ubiquitin–proteasome system → myofibrillar breakdown.
  - IL-6 in the chronic inflammatory context (as opposed to transient exercise-induced myokine release) is catabolic.
  - Malabsorption of protein (especially after ileal resection), anorexia, and avoidance of protein-rich foods during flares reduce anabolic substrate.
  - Corticosteroid-induced myopathy (see exercise-and-medications.md).
- **Gut dysbiosis–muscle axis:** Altered short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production in IBD may reduce butyrate-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling to muscle. Reviewed in Frontiers Immunol 2021 (PMC-indexed) "IBD and sarcopenia: role of inflammation and gut microbiota in muscle failure." **[C]**

### 1.4 Deconditioning from chronic illness

- Pediatric data (Ploeger et al., *Inflammatory Bowel Diseases*, 2012; PMID 22562735) show significantly lower anaerobic and aerobic capacity in IBD youth vs. healthy peers.
- Deconditioning is both cause and consequence: flares → bed rest → reduced VO₂max → more fatigue at any workload → further avoidance. **[B]**
- Tew et al. survey (n=877 IBD patients) found only 17% highly active, 50% minimally active, 33% inactive — roughly inverse to the general population's activity distribution.

### 1.5 Fatigue pathophysiology

- **Prevalence:** Fatigue affects **~48% of IBD patients in remission, ~72% in active disease** (D'Silva et al., *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol*, 2022; PMID 34216824).
- **Proposed mechanisms** — all relevant to exercise prescription:
  1. **Proinflammatory cytokines** (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) cross the blood–brain barrier and act on central fatigue pathways.
  2. **Anemia** — iron deficiency anemia in >40% of Crohn's patients reduces oxygen-carrying capacity and aerobic tolerance.
  3. **Sleep disruption** — nocturnal diarrhea, pain.
  4. **Micronutrient deficiencies** — B12 (especially after terminal ileal resection), folate, vitamin D, magnesium.
  5. **HPA axis dysregulation** and depression/anxiety comorbidity.
  6. **Deconditioning** (see §1.4) — circular cause and consequence.
  7. **Medications** — biologics, immunomodulators, steroids all contribute variably.
- Important clinical point: central (brain-derived) fatigue responds better to low-dose graded exercise than to rest (see fatigue-specific-prescription.md).
