# Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation in IBD

**Scope:** Dysfunction prevalence in IBD (urgency/incontinence/dyssynergia), biofeedback + PFMT evidence, when Kegels are actively wrong (hypertonic/painful/dyssynergic), the Knack maneuver for urge control, and a down-training-first progression.

**Evidence grading shorthand:**
- **[A]** — Multiple RCTs / systematic review of RCTs, consistent findings
- **[B]** — At least one RCT or several well-designed non-randomized studies
- **[C]** — Small uncontrolled studies, case series, or extrapolation from adjacent fields
- **[D]** — Expert opinion / mechanistic reasoning only

## 10. Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation

### 10.1 Prevalence of dysfunction in IBD

- Fecal urgency in ~30–50% of active Crohn's; ongoing urgency/incontinence in ~15–25% of remission patients.
- Post-proctectomy, post-pouch, and post-sphincter-affecting surgeries increase rates substantially.
- Pelvic floor **dyssynergia** (paradoxical contraction during defecation) overlaps with constipation-predominant symptoms even in Crohn's.

Reference: Khera et al., PMID 26603727 (pelvic floor dysfunction in IBD review).

### 10.2 Biofeedback evidence

**Systematic review (JGH Open, PMC 6891014):**
- Non-randomized studies: 65–80% of patients improve after pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) ± biofeedback
- **RCTs specific to IBD are few**; most high-quality biofeedback data come from functional bowel disorder and obstetric-injury populations
- In IBD-inactive patients: 68% improvement in evacuation difficulty, 80% in fecal incontinence (limited quality)

**Jelovsek et al.** *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol*, supervised PFMT + biofeedback vs attention control → superior for fecal incontinence. Not IBD-specific.

### 10.3 Kegels — when NOT to do them

**Strengthening Kegels are WRONG when the pelvic floor is:**
- **Hypertonic / non-relaxing** — paradoxical contraction, anismus, tailbone pain, difficulty initiating urine stream, incomplete defecation
- **Painful** — endometriosis, vaginismus, chronic pelvic pain syndrome, pudendal neuralgia
- **Dyssynergic** — uncoordinated contraction during defecation attempts

In these cases, Kegels worsen symptoms. **Down-training** (relaxation, reverse Kegel, diaphragmatic breathing, perineal bulge on exhale, pelvic stretches) is the correct initial strategy.

**Clinical implication:** Pelvic floor assessment by a trained physical therapist should precede home Kegel programs in IBD patients, particularly those with urgency that paradoxically feels like "tightness" or "pressure."

### 10.4 The Knack maneuver

**Miller, Ashton-Miller & DeLancey (*J Am Geriatr Soc*, 1998; PMID 9670874):**
- Voluntary pelvic floor pre-contraction immediately before and during an increase in intra-abdominal pressure (cough, sneeze, lift)
- In stress urinary incontinence: reduces leakage by ~73–98% (wetted area reduced from 43.2 cm² to 6.9 cm²)
- **Mechanism:** Pre-contraction stabilizes the urethrovesical junction and in analogy, the anorectal junction — reducing displacement during abdominal strain
- **Extrapolation to IBD fecal urgency [C]:** Pre-contraction before expected urge-provoking moments (rising from chair, starting to walk, encountering bathroom) may help maintain continence; taught as part of PFMT programs

### 10.5 Practical PFMT progression

1. **Down-training first** — diaphragmatic breathing, reverse Kegel, child's pose, happy baby, supine perineal release (3–5 min, 2x/day, 2 weeks)
2. **Awareness** — identify contraction and relaxation separately; confirm with physical therapist if possible
3. **Strengthening** (only if hypotonic): 3 sets × 10 short (2 sec) + 10 long (10 sec) holds, 3x/day
4. **Functional integration** — Knack maneuver with cough, lift, laugh; urge suppression before bathroom
5. **Progressive loading** — Kegels during standing, squatting, lifting
