# 09 — Exercise and Movement in Crohn's Disease

**Scope:** Reference file for Claude to use when answering questions about exercise physiology, movement, and rehabilitation in Crohn's disease. Covers pathophysiology, evidence for exercise interventions, prescriptive detail, contraindications, and post-operative considerations.

**Evidence grading shorthand (used throughout):**
- **[A]** — Multiple RCTs / systematic review of RCTs, consistent findings
- **[B]** — At least one RCT or several well-designed non-randomized studies
- **[C]** — Small uncontrolled studies, case series, or extrapolation from adjacent fields (general IBD or non-IBD populations)
- **[D]** — Expert opinion / mechanistic reasoning only
- **[Weak]** — Marked inline where the evidence base is particularly thin

## 1. How Crohn's Affects the Musculoskeletal System

### 1.1 Sarcopenia prevalence

Sarcopenia (loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength) is one of the most under-recognized complications of IBD. Prevalence estimates depend heavily on the diagnostic criteria used (EWGSOP1/EWGSOP2, AWGS, or simple cross-sectional CT-based skeletal muscle index).

- **Crohn's disease prevalence: ~42–52%** across systematic reviews. Ulcerative colitis lower at ~28–37%. **[A]**
  - Systematic review (Ryan et al., *Clin Nutr*, 2018; PMID 29889230) — over one-third of adult IBD patients have myopenia or pre-sarcopenia; ~20% meet sarcopenia criteria.
  - More recent follow-up data (PMID 39848333) — sarcopenia in 40% of IBD patients; one-fifth with severe sarcopenia.
  - Dedola et al. systematic review (PMID 37352818) — confirmed high prevalence across disease subtypes.
- **Clinical consequences of sarcopenia in IBD:**
  - Increased therapy failure (OR ~2.0, 95% CI 1.43–2.79)
  - Higher surgical risk
  - More postoperative complications
  - Lower bone mineral density (BMD)
  - Possible association with NAFLD
  - Reference: Nardone et al. systematic review and meta-analysis (PMID 37706492); Springer IJCD meta-analysis 2025.
- **Pediatric IBD sarcopenia** (PMID 37739718) — documented but data sparse; relevant for growth impairment and steroid exposure.

### 1.2 Osteopenia and osteoporosis

- **Osteopenia: 22–77%** of IBD patients; **osteoporosis: 17–41%**.
- **Crohn's-specific:** ~32–38% with osteopenia; 30–60% with low BMD overall in Crohn's/UC. **[A]**
- Etiology is multifactorial:
  - **Corticosteroids** — 30–50% of long-term users develop osteoporosis; dose- and duration-dependent.
  - **Malabsorption** of calcium, vitamin D, vitamin K, magnesium (particularly post-ileal resection).
  - **Chronic inflammation** — TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β directly upregulate osteoclast activity (RANKL pathway) and suppress osteoblast function.
  - **Hypogonadism** from chronic illness and steroid use.
  - **Low BMI, reduced weight-bearing activity, vitamin D insufficiency.**
- References: Lima et al. (PMC 8772394); Lewis et al. (PMC 7504246); Bernstein et al. *Gastroenterology* IBD bone loss review (PMID 15785432).
- **Relevance to exercise:** Mechanical loading is one of the few modifiable levers; progressive resistance and impact training are essential adjuncts to vitamin D, calcium, and bisphosphonates where indicated.

### 1.3 Muscle wasting and protein malabsorption

- **Mechanistic drivers:**
  - Chronic inflammation elevates systemic TNF-α and IL-6 (pathological, non-exercise–induced), activating the ubiquitin–proteasome system → myofibrillar breakdown.
  - IL-6 in the chronic inflammatory context (as opposed to transient exercise-induced myokine release) is catabolic.
  - Malabsorption of protein (especially after ileal resection), anorexia, and avoidance of protein-rich foods during flares reduce anabolic substrate.
  - Corticosteroid-induced myopathy (see §14).
- **Gut dysbiosis–muscle axis:** Altered short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production in IBD may reduce butyrate-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling to muscle. Reviewed in Frontiers Immunol 2021 (PMC-indexed) "IBD and sarcopenia: role of inflammation and gut microbiota in muscle failure." **[C]**

### 1.4 Deconditioning from chronic illness

- Pediatric data (Ploeger et al., *Inflammatory Bowel Diseases*, 2012; PMID 22562735) show significantly lower anaerobic and aerobic capacity in IBD youth vs. healthy peers.
- Deconditioning is both cause and consequence: flares → bed rest → reduced VO₂max → more fatigue at any workload → further avoidance. **[B]**
- Tew et al. survey (n=877 IBD patients) found only 17% highly active, 50% minimally active, 33% inactive — roughly inverse to the general population's activity distribution.

### 1.5 Fatigue pathophysiology

- **Prevalence:** Fatigue affects **~48% of IBD patients in remission, ~72% in active disease** (D'Silva et al., *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol*, 2022; PMID 34216824).
- **Proposed mechanisms** — all relevant to exercise prescription:
  1. **Proinflammatory cytokines** (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) cross the blood–brain barrier and act on central fatigue pathways.
  2. **Anemia** — iron deficiency anemia in >40% of Crohn's patients reduces oxygen-carrying capacity and aerobic tolerance.
  3. **Sleep disruption** — nocturnal diarrhea, pain.
  4. **Micronutrient deficiencies** — B12 (especially after terminal ileal resection), folate, vitamin D, magnesium.
  5. **HPA axis dysregulation** and depression/anxiety comorbidity.
  6. **Deconditioning** (see §1.4) — circular cause and consequence.
  7. **Medications** — biologics, immunomodulators, steroids all contribute variably.
- Important clinical point: central (brain-derived) fatigue responds better to low-dose graded exercise than to rest (see §15).

## 2. Exercise Effects on Crohn's Disease — The Evidence Base

### 2.1 Anti-inflammatory mechanisms

**Core concept:** Skeletal muscle, when contracting, is an endocrine organ. Petersen & Pedersen (*J Appl Physiol*, 2005; PMID 15772055) first labeled IL-6 as a myokine. Pedersen & Febbraio (*Nat Rev Endocrinol*, 2012; PMID 22473333) expanded this to the muscle secretome of hundreds of peptides.

**The exercise–cytokine cascade (acute exercise bout):**
1. Muscle contraction → IL-6 release via a **TNF-independent pathway** (critical distinction from pathological IL-6).
2. Myokine IL-6 → stimulates circulating IL-1ra (IL-1 receptor antagonist) and IL-10 (anti-inflammatory).
3. IL-10 and IL-1ra → suppress TNF-α production.
4. Net effect over weeks of training: reduced chronic low-grade inflammation.

**Additional anti-inflammatory mechanisms of chronic training:**
- **Reduced visceral adiposity** — visceral fat is a major source of inflammatory adipokines in IBD (Bilski et al., *BioMed Res Int*, 2014; PMID 24877092 / PMC 4022156).
- **Vagal tone enhancement** → cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (α7 nAChR on macrophages).
- **Improved gut barrier integrity** at moderate intensities (in animal models, exercise decreases intestinal permeability; the opposite at extreme intensities — see §6).
- **Microbiome shifts** — moderate exercise associated with increased SCFA-producing taxa in healthy populations; IBD-specific data weaker. **[C]**
- **Reductions in hs-CRP and fecal calprotectin** in some but not all trials (mixed) — see Perez-Martinez 2023 systematic review (PMID 37227593, *Int J Colorectal Dis*) on exercise + inflammation + body composition in IBD.

### 2.2 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

| Reference | PMID | Studies / n | Key finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jones, Kimble, Baker, Tew. *PLoS One*, 2022 | 36454911 | 15 studies (9 RCTs), n=637 | Exercise improved disease activity (SMD ≈ –0.44). IBDQ QoL not significantly changed (MD 3.52, 95% CI –2.00 to 9.04). Benefits in fatigue, muscular function, body composition, CRF, BMD, psychological well-being. Low certainty for disease activity, very low for QoL. 14 minor AEs. |
| Tiong et al. *J Crohns Colitis*, 2024 | 38597690 | Meta-analysis | Physical activity inversely associated with **risk of developing IBD**. For Crohn's: cohort RR 0.78 (high vs low activity); case-control RR 0.87. For UC: cohort RR 0.62. |
| Pérez-Martínez et al. *Int J Colorectal Dis*, 2023 | 37227593 | Systematic review on structured exercise + inflammation + body composition | Mixed CRP/calprotectin signals; modest body-composition benefits. |
| Eckert et al. *Clin Exp Gastroenterol*, 2019 | PMC 5743119 | Narrative review | Exercise generally safe in remission and mild disease; benefits across QoL, fatigue, BMD. |
| Engels et al. / earlier narrative reviews | — | — | Concordant conclusion: moderate exercise doesn't exacerbate disease in most patients; high-intensity data limited but emerging favorable. |

### 2.3 Disease activity — does exercise lower flare risk?

- **Prospective CCFA Partners cohort** (Jones et al., earlier work) — patients with higher baseline physical activity had **lower odds of active disease at 6 months** (HR reductions ~0.7–0.8 range, adjusted; "Exercise Decreases Risk of Future Active Disease in Patients with IBD in Remission").
- **Cramer yoga trial** (PMID 28378342) — disease activity reduced in yoga arm at 24 weeks (secondary endpoint).
- **Jones 2022 meta-analysis** — SMD improvement in disease activity ~–0.44 favoring exercise.
- **Caveat:** Most exercise trials exclude patients with severe active disease; evidence does not support exercise as treatment of active moderate–severe flares.

**Bottom line [B]:** Regular moderate exercise in remission probably reduces future flare risk modestly. High-certainty data is still lacking.

### 2.4 Quality of life and fatigue

- **Ng et al. *Clin J Sport Med*, 2007** (PMID 17873551) — low-intensity walking program (3x/week, 12 weeks) in 32 adults with mild/remission Crohn's → improved QoL, no disease exacerbations. Small sample, often cited.
- **Cronin et al. / Jones et al. combined impact + resistance RCT** (*Aliment Pharmacol Ther*, 2020; PMID 33119156) — 6 months, 47 adults with stable Crohn's; exercise arm improved lumbar spine BMD, muscular function, fatigue severity, and QoL.
- **Tew et al. HIIT vs MICT pilot RCT** (*BMC Gastroenterol*, 2019; PMID 30696423) — 36 participants; peak VO₂ improved more with HIIT (+2.4 vs +0.7 mL/kg/min over control); fatigue improved in both arms; 3 non-serious exercise-related AEs; 2 disease relapses during follow-up (not clearly exercise-attributable).
- **Fatigue specifically** — exercise improvements are consistent across interventions, even when disease activity is unchanged, suggesting independent mechanism (central/deconditioning contributions).

## 3. Exercise During Remission — What's Safe

### 3.1 General prescription (extrapolated from ACSM + IBD-specific data)

**Aerobic:**
- **Frequency:** 3–5 days/week
- **Intensity:** Moderate (40–60% VO₂R, ~RPE 12–14/20, "talk test" preserved) — most robust evidence base
- **Time:** 30–60 min continuous, or accumulated in 10-min bouts
- **Type:** Walking, cycling, swimming, elliptical, rowing — low- to moderate-impact preferred
- **Total volume:** 150 min/week moderate or 75 min/week vigorous (aligns with WHO/ACSM general guidance)

**Resistance:**
- **Frequency:** 2–3 non-consecutive days/week
- **Intensity:** Start 50–60% 1RM; progress to 60–80%
- **Volume:** 1–3 sets × 8–12 reps, 6–10 major muscle group exercises
- **Progression:** 2–10% weekly load increase when top of rep range reached with good form

**Flexibility / neuromotor:** 2–3 days/week (yoga, tai chi, mobility).

### 3.2 HIIT in IBD

- **Evidence [B]:** Tolerated and safe in mild/remission Crohn's (Tew 2019 PMID 30696423; single-session pediatric data, Cronin et al.). No exacerbation of fecal calprotectin 24 h or 1 week post-event in endurance athletes with CD (small prospective series).
- **Caveats:**
  - Data do not extend to moderate–severe active disease.
  - Consider symptom-titration approach in first 6–12 weeks of training.
  - Pediatric CD data show monocyte inflammatory response differs from healthy controls but does not translate to clinical flare.

**Reasonable protocol example:** 10 × 1 min at 85–95% HRmax with 1 min active recovery; 2x/week; alongside 1–2 sessions continuous moderate work. Progress from 4–6 intervals initially.

### 3.3 Team sports / running / cycling / swimming — tradeoffs

| Modality | Pros | Cons / Cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Walking** | Universal safe baseline, low-impact, cumulative | Limited stimulus for VO₂max gains; need progression |
| **Running** | High BMD stimulus, time-efficient, cardioprotective | Runner's diarrhea (§6); high mechanical impact on abdomen; bathroom planning |
| **Cycling** | Low-impact, controllable intensity | Saddle pressure uncomfortable with active perianal disease or hemorrhoids |
| **Swimming** | Zero-impact, whole-body, cooling effect | Pool access; ostomy/pouch considerations (§13); locker-room bathroom proximity |
| **Rowing / elliptical** | Low-impact, high aerobic stimulus | Forward-folded rowing posture can aggravate acute abdominal pain |
| **Team sports** | Social engagement, motivation | Scheduling unpredictable; contact sports have abdominal trauma risk, especially with stoma |
| **Resistance training** | BMD, sarcopenia prevention, metabolic | Valsalva and hernia considerations (§7) |
| **Yoga / Pilates** | Flexibility, stress reduction, parasympathetic | Deep abdominal compression (e.g., bow, full locust) contraindicated in active disease |

## 4. Exercise During a Flare — What's Safe

### 4.1 General principles

- **Mild flare** (Crohn's Disease Activity Index, CDAI 150–220; or Harvey–Bradshaw Index 5–7): light–moderate exercise often tolerated; reduce intensity ~30–50%, shorten duration, favor low-impact.
- **Moderate flare** (CDAI 220–450; HBI 8–16): restorative movement only (gentle walking, chair yoga, breathing, light mobility). Avoid resistance training progression and HIIT.
- **Severe flare** (CDAI >450; HBI >16; systemic symptoms, severe pain, bloody diarrhea, fever): rest. Exercise is contraindicated; focus on medical stabilization.

The Hoffman & Raftery 2023 commentary ("Intensity-specific considerations for exercise for patients with IBD," *Gastroenterology Report*) supports this intensity-layered approach. **[C/D]** — based on expert consensus and limited direct trial data.

### 4.2 Signs to stop or step down

- Worsening abdominal pain during or after exercise (not typical DOMS)
- Increased stool frequency, urgency, or blood
- Unusual fatigue persisting >24 h after normal exercise (post-exertional malaise pattern)
- Fever or night sweats appearing with exertion
- Orthostatic dizziness (consider anemia or dehydration)
- New joint swelling/pain (possible extraintestinal manifestation flare)

### 4.3 Low-impact options during flare

- **Walking** — start at 5–15 min, RPE 9–11; even very short bouts preserve conditioning and improve mood.
- **Restorative yoga** — supported poses, minimal core/abdominal compression.
- **Tai chi / qigong** — low metabolic cost, balance and vagal tone benefit (see §9).
- **Aquatic exercise** — buoyancy offloads joints; warm water (~32–34°C) reduces muscle tension. Contraindicated if perianal fistula with active drainage.
- **Breathing practices** (see §11) — essentially no contraindications short of pneumothorax.
- **Gentle resistance with very light loads / bands** — preserves motor pattern without significant inflammation.

## 5. Post-Surgical Exercise Timeline

### 5.1 Immediate post-operative (day 0–7)

- **Walking encouraged from day 1.** Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols emphasize early ambulation to reduce VTE risk, ileus duration, and atelectasis.
- **Initial targets:** 3–5 short walks/day (5–10 min), progressing to 15–20 min as tolerated.
- **No lifting >4–5 kg (10 lb) in first week** (laparoscopic) / stricter for open laparotomy.

### 5.2 Weeks 1–6 — the critical healing window

- **Collagen remodeling at incision site** reaches ~70% of ultimate tensile strength by 6 weeks.
- **Restrictions (open/major resection):**
  - No lifting >4.5–6.8 kg (10–15 lb)
  - No direct abdominal loading (crunches, sit-ups, planks, Pilates hundreds)
  - No Valsalva / maximal strain activities
  - Avoid high-impact running and jumping
- **Laparoscopic** (less tissue disruption): some surveys support as little as 2 weeks reduced activity (Frontiers Surg, PMC 8511488), though 4–6 weeks is the commonly recommended conservative window. **[B/C]**
- **Permitted by week 2–4:** Recumbent or upright stationary cycling (low resistance), swimming once incision fully healed (usually ~3 weeks, confirm no open wound), elliptical at low intensity.

### 5.3 Weeks 6–12 — graded resumption

- Reintroduce resistance training at ~50% of pre-op loads; emphasize form and full ROM.
- Core training begins with diaphragmatic breathing → dead bugs → bird dogs → progressive anti-rotation and anti-extension work.
- Avoid maximal-effort breath-holding; exhale through the effort phase.
- Compound lifts (squats, deadlifts, bench press) typically safe by 8–12 weeks; keep weights conservative initially.

### 5.4 Beyond 12 weeks — lifelong hernia-risk considerations

- **Incisional hernia rates:** ~10–20% at 2 years post-open abdominal surgery; higher with wound infection, steroids, obesity, malnutrition.
- **Lifelong caution** for very heavy lifting (1RM deadlifts, max back squats) in unconditioned patients; not a strict prohibition but demands proper progression, bracing technique, and awareness of bulging or pain at old incision.
- **Port-site hernias** after laparoscopy are possible — typically where trocars >10 mm were placed.
- Reference: Kjøbli/Benjamin et al., international survey on post-abdominal-surgery activity (PMC 8511488).

### 5.5 Sean-specific note

Sean is ~20 years post-resection — well beyond all acute surgical restrictions. Relevant residual considerations:
- Incision-site tissue weaker than uninvolved abdominal wall for life → respect proper bracing with heavy lifts
- Potential for adhesive obstruction — occasional cramping with heavy meals + vigorous exercise warrants medical attention, not just "push through"
- Bile acid malabsorption possible post-ileal resection (see §12)

## 6. Running and Crohn's — Practical Considerations

### 6.1 Runner's diarrhea — mechanism

Two pathways (de Oliveira et al., *Sports Med*, 2014; Costa et al. frameworks):

1. **Circulatory–gastrointestinal pathway.** At ~70% VO₂max, splanchnic blood flow decreases up to 80%. Mucosal hypoxia → ATP depletion → tight junction disruption → increased intestinal permeability → LPS translocation → systemic inflammatory response. Prolonged: mucosal ischemia, exercise-induced colitis.
2. **Neuroendocrine–gastrointestinal pathway.** Sympathetic drive during exercise accelerates colonic motility, reduces absorption, and can precipitate urgency.

**Prevalence:** 30–90% of endurance runners report GI symptoms. Likely higher in IBD runners (no direct comparative trials).

Reference: Pugh et al. *Med Sci Sports Exerc* (PMC 5694518) — intestinal permeability and GI function markers in symptomatic vs asymptomatic runners.

### 6.2 Dehydration and electrolytes

- Sweat losses of 500–1500 mL/h; sodium ~0.5–2.0 g/L sweat.
- IBD patients, especially post–ileal resection, may have baseline volume/sodium depletion.
- See §12 for detailed rehydration guidance.

### 6.3 Timing meals around runs

Pragmatic strategy (extrapolated from sports nutrition + IBD practice):
- **3–4 h pre-run:** full meal, low-fat, low-residue, moderate protein.
- **1–2 h pre-run:** small, low-fiber, low-FODMAP snack (banana, white toast with almond butter, rice cake).
- **<60 min pre-run:** sip only — water or dilute electrolyte.
- **Avoid pre-run:** high-fat meals (delay gastric emptying), high-fiber (osmotic draw, colonic fermentation), sugar alcohols, caffeine if prokinetic for you, dairy if lactose-intolerant.

### 6.4 Bathroom and route planning

- Map toilets along routes (iOS/Android toilet apps; out-and-back near home/park with facilities).
- Carry a go-bag (wipes, change of shorts, plastic bag) for longer runs.
- Loperamide (2–4 mg, 30–60 min pre-run) is commonly used prophylactically by IBD runners in remission — should be cleared with GI. Not appropriate in active flare or in unexplained diarrhea.
- Avoid dehydration-induced ischemia by pre-hydrating and sipping during runs.

### 6.5 Fiber before exercise

- **Pre-run:** Lower residue, lower FODMAP in the 12–24 h before a long/hard run.
- **Non-exercise days / off-season:** Restore usual fiber intake for microbiome benefits.
- In Crohn's with any known stricture: cumulative fiber modulation is also a stricture/obstruction-risk tool (see §17).

## 7. Resistance Training Specifics

### 7.1 Protein needs

- **ESPEN IBD guideline** (Bischoff et al., *Clin Nutr*, 2023; PMID 36739756; original 2017 guideline PMID 28131521):
  - **Active disease:** 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day
  - **Remission:** ~1.0 g/kg/day (as general population)
- **For active resistance-training IBD patients:** evidence-informed target of **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**, potentially up to **1.8–2.0 g/kg/day** post-operatively or during recomposition phases (extrapolation from ISSN / ACSM sports nutrition stands). **[C]**
- **Whey protein + leucine + vitamin D + resistance training** — significant benefit in sarcopenia (PROVIDE trial, *J Am Med Dir Assoc*; and systematic reviews PMID 36771225, 32928579). Whey has anti-inflammatory and intestinal-barrier-supporting properties that may benefit IBD specifically. **[B]** for sarcopenia generally; **[C]** for IBD-specific extrapolation.
- Spread protein across 3–4 meals, ~0.3–0.4 g/kg per meal, for optimal muscle protein synthesis pulses.

### 7.2 Bone loading for osteoporosis prevention

- **Resistance + impact combined** (Robinson et al., *Gastroenterology*, 1998; PMID 9649456): progressive low-impact exercise over 12 months in 117 Crohn's patients → significant BMD gain at greater trochanter (mean difference 4.67 g/cm², 95% CI 0.86–8.48; P=0.02). Hip/spine non-significant but trending.
- **Jones et al. RCT** (*APT*, 2020; PMID 33119156): 6 months combined impact + resistance → improved **lumbar spine BMD**.
- **Mechanical loading principles** (Frost's mechanostat):
  - Strain magnitude > habitual loading is required
  - Impact (jumps, hops, running) drives greater osteogenic response than isometric loading
  - Few, high-strain cycles outperform many low-strain cycles
  - Variety of directions/patterns superior to single-vector loading
- **Practical osteogenic programming for IBD:**
  - Resistance: squats, deadlifts, step-ups, overhead press, rows — 2–3x/week
  - Impact: jump rope, box jumps, hop variations, plyometric step-ups — 2x/week, short sessions (5–10 min), start 10–20 foot contacts and build gradually
  - Modify impact based on pelvic floor status and knee/hip joint health

### 7.3 Programming — major muscle groups, 2–3x/week

**Minimum effective dose** (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., PMC 11127831) — 1 set of resistance per muscle group per week produces measurable strength gains. Reasonable IBD starting point.

**Intermediate target:**
- Lower body (quads, hamstrings, glutes, calves): 2x/week
- Upper body push (chest, shoulders, triceps): 2x/week
- Upper body pull (back, biceps): 2x/week
- Core anti-rotation/anti-extension: 2–3x/week

**Sample 3-day split (remission, intermediate):**
- **Day A:** Goblet squat, Romanian deadlift, dumbbell bench press, row, carry variation
- **Day B:** Step-up, hip thrust, overhead press, lat pulldown, anti-rotation (Pallof press)
- **Day C:** Deadlift (moderate), split squat, incline press, face pull, farmer carry

### 7.4 Avoiding Valsalva with abdominal hernia concerns

- **Valsalva maneuver** (forceful expiration against closed glottis) spikes intra-abdominal pressure to 100–300 mmHg — useful for heavy lifting to stabilize spine, but increases hernia risk at surgical sites and with stoma.
- **Post-resection / stoma patients:**
  - Exhale through exertion phase rather than breath-hold
  - Avoid 1RM testing
  - Cap loads at ~80% 1RM or ~RPE 8 (2 reps in reserve)
  - Consider abdominal binder or stoma support belt during compound lifts (§13)
- **Core training without Valsalva:** dead bug, bird dog, Pallof press, suitcase carry, side plank (modified) — favor anti-movement over movement-through-range.

## 8. Yoga for IBD — What the RCTs Show

### 8.1 Cramer et al. 2017 — landmark yoga RCT

**Cramer H, Schäfer M, Schöls M, et al. Randomised clinical trial: yoga vs written self-care advice for ulcerative colitis.** *Aliment Pharmacol Ther*. 2017;45(11):1379–1389. **PMID 28378342.**

- n=77 adults with UC in clinical remission + impaired QoL
- Randomized to 12 weekly 90-min supervised hatha yoga sessions vs. written self-care booklet
- **Primary outcome (QoL, IBDQ):** significantly higher in yoga group at 12 and 24 weeks
- Secondary: disease activity (Clinical Activity Index) lower in yoga group at 24 weeks
- Mediating variable (Langhorst et al., 2019, secondary analysis PMID 31927346): effect mediated by reduced perceived stress
- Adverse events comparable between groups
- **Evidence grade: [A]** for UC in remission; extrapolation to Crohn's **[C]**

### 8.2 Sharma et al. 2015 — yoga in IBD

**Sharma P, Poojary G, Dwivedi SN, Deepak KK. Effect of yoga-based intervention in patients with inflammatory bowel disease.** *Int J Yoga Therap*. 2015;25(1):101–112. **PMID 26667293.**

- Smaller mixed IBD sample
- 8-week yoga intervention → improved anxiety, pain, IBS-like symptoms; less clear effect on disease activity
- Methodologically weaker than Cramer

### 8.3 Narrative reviews

- Balaji et al. *Crohns Colitis 360*, 2022 (PMC 9802393) — yoga effective for QoL and mental health in IBD; insufficient evidence for disease remission maintenance
- Other supportive small trials report reductions in depression, anxiety, perceived stress; benefits on fatigue reported but variable

### 8.4 Style and pose-specific guidance

**Favored styles in IBD:**
- **Restorative / Yin** — parasympathetic dominance, low mechanical stress, safe during mild flares
- **Gentle Hatha** — the style used in Cramer trial; the most evidence-supported
- **Iyengar** — props emphasize alignment and sustainable holds

**More caution:**
- **Vinyasa / Power / Ashtanga** — appropriate in remission with good baseline fitness; higher intensity
- **Hot yoga / Bikram** — dehydration risk; generally inadvisable in IBD with ongoing fluid losses

**Pose considerations:**
- **Emphasize:**
  - Child's pose (balasana) — gentle abdominal release
  - Supine twists (supta matsyendrasana) — gentle GI motility support
  - Cat–cow — spinal mobility with controlled abdominal movement
  - Legs up the wall (viparita karani) — venous return, parasympathetic
  - Bridge (setu bandhasana) — glute/hamstring activation, mild back extension
  - Diaphragmatic pranayama (see §11)
- **Avoid or modify during active disease:**
  - Deep forward folds with abdominal compression (full paschimottanasana, janu sirsasana with full flexion)
  - Bow pose (dhanurasana), locust (salabhasana) — deep anterior abdominal compression
  - Full boat (navasana) at maximum effort — rectus abdominis maximal contraction
  - Inversions (headstand, shoulder stand) — if uncontrolled hypertension, retinopathy, severe reflux, or perianal hemorrhoids
  - Deep twists with abdominal compression in the presence of strictures

## 9. Tai Chi / Qigong — Emerging Evidence

### 9.1 IBD-specific evidence — sparse

**No adequately-powered RCTs specifically on tai chi or qigong for IBD.** Current best estimates derive from:
- Adjacent chronic-illness conditions (ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, chronic pain, post-COVID)
- Mechanistic parasympathetic tone / HRV literature

### 9.2 Adjacent-condition evidence

- **Wang C et al. *NEJM*, 2010** — tai chi for fibromyalgia: significant fatigue, pain, depression improvements vs wellness control
- **Markwart et al. *Glob Adv Integr Med Health*, 2024 (PMC 11544658)** — systematic review on qigong/tai chi for ME/CFS: consistent fatigue reduction (Chalder Fatigue Scale, MFI-20)
- **Chronic-illness meta-analyses** (Jogh.org 2023; Science Direct reviews) — tai chi broadly improves HRQoL in chronic conditions

### 9.3 Mechanistic plausibility for IBD fatigue

- Autonomic rebalancing (vagal tone ↑, sympathetic ↓)
- Low metabolic cost (~2–4 METs) — accessible during partial recovery and mild flare
- Weight-bearing, modest BMD benefit
- Stress reduction through meditative movement

**Practical prescription [C/D]:**
- 20–30 min, 3–5 sessions/week
- Minimum 9 weeks to see effects per qigong/ME-CFS meta-analyses
- Accessible apps: Tai Chi Foundation resources; online Yang-style 24-form video instruction

## 10. Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation

### 10.1 Prevalence of dysfunction in IBD

- Fecal urgency in ~30–50% of active Crohn's; ongoing urgency/incontinence in ~15–25% of remission patients.
- Post-proctectomy, post-pouch, and post-sphincter-affecting surgeries increase rates substantially.
- Pelvic floor **dyssynergia** (paradoxical contraction during defecation) overlaps with constipation-predominant symptoms even in Crohn's.

Reference: Khera et al., PMID 26603727 (pelvic floor dysfunction in IBD review).

### 10.2 Biofeedback evidence

**Systematic review (JGH Open, PMC 6891014):**
- Non-randomized studies: 65–80% of patients improve after pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) ± biofeedback
- **RCTs specific to IBD are few**; most high-quality biofeedback data come from functional bowel disorder and obstetric-injury populations
- In IBD-inactive patients: 68% improvement in evacuation difficulty, 80% in fecal incontinence (limited quality)

**Jelovsek et al.** *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol*, supervised PFMT + biofeedback vs attention control → superior for fecal incontinence. Not IBD-specific.

### 10.3 Kegels — when NOT to do them

**Strengthening Kegels are WRONG when the pelvic floor is:**
- **Hypertonic / non-relaxing** — paradoxical contraction, anismus, tailbone pain, difficulty initiating urine stream, incomplete defecation
- **Painful** — endometriosis, vaginismus, chronic pelvic pain syndrome, pudendal neuralgia
- **Dyssynergic** — uncoordinated contraction during defecation attempts

In these cases, Kegels worsen symptoms. **Down-training** (relaxation, reverse Kegel, diaphragmatic breathing, perineal bulge on exhale, pelvic stretches) is the correct initial strategy.

**Clinical implication:** Pelvic floor assessment by a trained physical therapist should precede home Kegel programs in IBD patients, particularly those with urgency that paradoxically feels like "tightness" or "pressure."

### 10.4 The Knack maneuver

**Miller, Ashton-Miller & DeLancey (*J Am Geriatr Soc*, 1998; PMID 9670874):**
- Voluntary pelvic floor pre-contraction immediately before and during an increase in intra-abdominal pressure (cough, sneeze, lift)
- In stress urinary incontinence: reduces leakage by ~73–98% (wetted area reduced from 43.2 cm² to 6.9 cm²)
- **Mechanism:** Pre-contraction stabilizes the urethrovesical junction and in analogy, the anorectal junction — reducing displacement during abdominal strain
- **Extrapolation to IBD fecal urgency [C]:** Pre-contraction before expected urge-provoking moments (rising from chair, starting to walk, encountering bathroom) may help maintain continence; taught as part of PFMT programs

### 10.5 Practical PFMT progression

1. **Down-training first** — diaphragmatic breathing, reverse Kegel, child's pose, happy baby, supine perineal release (3–5 min, 2x/day, 2 weeks)
2. **Awareness** — identify contraction and relaxation separately; confirm with physical therapist if possible
3. **Strengthening** (only if hypotonic): 3 sets × 10 short (2 sec) + 10 long (10 sec) holds, 3x/day
4. **Functional integration** — Knack maneuver with cough, lift, laugh; urge suppression before bathroom
5. **Progressive loading** — Kegels during standing, squatting, lifting

## 11. Breathing Exercises and Vagal Tone

### 11.1 Physiological basis

- The **vagus nerve** (CN X) carries ~80% afferent (gut → brain) and ~20% efferent (brain → gut) fibers.
- Diaphragmatic excursion mechanically stretches vagal afferents; slow exhalation maximally activates parasympathetic outflow.
- **Respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA)** — HR rises with inspiration, falls with expiration; deeper breathing = larger RSA = higher HRV = better vagal tone.
- **Resonance frequency breathing** (~0.1 Hz = 6 breaths/min) maximally amplifies baroreflex gain and parasympathetic activity.

Reference: Gerritsen & Band, *Front Hum Neurosci*, 2018 (PMC 6189422) — respiratory vagal stimulation model; Magnon et al. *Sci Rep*, 2021 — deep slow breathing increases HF-HRV and reduces anxiety.

### 11.2 Protocols (all evidence [B/C] for general population; extrapolated to IBD [C])

| Protocol | Pattern | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| **Diaphragmatic breathing** | Belly expands on inhale, 4–6 sec in, 6–8 sec out, nasal breathing | Daily baseline; gut-directed |
| **4-7-8 breathing** | 4 in / 7 hold / 8 out | Sleep onset, acute anxiety, pre-bathroom urgency |
| **Box breathing** | 4-4-4-4 | Stress-down-regulation during workday |
| **Resonance breathing** | ~6 breaths/min, roughly 5 in / 5 out | HRV biofeedback training |
| **Slow exhale emphasis** | 1:2 in:out ratio | Acute parasympathetic boost |

### 11.3 Gut-specific relevance

- Vagal activation → cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway → reduced TNF-α from macrophages
- Gut motility normalization (reduces both delayed gastric emptying in some IBD patients and rapid colonic transit in others)
- Reduces visceral hypersensitivity — relevant for IBD–IBS overlap, ~30–50% of IBD patients
- Integrates with gut-directed hypnotherapy, progressive muscle relaxation, and mindfulness-based interventions evidenced in IBD (UCLA and others — §integrative gastroenterology)

### 11.4 Practical prescription

- **Baseline:** 5 min diaphragmatic breathing 2x/day (morning, pre-bed)
- **Acute urgency management:** 4-7-8 for 4 cycles; often defers urge long enough to reach bathroom or complete transit
- **Pre-exercise:** 5 slow diaphragmatic breaths — improves HRV and may reduce sympathetic GI symptoms
- **Pre-sleep:** extended exhale, 10 min

## 12. Hydration and Electrolytes for Exercise with Crohn's

### 12.1 Who is at elevated risk

- **Post–ileal resection** (>100 cm lost, approximately) — risk of BAM and fluid/electrolyte malabsorption
- **Short bowel syndrome** (<200 cm remaining small bowel) — major losses; often on ORS
- **Active disease with diarrhea** — ongoing daily losses
- **Stoma patients** — high-output ileostomy (>1.5 L/day) especially vulnerable

### 12.2 The "drink more water" trap

Drinking plain hypotonic fluid (water, tea, diet soda) in SBS / high-output ostomy settings **worsens losses** because the gut secretes sodium into the lumen to match osmotic gradient, pulling water with it.

Reference: Parrish C, UVA Ginutrition practitioner guide; St Mark's solution PDF (Imperial NHS); Nightingale, *Practical Gastroenterology*, SBS hydration 2019 (PMC 5718176).

### 12.3 Optimal ORS characteristics

- **Sodium:** 90–120 mmol/L (higher than WHO standard ORS used for childhood diarrhea, which targets 75 mmol/L)
- **Glucose:** equimolar with sodium (~1:1) — activates SGLT1 co-transport for optimal absorption
- **Osmolality:** isotonic or slightly hypotonic (~240–290 mOsm/L)
- **St Mark's solution recipe (classic):**
  - 1 L water
  - 20 g glucose (or 40 g sucrose)
  - 3.5 g NaCl (sodium chloride)
  - 2.5 g NaHCO₃ (sodium bicarbonate)
  - Total Na+ ~90 mmol/L, Cl– ~60 mmol/L, HCO₃– ~30 mmol/L, glucose ~110 mmol/L

### 12.4 Commercial options

- **WHO-ORS sachets** (lower sodium than ideal for SBS but available)
- **DripDrop, Liquid I.V.** — consumer ORS; sodium ~500–1000 mg/L range
- **Pedialyte** — closer to WHO-ORS; sodium ~1035 mg/L (~45 mmol/L) — borderline for SBS
- **LMNT** — 1000 mg sodium per stick; used by many athletes; adequate sodium for heavy-sweat or moderate malabsorption contexts
- **Plain coconut water** — insufficient sodium (~250 mg/L); high potassium; not appropriate as sole ORS for SBS

### 12.5 Electrolyte targets for IBD exercisers

| Electrolyte | Context | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **Sodium** | Active exercise, post-resection | 1000–2000 mg/h during prolonged exercise; total daily need may be 4–6 g |
| **Potassium** | Long sessions, stoma output | 300–600 mg/h during prolonged exercise; dietary emphasis |
| **Magnesium** | Common deficiency in IBD | 300–400 mg/day; glycinate/citrate well-tolerated; oxide causes diarrhea |
| **Calcium** | Bone health | 1000–1200 mg/day; not exercise-acute |
| **Zinc** | Wound healing, immunity | 8–11 mg/day; higher if high-output stoma |

### 12.6 Sean-specific hydration note

Sean, as a long-post-resection patient (~20 years), may have residual subclinical bile-acid malabsorption (§6.1, §17-adjacent). For him:
- Pre-, intra-, and post-exercise sodium emphasis is reasonable even without classic SBS
- Monitor morning body weight trends during training blocks — acute loss >1 kg often = fluid debt
- Urine color: pale straw as baseline target
- Daily sodium intake probably ~3–5 g minimum during training phases

## 13. Exercise with an Ostomy

### 13.1 Parastomal hernia — the key risk

- **Incidence:** Cumulative risk ~50% by 5 years post-stoma. By stoma type: urostomy ~27%, colostomy ~40%, ileostomy ~24% (range by study).
- **Inactivity association:** Statistically significant inverse relationship — median 579 MET-min/week with hernia vs 1689 MET-min/week without (North et al., *WOC Nurs*, PMID 35714337).

### 13.2 Prevention programming

**First 3 months post-stoma:**
- No lifting >4.5–6.8 kg (10–15 lb)
- No direct abdominal resistance (crunches, planks, sit-ups)
- Walking encouraged progressively

**Months 3–6:**
- Graded core retraining — diaphragmatic breathing → transverse abdominis activation → dead bug → bird dog → anti-rotation (Pallof)
- Avoid Valsalva
- Introduce light resistance training with stoma support belt

**Month 6+:**
- Full resistance training possible, with continued attention to technique
- Running, swimming, cycling all compatible
- Avoid pure crunch/sit-up patterns lifelong — anti-extension/rotation core preferable anyway

### 13.3 Support belts

- **Purpose:** Provide gentle circumferential compression around the stoma, reducing outward bulging during intra-abdominal pressure spikes
- **Evidence [C]:** Observational data suggest ~78% reduction in parastomal hernia risk with consistent belt use during activity; RCT evidence limited
- **Products:** Coloplast Brava, Stealthbelt, Salts Comfizz, nu-Hope. Sizing/fit matters — should sit level with stoma without pinching
- **When to use:** All resistance training, running, lifting anything >10 kg outside gym, contact sports

### 13.4 Swimming

- Ostomy pouches are water-resistant when seal is secure
- Empty pouch before entering water
- Apply wafer ≥1 h before swim for maximum adhesion
- Water-resistant tape strips around flange edge reduce lift/leak
- Mini/stealth pouches available for more discretion

### 13.5 Contact sports

- Protective guards: Stealthbelt X, Ostomy Armor, custom plastic shields
- Avoid in first 6 months post-stoma
- Rugby, American football, martial arts, wrestling — discuss explicitly with surgeon/stoma nurse
- Non-contact alternatives (tennis, golf, track) carry minimal stoma-specific risk

### 13.6 Running specifically

- No contraindication once fully healed (~3 months)
- Long runs: stoma output management (small, frequent ORS sips); pouch emptying every 30–60 min on longer efforts
- Public examples: Adele Roberts (first woman with stoma to run all World Marathon Majors)

## 14. Exercise and Medications

### 14.1 Biologics (anti-TNF, anti-integrin, anti-IL-12/23)

- No significant exercise contraindications
- No dose-timing restrictions around workouts
- Injection-site considerations only: avoid heavy bag/resistance work on injection arm in first 24 h if soreness
- Biologics improve disease activity → typically enable higher exercise tolerance

### 14.2 Corticosteroids (prednisone, budesonide)

- **Corticosteroid-induced myopathy** (StatPearls NBK557731): proximal pelvic girdle weakness, type 2b fiber atrophy; dose-dependent (>10 mg prednisone/day for >4 weeks elevates risk); typically painless; normal CK
- **Resistance training prevents and partially reverses this** (Horber et al., *J Clin Invest*, 1985; PMID 3998075 — prednisone-induced myopathy reversed by physical training) **[A]** for the phenomenon; **[B]** for intervention
- **Practical implications during steroid courses:**
  - Increase resistance training frequency (3x/week)
  - Emphasize lower body compound movements (hip/knee extension loading)
  - Additional protein (up to 1.6–2.0 g/kg) to offset catabolism
  - Vitamin D 2000–4000 IU/day (often co-prescribed)
  - Calcium 1000–1200 mg/day
  - Tendon/ligament tensile strength may be reduced during steroid courses — progress loads conservatively; avoid max efforts
- **Avascular necrosis (AVN)** risk, particularly femoral head — hip pain during/after steroid course warrants imaging before progressive loading

### 14.3 Azathioprine / 6-mercaptopurine / methotrexate

- Monitor for fatigue, cytopenias (CBC q3mo)
- Methotrexate: avoid alcohol around training days; consider day-after-dose light sessions if fatigue pattern
- No direct exercise contraindications at therapeutic doses

### 14.4 JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib, filgotinib)

- **FDA boxed warning (2021)** for JAK inhibitors: increased MACE, malignancy, VTE, mortality vs TNFi — based on **ORAL Surveillance** (Ytterberg et al., *NEJM*, 2022; PMID 34965625) in RA patients ≥50 with ≥1 CV risk factor
- **VTE signal is dose-dependent:** HR ~3.52 at tofacitinib 10 mg BID (ORAL Surveillance); milder at 5 mg BID. IBD indication uses 10 mg BID induction, 5–10 mg BID maintenance.
- **Exercise implications:**
  - No direct exercise restriction in current guidelines
  - **Long-haul travel + JAK + sedentary** = elevated VTE risk context; patients on JAK inhibitors should hydrate, move every 60–90 min during travel
  - **Maintain regular aerobic exercise** as part of baseline VTE risk mitigation
  - **Avoid prolonged immobilization** (post-procedure recovery periods — consult prescriber)
  - Discuss with GI if planning high-altitude or extreme-endurance events
- Risk factors to flag: prior VTE, BMI ≥35, age ≥65, chronic lung disease, known thrombophilia

### 14.5 Antibiotics (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin for perianal disease)

- **Fluoroquinolones** (cipro, levofloxacin): tendinopathy and tendon rupture risk — particularly Achilles. **Avoid high-strain tendon loading, plyometrics, heavy jumping, maximal sprints during and for 2–4 weeks after course.**
- Metronidazole: GI disturbance, neuropathy with long courses; no direct exercise effect

### 14.6 Aminosalicylates (mesalamine)

- Minimal exercise interaction
- Rare nephrotoxicity warrants hydration vigilance

## 15. Fatigue-Specific Exercise Prescription

### 15.1 Why exercise reduces fatigue (paradoxically)

- **Central mechanism:** improved cerebral BDNF, dopaminergic/serotonergic function
- **Mitochondrial biogenesis:** endurance training increases mitochondrial density, partly reversing central fatigue
- **Reduced systemic inflammation:** see §2.1
- **Improved sleep architecture:** deeper slow-wave sleep with regular exercise
- **Reduced deconditioning:** the "energy envelope" expands with training

### 15.2 Graded exercise therapy (GET) — cautions

**Important distinction:** GET in traditional CFS/ME literature has been controversial (NICE 2021 guidance de-emphasized GET for ME/CFS). For IBD fatigue without post-exertional malaise (PEM) pattern, graded progressive exercise is generally appropriate. When PEM pattern exists (symptom worsening 24–72 h after exertion, disproportionate to intensity), **pacing** is preferred over GET.

### 15.3 Pacing strategy

- **Activity budget concept:** Estimate daily "energy envelope" in units (1 unit = 5–15 min of moderate activity).
- **Use 70–80%** of the envelope consistently, preserving margin for setbacks.
- **HR-based pacing:** Stay below ~60% HRmax or anaerobic threshold during bad days.
- **Interval-of-rest approach:** Microbreaks (30–90 sec sit or standing rest) every few minutes during light activities.
- **Avoid the "push–crash" cycle** — big activity day → multi-day bed rest → deconditioning → worse fatigue.

### 15.4 Symptom-titrated exercise (evidence-supported variant)

- Martin et al., *Sci Rep*, 2024 — symptom-titrated exercise RCT in post-COVID showed fatigue/QoL improvement
- **Principle:** Progress volume only when previous week tolerated without symptom worsening. If worsened, drop back 1–2 steps and re-advance.
- Weekly progression example: +5 min walking time OR +1 rep per set, not both. Monitor 2-week rolling average rather than single bad/good day.

### 15.5 Starting prescription for deconditioned fatigued IBD patient

**Week 1–2:** Walking 5–10 min 3–5x/day (light conversational pace). Diaphragmatic breathing 2x 5 min. No resistance.
**Week 3–4:** Combine into 15–20 min walks 3x/day. Add resistance — bodyweight squats 2 × 8, wall push-ups 2 × 8, bird dog 2 × 5/side, 2x/week.
**Week 5–8:** 30 min walking 4–5 days/week. Resistance 2x/week with 6 exercises, 2 × 10.
**Week 9–12:** Add light intervals (3 × 1 min brisk / 1 min easy) 1x/week. Progress resistance volume.
**Beyond:** Full ACSM-style prescription (§3.1) if tolerated.

## 16. Practical Programming for Sean's Situation

### 16.1 Baseline assumptions

- Long-post-resection (~20 yr) — well past acute surgical restrictions
- Likely subclinical bile-acid malabsorption consideration (§12, §6)
- Unknown current fitness baseline — programming here errs on the side of graded entry
- Age likely enters the "maintain/prevent sarcopenia" zone regardless of Crohn's

### 16.2 Maintenance program (remission, moderate fitness)

**Weekly template:**
- **Monday:** Full-body resistance, 45 min. 5 compound + 1 core. 3 sets of 8–10, RPE 7.
- **Tuesday:** 30–40 min zone 2 cardio (walking, cycling, swim). Breathing 10 min.
- **Wednesday:** Full-body resistance or mobility/yoga (gentle hatha).
- **Thursday:** Interval session — 5 × 3 min at RPE 7–8 with 2 min recovery. Or hill walk.
- **Friday:** Rest or gentle walk / yoga.
- **Saturday:** Longer cardio session 45–75 min low–moderate; include some impact (jogging, jump rope, hiking with varied terrain) for BMD.
- **Sunday:** Mobility, tai chi, or complete rest.

### 16.3 Deconditioning recovery (post-flare or long sedentary stretch)

**Weeks 1–4:** §15.5 protocol above.
**Weeks 5–8:** Consolidate walking base (30–45 min most days). Resistance 2x/week, 2 sets. Add 1 yoga/mobility.
**Weeks 9–16:** Layer in zone 2 cycling/swimming. Resistance 3x/week, progress to 3 sets. Add impact (jump rope doubles, 5 min). First running re-introduction 1 min run / 2 min walk intervals.

### 16.4 Flare-appropriate movement

During a mild flare:
- Walking 10–20 min 2–3x/day, conversational pace
- Restorative yoga 15–20 min (supported child's pose, legs up wall, supine twist, viparita karani)
- Breathing practice 10–15 min (4-7-8 or box breathing)
- Very light bodyweight movement: 1 set × 5 squats, 1 set × 5 wall push-ups — preserve motor pattern

Halt and consult GI if:
- New severe abdominal pain
- Fever
- Bloody diarrhea new or escalating
- Vomiting
- Persistent worsening fatigue despite rest

### 16.5 Long-term focus areas specific to Sean

1. **Bone density maintenance** — resistance + impact 2–3x/week
2. **Sarcopenia prevention** — protein 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day, resistance 2–3x/week
3. **Cardiovascular health** — zone 2 majority, some zone 4 intervals
4. **Autonomic regulation** — daily breathing practice, weekly yoga or tai chi
5. **Injury prevention with aging joint/tendon tissue** — consistent mobility, moderate plyometric load
6. **Sustainability over maximum intensity** — the program that gets done for decades beats the program that gets done hard for 8 weeks

## 17. When to NOT Exercise

Absolute or near-absolute contraindications:

| Condition | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Active or suspected bowel obstruction** | Exertion + Valsalva may worsen distension; abdominal pain + vomiting + no passage of stool/gas = ED visit, not the gym |
| **Severe anemia (Hb <8–9 g/dL)** | Inadequate oxygen delivery; risk of cardiac ischemia at exertion; treat first |
| **Acute intra-abdominal abscess** | Mechanical compression and thermogenesis may propagate infection |
| **Major surgery <4–6 weeks** | Wound dehiscence, hernia, adhesive complications |
| **Uncontrolled cardiovascular disease** | Unstable angina, uncontrolled arrhythmia, decompensated heart failure, severe aortic stenosis |
| **Acute toxic megacolon** | Life-threatening; ICU-level care, not exercise |
| **Severe dehydration / electrolyte disturbance** | Correct first; cardiac arrhythmia risk |
| **Active GI bleeding** | Stabilize, transfuse, investigate — not exercise |
| **Uncontrolled DVT** (on pre-therapeutic anticoagulation) | Embolization risk |
| **Active perianal abscess requiring drainage** | Pressure-based activity (cycling, rowing, squatting deep) may worsen; light walking ok |
| **Severe pelvic or abdominal pain of unclear etiology** | Investigate before exercising through it |

Relative cautions (dial intensity down, discuss with GI):
- CRP markedly elevated, active inflammation per labs, regardless of symptoms
- Known high-grade stricture (even asymptomatic) + vigorous training → stricture may become obstructive with transit alteration
- Fluoroquinolone course (§14.5)
- Prednisone >20 mg/day for >4 weeks (load management, tendon caution)
- Severe fatigue with PEM pattern (pacing, not GET)

## 19. Gaps I Couldn't Verify

The following topics have **limited high-quality IBD-specific evidence** or where my sources were indirect/adjacent. These need explicit caveat when used in answers:

1. **Tai chi / qigong RCTs in IBD populations specifically.** All evidence cited is from ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, or generic chronic illness. No dedicated IBD RCT identified in the search.
2. **Effect of HIIT on disease activity in moderately active Crohn's.** Tew 2019 excluded active disease. Extrapolation to active disease is speculative.
3. **Optimal protein intake specifically for active resistance-training Crohn's patients** (vs. non-exercising IBD patients). The 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day figure is derived from ESPEN IBD + ISSN athlete recommendations; no head-to-head trial in IBD exercisers.
4. **Long-term safety of heavy lifting post-resection at 20+ year interval.** Surgical literature focuses on first 12–24 months. The lifelong incisional hernia residual risk is real but incremental absolute risk from heavy lifting in remote post-op patients is not well quantified.
5. **JAK inhibitor–specific VTE risk modulation by exercise status.** ORAL Surveillance reported the risk signal but did not stratify by physical activity level. Assumption that exercise lowers risk is based on general VTE epidemiology, not trial data.
6. **Whether prophylactic loperamide before exercise is safe long-term in Crohn's.** Widely used empirically among IBD runners; I found no RCT of prophylactic loperamide in IBD exercisers.
7. **Effect of specific yoga styles on disease activity** — Cramer used gentle hatha. Whether vinyasa, Iyengar, or Ashtanga produce different disease-activity outcomes is untested.
8. **Pelvic floor rehabilitation in post-proctectomy / pouch patients specifically.** Most IBD PFMT evidence is in patients with intact anatomy. Pouch patients likely benefit but are under-represented in trials.
9. **Exercise-induced microbiome changes in IBD specifically.** Moderate exercise increases SCFA-producing taxa in general populations; IBD-specific human data are sparse and observational.
10. **Detailed VO₂ / METs targets for post-surgical return in Crohn's.** Extrapolated from general post-abdominal-surgery rehab; no Crohn's-specific staged cardiovascular return-to-activity guidelines identified.
11. **Specific sodium dosing for post-resection exercisers (non-SBS).** St Mark's solution designed for SBS; lighter post-resection without SBS may need less but empirical guidance is by trial-and-error clinically.
12. **Safety of inversions (headstand, shoulder stand) in IBD** with reflux, hiatal hernia, or hemorrhoids — mostly based on yoga-tradition caution; no IBD-specific studies.
