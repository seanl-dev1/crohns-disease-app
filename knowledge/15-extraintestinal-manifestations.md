# 15 — Extraintestinal Manifestations (EIMs) of Crohn's Disease

Reference file for Claude. Technical language. Evidence graded where available. Citations are to published literature; speculative or low-confidence claims are flagged [UNVERIFIED] or [LOW EVIDENCE].

Key sources referenced throughout:

## 1. Overview and Framework

### Prevalence
- **Cumulative lifetime prevalence**: Up to **50% of IBD patients** develop at least one EIM over their lifetime (ECCO 2024).
- **Cross-sectional/pooled prevalence**: Meta-analysis (Rogler 2024) pooled **24% of IBD patients overall**; 27% UC; **35% Crohn's disease** have joint, ocular, or skin EIMs.
- **Point prevalence at diagnosis**: 6-25% (varies by study).
- **Multiple EIMs**: ~25% of IBD patients with one EIM develop another.
- Ocular EIMs and peripheral joint/erythema nodosum are more common in CD than UC. Pyoderma gangrenosum and axial arthritis have similar prevalence in CD and UC.

### ECCO 2024 Classification Framework
ECCO reorganized EIMs into four categories:
1. **Classical EIMs** — immune-mediated inflammation at extra-intestinal sites (peripheral arthritis, erythema nodosum, uveitis, episcleritis, aphthous stomatitis)
2. **Associations** — linked immune-mediated disorders that may predate or postdate IBD (axial spondyloarthritis/AS, PSC, psoriasis, autoimmune thyroid disease)
3. **Complications** — downstream effects of chronic inflammation, malabsorption, or systemic disease (anemia, osteoporosis, nephrolithiasis, VTE, amyloidosis)
4. **Treatment-related** — adverse effects of IBD therapy (paradoxical psoriasis, drug-induced hepatitis, JAK-associated VTE, etc.)

### Legacy "Reactive vs. Independent" Framework
- **Reactive / gut-parallel**: Peripheral arthritis Type 1, erythema nodosum, episcleritis, aphthous ulcers, metastatic CD — activity tracks with bowel inflammation; treat the gut.
- **Independent / gut-discordant**: Peripheral arthritis Type 2, axial SpA/AS, uveitis, PSC, pyoderma gangrenosum — often run independently of luminal activity and require their own directed therapy.

### Pearl
> If the EIM parallels the gut, treating the gut usually treats the EIM. If the EIM is independent, you likely need a biologic with dual indication (anti-TNF) or a separate therapy in co-management.

## 2. Musculoskeletal EIMs (Most Common Category)

Joint involvement is the single most common EIM category, affecting **17-39%** of IBD patients depending on definition (ECCO 2024).

### 2.1 Peripheral Arthritis — Orchard Classification (1998)
The Orchard TR et al. classification (*Gastroenterology* 1998;114(3):478-485) subdivides IBD-associated peripheral arthritis:

**Type 1 — Pauciarticular**
- **Joints**: ≤5 joints; large, asymmetric; lower extremities (knees, ankles, hips)
- **Course**: Acute, self-limited (<10 weeks typically)
- **Gut correlation**: **Parallels** bowel activity; often precedes/heralds flare
- **Prevalence**: ~4-17% of IBD
- **HLA**: Associated with HLA-B27, HLA-B35, HLA-DR103
- **Treatment**: Treat the gut flare. Acute NSAID use is controversial (see below). Intra-articular steroids, short-course systemic steroids, anti-TNF if severe.

**Type 2 — Polyarticular**
- **Joints**: >5 joints; small joints; symmetric; upper extremities (MCPs especially)
- **Course**: Chronic, months to years
- **Gut correlation**: **Independent** of bowel disease activity
- **Prevalence**: ~2-4% of IBD
- **HLA**: Associated with HLA-B44
- **Treatment**: Often requires dedicated therapy — anti-TNF (adalimumab, infliximab have strongest evidence), upadacitinib, sometimes sulfasalazine.

**Type 3 (Smale classification)** — combined axial + peripheral features.

### Key pearl: IBD peripheral arthritis is typically **non-erosive and non-deforming** (contrast to RA). Absence of bony erosion on imaging does not rule it out.

### 2.2 Axial Spondyloarthritis (axSpA) / Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS)
- **Prevalence**: Sacroiliitis on imaging in **20-50%** of IBD patients (often asymptomatic); clinically symptomatic AS in **3-10%** of CD.
- **HLA-B27**: Present in ~50-70% of IBD-associated AS (lower than primary AS at 74-89%); HLA-B27 status in IBD does not reliably predict axial disease.
- **IBD strongly predicts positive MRI-sacroiliitis**: In one cohort, IBD raised pre-test probability of MRI sacroiliitis from 41% to 81%.
- **Course**: Runs **independently** of bowel disease; can progress even when gut is quiescent.
- **Symptoms**: Inflammatory back pain — >3 months, onset <45 years, morning stiffness >30 min, improves with activity, worsens with rest, nocturnal awakening.
- **Diagnosis**: MRI sacroiliac joints (bone marrow edema, erosions) is gold standard for early disease; X-ray sacroiliitis is later finding.
- **Treatment**:
  - Physiotherapy is foundational.
  - **Anti-TNF** (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab, golimumab) — strong evidence for both gut and axial disease.
  - **Etanercept is NOT effective** for luminal IBD despite being effective for AS — avoid in IBD-AS.
  - **IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab)** — effective for AS but may precipitate or worsen IBD; **generally avoided** in IBD-AS.
  - **IL-23 inhibitors (risankizumab, ustekinumab)**: emerging evidence in IBD; ustekinumab has modest effect on axial symptoms [LOW EVIDENCE for axial].
  - **JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, tofacitinib)**: dual efficacy, approved for both AS and UC (upadacitinib also for CD).

### 2.3 Enthesitis and Dactylitis
- **Enthesitis**: Inflammation at tendon/ligament insertion; Achilles, plantar fascia most common. Prevalence 7-10% in IBD.
- **Dactylitis** ("sausage digit"): Diffuse swelling of entire finger/toe; ~2-5% prevalence.
- Treated similarly to axSpA (anti-TNF, JAK inhibitors).

### 2.4 NSAIDs and IBD
- **ECCO 2024**: Avoid non-selective NSAIDs when possible in IBD due to risk of flare induction/exacerbation.
- **Mechanism**: COX-1 inhibition → disruption of mucosal prostaglandins.
- **Alternatives**: Acetaminophen (paracetamol) first line for analgesia. **COX-2 selective** inhibitors (celecoxib, etoricoxib) appear safer in short courses per small RCT data, but still avoid prolonged use.
- Topical NSAIDs are generally safe.

### 2.5 Metabolic Bone Disease (Osteopenia/Osteoporosis)
- **Prevalence**: Osteoporosis **13-42%** in IBD; osteopenia 30-50%.
- CD > UC risk, likely due to terminal ileal disease, malabsorption, higher steroid exposure.
- **Risk factors**: Cumulative corticosteroid exposure (>3 months of prednisone ≥7.5 mg/day), low BMI, postmenopausal, hypogonadism, smoking, vitamin D deficiency, calcium malabsorption, disease duration, bowel resection.
- **Screening (ACG 2017 / CCF guideline)**: DEXA at IBD diagnosis in any patient with risk factors; repeat every **2-3 years** depending on baseline and risk. More frequent if on chronic steroids.
- **Treatment**: Calcium (1000-1200 mg/day), vitamin D (800-2000 IU/day to 25-OH-D >30 ng/mL), weight-bearing exercise, bisphosphonates if T-score ≤-2.5 or prior fragility fracture. Avoid chronic steroids; use budesonide over prednisone when possible.

## 3. Cutaneous EIMs

Skin is involved in **~22-44% of CD patients** (broadly defined, including perianal extension).

### 3.1 Erythema Nodosum (EN)
- **Prevalence**: **4-15%** in CD (higher than UC); F:M ~3-4:1.
- **Appearance**: Tender, warm, 1-5 cm erythematous-to-violaceous nodules; **anterior shins most common**, also forearms, thighs.
- **Course**: **Parallels gut activity**; self-resolving over 2-4 weeks leaving "bruise-like" discoloration (no ulceration or scarring).
- **Workup**: Clinical diagnosis; biopsy only if atypical (shows septal panniculitis without vasculitis).
- **Treatment**: Treat the underlying IBD flare (steroids, 5-ASA, biologics). Compression, leg elevation, NSAIDs (cautiously). Anti-TNF (adalimumab, infliximab) reported effective in refractory cases.

### 3.2 Pyoderma Gangrenosum (PG)
- **Prevalence**: **1-2% in IBD** (up to 5% in some cohorts); more common in UC than CD.
- **Appearance**: Starts as pustule/papule, rapidly progresses to painful ulcer with **violaceous undermined border**; most often on shins, peristomal sites.
- **Pathergy**: Lesions worsen with trauma/debridement — **avoid surgical debridement**.
- **Course**: Often **independent** of gut activity; can occur during remission.
- **Diagnosis**: Clinical + biopsy of edge (neutrophilic dermatosis; no specific finding); exclude infection, vasculitis, malignancy.
- **Treatment**:
  - **Systemic corticosteroids** (prednisone 0.5-1 mg/kg/day) — first-line for most patients.
  - **Cyclosporine** — alternative first-line.
  - **Infliximab** — supported by a **randomized controlled trial** (Brooklyn TN et al. *Gut* 2006;55(4):505-509): 46% improvement at 2 weeks with infliximab 5 mg/kg vs. 6% placebo; 69% improved at 6 weeks (21% complete resolution). **ECCO and most algorithms list infliximab as first-line for IBD-associated PG**.
  - Adalimumab, ustekinumab, and more recently JAK inhibitors have case-series support.
  - Topical tacrolimus or intralesional steroids for small lesions.

### 3.3 Sweet Syndrome (Acute Febrile Neutrophilic Dermatosis)
- Rare in IBD.
- Tender, erythematous, edematous plaques; fever; neutrophilia; often on face, neck, upper extremities.
- Treat with systemic steroids; anti-TNF reported effective. [LOW EVIDENCE — case reports only]

### 3.4 Cutaneous (Metastatic) Crohn's Disease
- **Definition**: Non-caseating granulomas in skin **non-contiguous** with GI tract — a true EIM rather than fistula/fissure extension.
- **Presentation**: Plaques, nodules, ulcers, lichenoid lesions, "knife-cut" linear ulcers in skin folds (submammary, inguinal, perineal), or limb lesions.
- **Diagnosis**: Biopsy — non-caseating granulomas, Langhans giant cells, dermal/subcutaneous involvement.
- **Treatment**: Topical/intralesional steroids for limited disease; systemic steroids; immunomodulators; anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab); upadacitinib reported effective in recent case series.

### 3.5 Psoriasis
- **Two distinct entities**:
  1. **Pre-existing / concurrent psoriasis**: **6-10%** prevalence in IBD (vs. 2% general population). Shared genetic susceptibility (IL23R, IL12B loci).
  2. **Paradoxical psoriasis** induced by anti-TNF therapy: **Meta-analysis pooled incidence ~19% of IBD patients on anti-TNF** (some studies 1-30%). Distinct pathology: type I interferon-driven; plasmacytoid DC infiltrate.
- **Paradoxical psoriasis management**:
  - Mild/moderate: topical steroids, vitamin D analogs — continue anti-TNF if gut response good.
  - Severe/disabling: switch to **ustekinumab** (anti-IL-12/23; covers both gut and skin), **risankizumab**, or consider JAK inhibitor.
  - Avoid switching to another anti-TNF (recurrence risk >50%).

### 3.6 Aphthous Stomatitis (Mouth Ulcers)
- **Prevalence**: 10-30% of CD patients.
- Typically parallels gut activity; minor aphthae resolve within 2 weeks.
- Treat with topical steroids (triamcinolone in Orabase, dexamethasone mouthwash); address iron/B12/folate deficiency.

### 3.7 Perianal Disease (Tags, Fissures, Fistulas)
**Not strictly EIM** — classified as luminal disease extension in Montreal/ECCO. Covered in fistulizing/perianal disease files; flagged here for completeness.

## 4. Ocular EIMs

Overall ocular EIMs: **4-12%** of IBD; CD > UC. Annual eye exam recommended by ECCO 2024, more frequent if symptoms or history.

### 4.1 Episcleritis
- **Most common ocular EIM**.
- **Appearance**: Painless or mildly uncomfortable red eye; no vision change; sectoral or diffuse.
- **Course**: **Parallels gut activity**.
- **Treatment**: Cool compresses, topical NSAIDs or lubricants; often self-limiting; treat gut.
- Can be distinguished from scleritis by phenylephrine test (blanches the episcleral vessels).

### 4.2 Scleritis
- **Less common but more serious**; can threaten vision.
- **Appearance**: Deep, boring ocular pain (worse at night), violaceous hue, tenderness to palpation, photophobia.
- **Urgency**: Same-day ophthalmology referral.
- **Treatment**: Systemic NSAIDs (cautiously, or COX-2), systemic steroids, immunomodulators, anti-TNF.

### 4.3 Uveitis (Iritis / Anterior Uveitis)
- **Prevalence**: **2-5%** of IBD; higher in those with axSpA.
- **HLA-B27**: Associated in ~50% of IBD uveitis (HLA-B27 is found in ~50% of all acute anterior uveitis generally).
- **Appearance**: **Painful red eye, photophobia, blurred vision**, ciliary flush, miotic pupil; cells and flare in anterior chamber on slit lamp.
- **IBD uveitis pearl**: More often **bilateral and chronic/subacute** (~50% of CD uveitis is bilateral or chronic) vs. classic HLA-B27 AAU which is unilateral/acute.
- **Urgency**: **Same-day ophthalmology referral — vision-threatening emergency**.
- **Treatment**:
  - Topical cycloplegic (atropine 1%) + high-potency topical corticosteroid (prednisolone acetate 1%, q1-2h taper).
  - Systemic steroids if severe or posterior involvement.
  - Refractory: anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab — adalimumab is FDA-approved for non-infectious uveitis), methotrexate, sulfasalazine (reduces recurrence rate).
  - Avoid etanercept (less effective for uveitis).

### 4.4 Dry Eye / Sicca Syndrome
- Increased in IBD; overlap with Sjögren-like features.
- Treat with preservative-free artificial tears, punctal plugs; consider topical cyclosporine ophthalmic emulsion.

### 4.5 Rare Ocular
- Retinal vasculitis, optic neuritis (red flag for demyelinating disease — see section 10), central retinal vein occlusion (consider CV risk).

### 4.6 Drug-Induced Ocular Disease
- **Corticosteroid-induced cataract and glaucoma** — risk with systemic or topical ocular steroids; dose and duration dependent.
- **Hydroxychloroquine retinopathy** — cumulative dose risk; screen annually after 5 years of use or earlier if renal impairment (per AAO guidelines).
- **Anti-TNF-associated optic neuritis / demyelinating syndromes** — rare but a contraindication to continued therapy.

### Monitoring: **Annual comprehensive eye exam** for all IBD patients (ECCO 2024).

## 5. Hepatobiliary EIMs

### 5.1 Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC)
- **Prevalence**: **~5% of CD, ~8% of UC** have PSC. Conversely, **60-80% of PSC patients have IBD** (48-86% UC, 13-25% CD).
- **PSC-IBD phenotype**: Often mild, quiescent, right-sided (pancolitis with rectal sparing), **backwash ileitis**.
- **Diagnosis**: **MRCP is first-line imaging** — multifocal strictures, beading of intra/extrahepatic ducts. ERCP if intervention needed.
- **Labs**: Elevated alkaline phosphatase > ALT/AST; may have normal labs. Anti-nuclear, anti-smooth muscle, p-ANCA may be positive.
- **Screening**: AASLD recommends checking ALP at IBD diagnosis and serially; MRCP if elevated ALP or cholestatic symptoms.
- **Natural history**: Progressive; median transplant-free survival ~20 years.
- **Complications**:
  - **Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA)**: Annual incidence **0.5-1.5%**; **lifetime risk ~10-20%**; **584-fold relative risk vs. general population** (meta-analysis). Screen with annual MRCP ± CA 19-9 per AASLD.
  - **Gallbladder cancer**: Increased; annual gallbladder ultrasound; prophylactic cholecystectomy for polyps ≥8 mm.
  - **Colorectal cancer in PSC-IBD**: **4× risk vs. UC alone**, **10× vs. general population**; 10-year CRC risk 14%, 20-year risk 31%. **Annual surveillance colonoscopy starting at PSC diagnosis** (not 8 years after, per multi-society guidelines).
- **Treatment**:
  - **No proven disease-modifying therapy**. High-dose UDCA (>28 mg/kg/day) is harmful (Lindor trial); moderate-dose UDCA (13-15 mg/kg/day) is controversial — AASLD does not recommend routine use but many centers still prescribe.
  - Vancomycin (oral) has some signal in pediatric PSC [LOW EVIDENCE].
  - Dominant strictures: ERCP balloon dilation.
  - **Liver transplant** for end-stage disease.

### 5.2 Cholelithiasis (Gallstones)
- **Prevalence**: **~2-3× baseline** in CD, especially ileal disease or post-ileal resection. Crude prevalence ~13-34% in ileal CD.
- **Mechanism**: Ileal bile acid malabsorption → bile acid pool depletion → cholesterol supersaturation (cholesterol stones). A second mechanism: malabsorbed bile acids in colon solubilize unconjugated bilirubin → reabsorption → pigment stones.
- **Management**: Standard indications for cholecystectomy; no prophylactic surgery.

### 5.3 Autoimmune Hepatitis (AIH) / Overlap Syndromes
- Rare but recognized association.
- PSC-AIH overlap ~8-10% of PSC.
- Diagnosis: transaminitis, elevated IgG, autoantibodies (ANA, ASMA, anti-LKM), biopsy (interface hepatitis).
- Treatment: steroids + azathioprine; budesonide alternative in non-cirrhotic.

### 5.4 Drug-Induced Liver Injury (DILI)
- **Thiopurines (azathioprine, 6-MP)**: Elevated LFTs in 3-10%; nodular regenerative hyperplasia (rare); sinusoidal obstruction syndrome. Check TPMT and/or NUDT15 pre-treatment; monitor LFTs q3 months.
- **Methotrexate**: Cumulative dose-related fibrosis; rare with modern monitoring. Check LFTs monthly initially, then q3 months. Folate supplementation mandatory.
- **Anti-TNF**: Rare DILI/autoimmune hepatitis-like picture with infliximab > others. Check LFTs periodically.
- **Sulfasalazine / 5-ASA**: Hypersensitivity hepatitis (rare, early).

### 5.5 NAFLD/MAFLD
- **Prevalence ~30-40%** in IBD cohorts; higher than expected given often-lower BMI. Likely driven by corticosteroids, methotrexate, chronic inflammation, altered microbiome.
- Screen with ALT/AST, ultrasound, FIB-4 score.

### Monitoring summary: LFTs at baseline and every 3-12 months; MRCP if unexplained ALP elevation; annual surveillance MRCP/ultrasound for PSC patients.

## 6. Renal and Urologic EIMs

### 6.1 Nephrolithiasis
- **Prevalence**: **9-18%** of IBD patients vs. ~5% general population.
- **Types**:
  - **Calcium oxalate stones** — most common; especially after **ileal resection** (enteric hyperoxaluria): fat malabsorption → unabsorbed fatty acids bind calcium in gut → free oxalate is absorbed → hyperoxaluria. Details covered in `post-resection-dietary-guide.md`.
  - **Uric acid stones** — dehydration, chronic diarrhea, metabolic acidosis.
- **Prevention**: Adequate hydration (>2 L/day), low-oxalate diet (avoid spinach, rhubarb, nuts, chocolate), calcium with meals (binds oxalate in gut), citrate supplementation (potassium citrate), reduce animal protein intake.

### 6.2 IgA Nephropathy
- Increased prevalence in IBD (shared mucosal immunity hypothesis) [LOW EVIDENCE - population studies with mixed findings].
- Monitor urinalysis periodically; refer nephrology if hematuria/proteinuria.

### 6.3 Tubulointerstitial Nephritis
- **5-ASA-associated**: Rare but recognized — idiosyncratic; check creatinine at baseline, at 3 months, then annually on mesalamine.
- **Granulomatous interstitial nephritis** — rare; may be EIM per se or drug-induced.

### 6.4 Amyloidosis (AA / Secondary Amyloidosis)
- **Rare** (<1% of IBD); associated with long-standing uncontrolled inflammation, typically >15-year duration.
- Affects kidneys (nephrotic syndrome), heart, GI tract.
- Diagnosis: biopsy (Congo red, apple-green birefringence).
- **Poor prognosis**; treat aggressive IBD control; anti-TNF may stabilize.

### 6.5 Bladder/Urinary Fistulas
- Enterovesical fistulas (pneumaturia, fecaluria) — complication of CD, not strictly EIM.

## 7. Hematologic EIMs

### 7.1 Anemia — The Most Common EIM (or "complication")
- **Prevalence**: **~24% of IBD patients** have anemia (pooled; higher in CD, in active disease, and in hospitalized patients — up to 68%).
- **Types**:

**Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA)** — most common
- **ECCO ferritin cutoffs**:
  - Mild disease / remission (no inflammation): **ferritin <30 µg/L** = iron deficiency.
  - Active inflammation (CRP elevated): **ferritin <100 µg/L + TSAT <20%** = iron deficiency likely (inflammation elevates ferritin as acute-phase reactant, masking deficiency).
  - Ferritin >100 + TSAT <20% = anemia of chronic disease (functional iron deficiency).
- **Oral iron**: Ferrous sulfate 65 mg elemental daily or every other day (every-other-day dosing improves fractional absorption). Can worsen GI symptoms.
- **IV iron**: Indicated for intolerance, severe anemia (Hb <10 g/dL), active flare, hepcidin blockade, Hb inadequate response to oral iron by 4 weeks. Options: ferric carboxymaltose (Ganz-Katz protocol), iron isomaltoside, ferumoxytol, iron sucrose.
- **Maintenance / reinitiation**: Restart IV iron if ferritin falls <100 µg/L or Hb <12 g/dL (ECCO).

**Vitamin B12 Deficiency**
- **Prevalence**: ~16% in CD overall.
- **Risk factors**:
  - **Terminal ileal disease or resection** — the classic high-risk group. Resections **>20 cm** of ileum: high risk; <20 cm: low risk; 20-60 cm: ~50% abnormal Schilling/B12 malabsorption.
  - Ileal bypass, small bowel bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), gastric acid suppression.
- **Testing**: Serum B12 (cutoff typically <200 pg/mL deficient; 200-350 borderline — confirm with MMA and homocysteine).
- **Treatment**: IM cyanocobalamin 1000 mcg weekly × 4, then monthly for life (post-resection). Oral high-dose (1000-2000 mcg daily) reasonable for absorption-intact.
- **Manifestations to watch**: macrocytic anemia, glossitis, peripheral neuropathy, subacute combined degeneration, cognitive changes. B12 neuropathy can be irreversible if untreated.

**Folate Deficiency**
- **Prevalence**: ~22% in CD.
- **Causes**: Methotrexate antagonism (mandatory folate supplementation 1-5 mg/day on MTX), sulfasalazine (competitive inhibitor), malabsorption (jejunal disease), poor intake.
- **Testing**: RBC folate more reliable than serum folate for chronic status.
- **Treatment**: Folic acid 1-5 mg/day.

### 7.2 Anemia of Chronic Disease (ACD / AI — Anemia of Inflammation)
- Hepcidin-mediated iron sequestration in macrophages; normocytic, low reticulocyte.
- Treat the underlying disease; IV iron often needed since oral iron is poorly absorbed under high hepcidin.

### 7.3 Thrombocytosis
- Reactive; correlates with disease activity; generally doesn't require direct treatment (treat IBD).

### 7.4 Venous Thromboembolism (VTE) — **CRITICAL to recognize**
- **Risk**: **2-3× baseline** in IBD, increasing to **~6× during flares** and **>10× during hospitalization**.
- Includes DVT, PE, mesenteric vein thrombosis, portal vein thrombosis, CVST.
- **Mechanism**: Chronic inflammation → platelet activation, elevated coagulation factors (VIII, fibrinogen), acquired protein C/S deficiency, endothelial dysfunction.
- **Prophylaxis** (ACG, ECCO, CAG):
  - **All hospitalized IBD patients** (even with rectal bleeding in most cases — evidence does not show increased significant bleeding with LMWH prophylaxis): prophylactic **LMWH (enoxaparin 40 mg daily or equivalent)** or fondaparinux preferred over unfractionated heparin.
  - **Do not withhold for rectal bleeding** unless bleeding is severe/hemodynamically significant — a common clinical mistake.
  - Outpatient flare: not routinely anticoagulated unless other risk factors; individualize.
  - Post-op IBD surgery: extended prophylaxis 4 weeks consideration (particularly abdominal/pelvic surgery).
- **Treatment**: Standard VTE management (DOACs acceptable, LMWH bridge, duration 3 months for provoked, longer if unprovoked/recurrent).

## 8. Cardiovascular EIMs

### 8.1 Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (MI, Stroke)
- **Disease-activity-dependent**: Danish nationwide cohort (Kristensen SL et al. *PLoS One* 2013):
  - During **flare**: RR MI 1.49 (1.16–1.93)
  - During **persistent activity**: RR MI 2.05 (1.58–2.65)
  - During **remission**: RR MI 1.01 (0.89–1.15) — **not elevated**.
- After first MI, IBD flare was associated with HR 3.09 for recurrent MI.
- **Message**: Inflammation = CV risk. Aggressive IBD control is CV prevention.
- **Clinical pearl**: Manage traditional CV risk factors aggressively in IBD — don't assume low BMI = low risk.

### 8.2 Venous Thromboembolism
Covered above under hematologic.

### 8.3 Pericarditis and Myocarditis
- Rare; can be IBD-related (classical EIM, often serositis) or drug-induced (mesalamine — usually within first month of therapy; discontinuation resolves).

### 8.4 Drug-Related Cardiovascular Signals
- **Anti-TNF**: NYHA Class III-IV heart failure is a **contraindication** to anti-TNF (black box warning, based on ATTACH trial with infliximab in heart failure patients). Rare new-onset heart failure reported.
- **JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib)**:
  - Boxed warning for MACE and VTE based on ORAL Surveillance trial (tofacitinib vs anti-TNF in RA >50yo with ≥1 CV risk factor).
  - **IBD data less concerning**: 2025 network meta-analysis of 26 IBD RCTs (10,537 patients) showed **no significant increase in MACE or VTE** overall. Dose-dependent trends: tofacitinib 5 mg BID trend toward MACE; upadacitinib 30 mg QD trend toward VTE/CVE.
  - Clinical approach: risk-stratify (age >65, prior CV/VTE, smoking); prefer JAK in lower-risk patients or when other options exhausted.

## 9. Pulmonary EIMs

Uncommon but underdiagnosed; estimated **<5%** clinically apparent.

### 9.1 Airway Disease
- Chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis, tracheobronchitis, subglottic stenosis — reported in IBD.
- Bronchiectasis post-colectomy in UC is a recognized entity [LOW-MODERATE EVIDENCE].

### 9.2 Parenchymal Disease
- Organizing pneumonia (COP/BOOP), interstitial lung disease, pulmonary granulomas (rare).

### 9.3 Drug-Induced Pulmonary Disease
- **Methotrexate pneumonitis**: Idiosyncratic, can occur any time on therapy; presents with dry cough, dyspnea, fever, hypoxia; HRCT — ground glass. Discontinue MTX immediately; steroids.
- **Anti-TNF and TB reactivation**: Screen with IGRA (QuantiFERON/T-SPOT) or PPD + CXR before starting anti-TNF. Treat latent TB (INH × 9 months or rifampin × 4 months) before or with anti-TNF initiation. Re-screen periodically in endemic areas.
- **Anti-TNF and other opportunistic infections**: histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis (geographic), Pneumocystis, Listeria.
- **Mesalamine**: rare eosinophilic pneumonitis.

## 10. Neurologic EIMs

### 10.1 Peripheral Neuropathy
- Causes:
  - **Vitamin deficiencies**: B12 (classic post-ileal-resection), B6, folate, copper (rare).
  - **Drug-induced**: metronidazole (dose/cumulative-dose; usually reversible but can be permanent after prolonged exposure), thalidomide (historically used for CD; now rare).
  - Primary IBD-associated neuropathies (axonal and demyelinating) reported.
- Management: replete deficiencies, discontinue offending drug, neurology referral for workup.

### 10.2 Demyelinating Disease (CNS)
- **Anti-TNF signal**: Anti-TNFs are associated with rare de novo multiple sclerosis or MS-like demyelination (optic neuritis, myelitis).
- **Absolute risk low**: <1 per 1000 patient-years; relative risk possibly ~2×.
- **Action**:
  - **Pre-existing MS / demyelinating disease is a contraindication to anti-TNF** (ECCO, FDA warning).
  - If demyelination develops on anti-TNF: **discontinue immediately; do not rechallenge**. Switch class (vedolizumab, ustekinumab, JAK inhibitor — preferred alternatives per ECCO 2026 demyelinating-practical-guide).
- Pathogenesis: Anti-TNF may unmask subclinical MS or promote autoreactive T-cell trafficking.

### 10.3 Cerebrovascular Disease
- Elevated stroke risk during flares (see section 8).
- Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis is a recognized IBD VTE manifestation — new severe headache or focal deficit in flare → imaging.

### 10.4 Headache / Migraine
- Increased migraine prevalence in IBD (~20-30% vs. ~15% population baseline) [LOW-MODERATE EVIDENCE].
- Consider mesalamine-related headache (common; usually dose-dependent and reversible).

### 10.5 Cognitive / "Brain Fog"
- Subjective cognitive dysfunction in 40-50% of active IBD; mechanistic basis: gut-brain axis, TNF-α, IL-6 effects on CNS, sleep disruption, depression, anemia.
- Reversible with disease control.

## 11. Psychiatric EIMs

### 11.1 Depression
- **Pooled prevalence**: **~25% (depression symptoms)**; **~15% (clinical depression)** in IBD (Barberio B et al. meta-analysis, *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2021).
- **Active disease**: Depression prevalence rises to ~35%; anxiety to ~50%.
- **Women > men**: depression 21% vs. 16%; anxiety 34% vs. 23%.

### 11.2 Anxiety
- **Pooled prevalence**: **~32% (symptoms)** in IBD.

### 11.3 Bidirectional Relationship
- Depression at IBD diagnosis → worse disease course.
- Active inflammation → neuroinflammation → mood symptoms.
- Depression increases risk of future IBD onset (meta-analyses show modest bidirectional association).

### 11.4 Clinical Pearls
- Screen annually with PHQ-9 and GAD-7 (ECCO 2024 recommendation).
- Consider integrated behavioral health, CBT, gut-directed hypnotherapy.
- Avoid long-term benzodiazepines; SSRIs/SNRIs first line for depression; TCAs useful for functional overlap symptoms.

*(See `mental-health.md` for deeper coverage.)*

## 12. Endocrine EIMs

### 12.1 Corticosteroid-Induced Disorders
- **Adrenal suppression**: After >2-3 weeks of supraphysiologic steroid (≥7.5 mg prednisone). Taper slowly; consider stress-dose steroid for illness/surgery for up to 1 year after discontinuation.
- **Steroid-induced diabetes**: ~20% of IBD patients on chronic steroids develop hyperglycemia/diabetes. Monitor fasting glucose, HbA1c.
- **Steroid-induced hypertension**: Mineralocorticoid effect; monitor BP.
- **Cushingoid features, weight gain, mood changes**: Common on prolonged prednisone; minimize with budesonide (high first-pass metabolism — less systemic effect) when possible.

### 12.2 Thyroid Disease
- Hashimoto thyroiditis and Graves disease increased in IBD (shared autoimmune susceptibility) [MODERATE EVIDENCE].
- Screen TSH periodically, especially if symptomatic (fatigue, weight change — overlap with IBD symptoms).

### 12.3 Hypogonadism / Sexual Dysfunction
- Common in chronic illness; amplified by corticosteroids (reduce gonadal steroid production), chronic disease, malnutrition, depression.
- Men: check testosterone if symptomatic. Women: menstrual irregularities correlate with disease activity.
- Contributes to bone loss (endocrine → musculoskeletal overlap).

### 12.4 Growth Failure (Pediatric) — See section 13.

---

## 13. Pediatric Growth and Development (Brief)
- **Growth failure** in **~30% of pediatric CD** at diagnosis; puberty delay in ~25%.
- Mechanisms: chronic inflammation (TNF, IL-6 suppress GH/IGF-1 axis), malnutrition, corticosteroids (direct growth plate effect).
- **Management**: Aggressive inflammation control; avoid prolonged corticosteroids; nutritional support (EEN — exclusive enteral nutrition — has evidence for induction + growth benefit); monitor height velocity, Tanner stage.

## 14. Oral Manifestations

### 14.1 Aphthous Ulcers
Covered in section 3.6.

### 14.2 Angular Cheilitis
- Cracking/fissures at mouth corners — often B vitamin (B2 riboflavin, B12, folate) or iron deficiency. Address deficiency.

### 14.3 Orofacial Granulomatosis (OFG)
- Persistent painless/painful swelling of lips, cheeks, gingiva; cobblestoning of buccal mucosa; mucosal tags.
- Histology: non-caseating granulomas (same as intestinal CD).
- **Relationship to CD**: Many cases of OFG represent oral CD; workup with colonoscopy + CRP + calprotectin. Some OFG is idiopathic/dietary (cinnamaldehyde, benzoates).
- **Treatment**: Topical/intralesional steroids, systemic steroids, metronidazole, anti-TNF, dietary elimination in select cases.

### 14.4 Periodontal Disease
- Higher prevalence and severity in IBD; shared inflammatory milieu; microbiome overlap. Encourage regular dental care.

## 15. Malignancy Risk (Cross-Reference)

### 15.1 Colorectal Cancer (CRC)
- **Risk**: Increased with extensive/long-standing colonic disease; baseline after 8-10 years of colitis; higher in PSC-IBD.
- **Surveillance (SCENIC + ACG/AGA)**:
  - Start 8 years after symptom onset for extensive colitis (UC or CD ≥30% colonic involvement or >1 colonic segment).
  - **PSC-IBD: annual surveillance from PSC diagnosis.**
  - Family history of CRC <50yo: start 10 years before relative's diagnosis age, or 8 years after IBD onset, whichever first.
  - **Interval**: 1-3 years based on risk stratification (AGA update allows up to 5 years for very low-risk normal prior exam).
  - **Technique**: High-definition white light ± chromoendoscopy; targeted biopsies of visible lesions; some centers still use random 4-quadrant biopsies q10 cm.

### 15.2 Small Bowel Adenocarcinoma (SBA)
- **Relative risk 10-30× in CD** (ileal/ileocolonic disease). Absolute rate ~1.15 per 1000 CD patients; highest in long-duration stricturing small bowel CD.
- **Routine surveillance not recommended** (low absolute risk, poor tools); high index of suspicion for obstruction/weight loss/atypical course → CT/MR enterography.

### 15.3 Lymphoproliferative Disease
- **Thiopurine + anti-TNF combination therapy**: modest increased risk of lymphoma (absolute risk ~6 per 10,000 patient-years).
- **Hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (HSTCL)**:
  - Very rare but devastating (5-yr survival <10% without allo-HSCT).
  - **Risk profile**: Young males (<35), Crohn's disease, long-term (≥2 years) thiopurine therapy (monotherapy or combination with anti-TNF).
  - **Absolute risk with thiopurines: ~1:45,000**.
  - Consideration for stopping thiopurines after 4-5 years in young men; alternatives: vedolizumab, ustekinumab, JAK inhibitor.

### 15.4 Skin Cancer
- **Non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC)**: **Increased with thiopurines** (relative risk ~2-6×); cumulative and duration-dependent; may persist after drug discontinuation.
- **Melanoma**: Modest increased risk associated with **anti-TNF** therapy (HR ~1.3-1.9 in some studies); inconsistent across studies.
- **Prevention**: Broad-spectrum sunscreen daily, sun avoidance, **annual full-body skin exam** — especially if on thiopurines or anti-TNF.

### 15.5 Cervical Cancer
- Modestly increased in IBD on immunosuppression; HPV vaccination recommended; annual Pap smears in women on immunosuppressants.

*(Deeper coverage in `complications.md` / malignancy-specific file.)*

## 16. Diagnostic Approach to EIMs

### When to suspect an EIM
- New musculoskeletal, dermatologic, ocular, hepatobiliary, or neurologic complaint in an IBD patient.
- Symptom pattern parallels (or starkly diverges from) gut activity.
- Unexplained lab abnormalities (ALP, LFTs, creatinine, CBC).

### Workup (general principles)
1. **Characterize the manifestation**: specific organ, severity, course (acute/chronic), correlation with gut activity.
2. **Targeted labs**: CBC, CMP, CRP, ESR, ferritin + TSAT, LFTs (with GGT/ALP), urinalysis, TSH, vitamin D, B12, folate, HLA-B27 if axial or uveitis.
3. **Imaging**: MRI SI joints if axial symptoms; MRCP if cholestasis; DEXA at baseline ± periodically.
4. **Specialist referral**: Low threshold — rheumatology, dermatology, ophthalmology, hepatology.

### Specialist Referrals
- **Rheumatology**: peripheral arthritis Type 2, suspected axSpA, refractory joint symptoms.
- **Dermatology**: pyoderma gangrenosum, suspected metastatic CD, paradoxical psoriasis.
- **Ophthalmology**: any red eye with pain/photophobia/vision change — same-day if uveitis or scleritis suspected.
- **Hepatology**: persistent ALP elevation, cirrhosis features, PSC suspicion.
- **Nephrology**: unexplained proteinuria, hematuria, or creatinine rise.
- **Neurology**: peripheral neuropathy, new demyelinating symptoms.
- **Mental health**: PHQ-9 ≥10 or GAD-7 ≥10.

## 17. Treatment Principles

### EIMs that typically respond to gut-targeted therapy
- Peripheral arthritis Type 1
- Erythema nodosum
- Episcleritis
- Aphthous ulcers
- Metastatic CD (mostly)

### EIMs that typically need their own/additional therapy
- Peripheral arthritis Type 2 (often independent course)
- Axial SpA / AS
- Uveitis / scleritis
- Pyoderma gangrenosum
- PSC (no effective disease-modifying therapy; manage complications)
- Paradoxical psoriasis (may need switch of biologic class)

### Biologics with Dual Efficacy
| Drug | Gut | Peripheral Arth | Axial SpA | Skin (Psoriasis) | PG | Uveitis |
|------|-----|-----------------|-----------|------------------|----|---------|
| Infliximab | ++ | ++ | ++ | ++ | ++ (RCT) | + |
| Adalimumab | ++ | ++ | ++ | ++ | + | ++ (FDA-approved) |
| Certolizumab | ++ | ++ | ++ | ± | + | ± |
| Golimumab | ++ (UC) | ++ | ++ | + | + | + |
| Etanercept | ✗ (ineffective IBD) | + | ++ | ++ | ± | – |
| Ustekinumab | ++ | + | ± | ++ | + | [LIMITED] |
| Risankizumab | ++ (CD/UC) | + | – | ++ | [LIMITED] | – |
| Vedolizumab | ++ | ± (parallels gut) | – (gut-selective) | ± (EN) | ± | – |
| Upadacitinib (JAK) | ++ (CD/UC) | ++ | ++ (AS approved) | + | [LIMITED] | ± |
| Tofacitinib (JAK) | ++ (UC) | ± | + | + | [LIMITED] | [LIMITED] |

Key: ++ strong evidence/approval; + supportive evidence; ± variable/case reports; – no/negative evidence; ✗ contraindicated/ineffective.

### When to Co-Manage
- **Any structural damage or function-limiting EIM**: co-manage with specialist.
- **Severe or vision-threatening ocular EIM**: co-manage with ophthalmology.
- **PSC**: hepatology shared care, often at transplant-capable center.
- **Refractory or atypical skin EIM**: dermatology for biopsy and diagnosis.

## 18. "When to Worry" — Quick Reference

| Scenario | Why it matters | Action |
|----------|----------------|--------|
| Painful red eye + photophobia + blurred vision | Uveitis or scleritis — vision-threatening | **Same-day ophthalmology** |
| Deep ocular pain worse at night + violaceous sclera | Scleritis | **Same-day ophthalmology** |
| Calf swelling/pain, unexplained dyspnea in flare/hospitalized | DVT/PE — 2-3× IBD risk | Duplex US, CT-PA; LMWH |
| New severe headache in flare | Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis | MRV |
| Chest pain/SOB in young IBD patient | MI risk elevated during flare | ECG, troponin |
| Painful rapidly expanding skin ulcer with violaceous border | Pyoderma gangrenosum (don't debride — pathergy) | Dermatology; systemic steroid + infliximab |
| New jaundice or pruritus, ALP elevation | PSC progression or CCA | MRCP ± ERCP, CA 19-9 |
| New peripheral numbness/weakness | B12 deficiency, demyelination, drug toxicity | B12, MRI brain/cord; stop anti-TNF if demyelination |
| Inflammatory back pain >3 months, age <45 | Axial SpA | MRI SI joints, HLA-B27, rheum |
| Lip/face swelling with buccal cobblestoning | Orofacial granulomatosis (often oral CD) | Dermatology/GI; topical steroids |
| Fragility fracture, height loss, chronic steroid use | Osteoporosis | DEXA, start bisphosphonate if T≤-2.5 |
| Unexplained anemia | IDA (common), ACD, B12 deficiency | Ferritin + TSAT + CRP, B12, folate; IV iron if active disease |
| Patient with MS wants anti-TNF | Contraindication | Use vedolizumab, ustekinumab, or JAK instead |
| Rash or new psoriasis on anti-TNF | Paradoxical psoriasis | Topical tx; switch to ustekinumab if severe |

## 19. Annual EIM Monitoring Checklist

### At IBD diagnosis and at least annually
- **Full history review**: joint pain, back pain, eye symptoms, skin changes, mood (PHQ-9, GAD-7).
- **Physical exam**: joints (tenderness, swelling, enthesitis, dactylitis), skin (including perianal, sacrum, stoma if present), oral cavity.
- **Labs**:
  - CBC + differential
  - CMP (LFTs + renal function)
  - CRP, ESR (activity markers)
  - **Ferritin + TSAT** (use ECCO cutoffs)
  - B12 (especially CD with ileal disease/resection)
  - 25-OH vitamin D
  - TSH (baseline + if symptomatic)
  - Urinalysis
  - ALP trend (if rising → MRCP for PSC)

### Periodic / indication-based
- **DEXA scan**: Baseline if risk factors; repeat **every 2-3 years** (more frequently if on ≥3 months steroids).
- **Annual eye exam**: Comprehensive ophthalmologic exam for all IBD patients (ECCO 2024).
- **Annual full-body skin exam**: Especially on thiopurines or anti-TNF.
- **Annual dental exam** (periodontal risk).
- **MRCP**: If unexplained cholestasis or PSC-IBD patient — then annual for CCA surveillance.
- **Colonoscopy surveillance**:
  - 8 years post-diagnosis for extensive colitis, then q1-3 years.
  - **PSC-IBD: annual from PSC diagnosis.**
- **VTE prophylaxis**: For all hospitalized IBD patients (LMWH default).
- **Vaccinations**: Annual flu (inactivated), pneumococcal (PCV20 or PCV15→PPSV23), HPV (up to age 45 on immunosuppression), COVID boosters, RSV (eligible patients), zoster (Shingrix — especially on immunosuppression, age 19+ per ACIP 2022 for immunocompromised). **Avoid live vaccines** (MMR, varicella, yellow fever, live zoster, live typhoid, BCG) in patients on immunosuppressants.
- **TB screening before anti-TNF / JAK initiation** (IGRA + CXR); periodic re-screen.
- **HBV screening before immunosuppression** (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs); vaccinate non-immune.
- **Cervical cancer screening** (annual Pap on immunosuppression).

### Patient-facing summary
Tell patients with IBD that even in remission they need these check-ins:
1. Labs every 3-12 months
2. Eye exam annually
3. Skin exam annually
4. Dental exam annually
5. DEXA every 2-3 years (risk-dependent)
6. Colonoscopy per surveillance schedule
7. Mental health check at every visit
8. Immunizations up to date
9. Aggressive CV risk factor control (BP, lipids, DM, smoking)
