# Article 1: What Is Crohn's Disease?

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider.*

## Your Immune System Is Confused

Crohn's disease is a type of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). In simple terms, your immune system — which is supposed to protect you from germs — has gotten confused. Instead of only attacking harmful bacteria and viruses, it's also attacking the healthy tissue in your digestive tract.

This causes inflammation. Think of inflammation like a fire burning inside your intestines. When that fire is active, it damages the lining of your gut, causing pain, diarrhea, bleeding, and a long list of other symptoms.

## Where Does It Happen?

Crohn's can affect any part of your digestive tract, from your mouth all the way to your anus. But it most commonly hits the end of the small intestine (called the ileum) and the beginning of the large intestine (the colon). Unlike ulcerative colitis, which only affects the colon and moves in a continuous pattern, Crohn's can skip around — you might have a healthy section right next to an inflamed one.

The inflammation in Crohn's also goes deeper. While ulcerative colitis only affects the inner lining of the intestine, Crohn's can burrow through the full thickness of the intestinal wall. This is why Crohn's can lead to complications like fistulas (abnormal tunnels between organs) and strictures (narrowed sections).

## How Is It Different From Ulcerative Colitis?

People often confuse the two. Here's the key difference:

- **Crohn's disease**: Can affect any part of the GI tract. Inflammation goes deep into the bowel wall. Can skip areas (patches of healthy tissue between inflamed areas). May cause fistulas and strictures.
- **Ulcerative colitis**: Only affects the colon and rectum. Inflammation stays in the inner lining. Moves in a continuous pattern (no skipping). Rarely causes fistulas.

About 5-15% of IBD patients have features of both and are diagnosed with "indeterminate colitis." If that's you, don't worry — the treatment approach is similar.

## Who Gets Crohn's?

Crohn's affects about 1.6 million Americans, and that number is growing. It can start at any age, but it's most often diagnosed between ages 15 and 35. It affects men and women equally. Some key facts:

- **Genetics play a role.** If a close family member has IBD, your risk is higher. Researchers have identified over 200 genes linked to IBD. But having the genes doesn't mean you'll definitely get it — most people with these genes never develop Crohn's.
- **Environment matters.** Crohn's is more common in developed countries and urban areas. Smoking significantly increases your risk (and makes existing Crohn's worse). Diet, antibiotics in childhood, and other environmental factors may contribute.
- **Your gut bacteria are involved.** People with Crohn's often have different gut bacteria than healthy people — fewer beneficial species and more potentially harmful ones. Researchers are still figuring out whether this is a cause or a result of the disease.

## It's Not Your Fault

Let's be clear about something: **you did not cause your Crohn's disease.** It's not from eating the wrong foods, being too stressed, or not taking care of yourself. Crohn's is an immune-mediated disease with genetic and environmental factors. You didn't choose it, and you couldn't have prevented it.

What you *can* do is manage it. And that's what this app is here to help with.

## What Causes Flares?

A "flare" is when your disease becomes active after a period of feeling okay (remission). Flares can be triggered by:

- Stopping or missing your medications
- Certain foods (which vary from person to person — that's why tracking matters)
- Infections
- Stress (it doesn't cause Crohn's, but it can trigger flares)
- NSAIDs like ibuprofen and aspirin
- Smoking
- Sometimes, flares happen for no clear reason at all

The unpredictability is one of the hardest parts. That's why tracking your symptoms, food, stress, and sleep over time can help you identify YOUR patterns.

# Article 2: Types of Crohn's Disease

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider.*

Crohn's disease is classified by where it occurs in your digestive tract. The location matters because it affects your symptoms, which nutrients you might be missing, and how your doctor treats it.

## Ileocolitis — The Most Common Type

**Where:** The end of the small intestine (ileum) and the beginning of the large intestine (colon).

**How common:** About 50% of Crohn's patients have this type.

**Typical symptoms:** Diarrhea, cramping in the lower right side of your abdomen, weight loss, and fatigue. You may also have pain after eating.

**What to know:** This is the most common site for surgery. If you've had your ileocecal valve removed (the valve between your small and large intestine), you have this type. After ileal resection, you're at risk for B12 deficiency and bile acid malabsorption — two things this app helps you track.

## Ileitis

**Where:** Only the ileum (the last section of the small intestine), without colon involvement.

**How common:** About 30% of Crohn's patients.

**Typical symptoms:** Similar to ileocolitis — pain in the lower right abdomen, diarrhea, weight loss. Fistulas and abscesses can form in this area.

**What to know:** The ileum is where your body absorbs vitamin B12 and bile acids. If this section is inflamed or has been surgically removed, you'll need to monitor these nutrients closely.

## Crohn's Colitis (Granulomatous Colitis)

**Where:** Only the colon (large intestine).

**How common:** About 20% of Crohn's patients.

**Typical symptoms:** Diarrhea (often bloody), rectal bleeding, urgency, and joint pain. Can look similar to ulcerative colitis, which sometimes makes diagnosis tricky.

**What to know:** Colon involvement increases the risk of certain complications like skin problems and joint pain. Colonoscopies are the primary way to monitor this type.

## Gastroduodenal Crohn's

**Where:** The stomach and the first part of the small intestine (duodenum).

**How common:** Rare — about 5% of cases.

**Typical symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, and weight loss. Pain in the upper abdomen, especially after eating. Some people feel full quickly.

**What to know:** This type can affect how you absorb nutrients from food since the duodenum is a major absorption site. Iron and calcium absorption happen here, so deficiencies are common.

## Jejunoileitis

**Where:** The jejunum — the middle section of the small intestine.

**How common:** Rare.

**Typical symptoms:** Cramping after meals, diarrhea, and malnutrition. Pain tends to be in the middle or upper abdomen.

**What to know:** Extensive small bowel involvement can lead to malabsorption of many nutrients. Patients with this type may need more aggressive nutritional support.

## Perianal Crohn's

**Where:** The area around the anus.

**How common:** About 30% of Crohn's patients develop perianal involvement at some point.

**Typical symptoms:** Pain around the anus, drainage, swelling, skin tags, fistulas (abnormal tunnels), and abscesses. This can be one of the most debilitating forms.

**What to know:** Perianal Crohn's often requires a combination of medications and surgery. It can significantly impact quality of life and is an area where early, aggressive treatment makes a big difference.

## Why Location Matters for YOU

Your Crohn's type determines:
- Which nutrients you need to monitor most closely
- Which symptoms to expect and track
- How your food is digested and absorbed
- Which medications are most likely to work
- What kind of surgery you might need (or have had)

When you set up this app, telling us where your Crohn's is located helps us tailor everything — from which nutrient deficiencies to flag to which foods to watch out for.

# Article 3: Understanding Your Diagnosis

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider.*

Getting diagnosed with Crohn's can feel overwhelming. You've probably had a lot of tests, heard a lot of terms, and may not have had time to process what it all means. Here's a plain-language guide.

## The Tests and What They Mean

### Colonoscopy
A camera on a flexible tube that looks at the inside of your colon and sometimes the end of your small intestine. Your doctor is looking for inflammation, ulcers, and narrowing. They'll take biopsies (tiny tissue samples) to examine under a microscope. This is the most important test for diagnosing and monitoring Crohn's.

### MR Enterography (MRE)
An MRI scan of your abdomen after you drink a contrast solution. This gives detailed pictures of your small intestine — an area the colonoscope can't always reach. It can show thickening of the bowel wall, narrowing, fistulas, and abscesses. No radiation, which makes it safe for repeated use.

### CT Scan
Similar to MRE but uses X-rays instead of magnets. Faster but involves radiation. Often used in emergencies to check for complications like bowel obstruction or abscess.

### Capsule Endoscopy
You swallow a tiny camera in a pill that takes thousands of pictures as it travels through your small intestine. Useful when MRE and colonoscopy don't show the full picture. You can't have this if you have a known stricture (the capsule could get stuck).

### Blood Tests
- **CRP (C-Reactive Protein):** A marker of inflammation in your body. Higher = more inflammation. Normal is usually below 5 mg/L.
- **ESR (Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate):** Another inflammation marker. Measures how fast your red blood cells settle in a tube.
- **CBC (Complete Blood Count):** Checks for anemia (low red blood cells), infection (high white blood cells), and overall blood health.
- **Albumin:** A protein that reflects your nutritional status. Low albumin can mean you're not absorbing enough nutrients.
- **Iron panel and ferritin:** Checks for iron deficiency, which is very common in Crohn's.
- **Vitamin B12 and folate:** Checks for deficiencies — especially important if your ileum is involved.
- **Liver function tests:** Important if you're on certain medications like azathioprine or methotrexate.

### Stool Tests
- **Fecal Calprotectin:** This is a game-changer for monitoring Crohn's. It measures inflammation specifically in your gut (not your whole body like CRP). A level below 150 generally means your gut inflammation is well controlled. Above 250 usually means active inflammation. It can help your doctor decide if you need a colonoscopy or if your treatment is working.

## What "Mild," "Moderate," and "Severe" Mean

Your doctor classifies your disease activity to guide treatment:

- **Remission:** Few or no symptoms. You feel mostly normal. Inflammation markers are low.
- **Mild-to-moderate:** You can eat and walk around, but you have symptoms — diarrhea, some pain, maybe weight loss. No signs of serious complications.
- **Moderate-to-severe:** Symptoms are impacting your daily life. You may have fevers, significant weight loss, or pain that doesn't respond to basic treatment.
- **Severe/fulminant:** Very sick. Can't eat, severe pain, possible complications requiring hospitalization.

## Questions to Ask Your GI Doctor

Bring this list to your next appointment:

1. Where exactly is my Crohn's located?
2. How severe is my disease right now?
3. What is the goal of my current treatment?
4. How will we know if my treatment is working?
5. How often should I get blood work and stool tests?
6. When is my next colonoscopy?
7. Should I be taking any supplements?
8. Are there any foods I should specifically avoid?
9. Should I see an IBD-specialized dietitian?
10. What symptoms should make me call you between appointments?

# Article 4: Crohn's Disease Stages and Progression

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider.*

Crohn's disease isn't static — it changes over time. Understanding how it can progress helps you make informed decisions about your treatment.

## Remission — The Goal

Remission means your disease is under control. There are different levels:

- **Clinical remission:** You feel good. Symptoms are gone or minimal. This is what you notice day-to-day.
- **Endoscopic remission:** Your doctor looks inside with a colonoscopy and sees healing — the inflammation is actually gone, not just masked. This is the deeper goal.
- **Deep remission:** Both clinical AND endoscopic remission. Your symptoms are controlled AND the inflammation has healed. This is the gold standard that modern treatment aims for.

Why does the distinction matter? Because you can feel fine while inflammation is still silently damaging your intestines. That's why your doctor monitors with blood tests, calprotectin, and periodic colonoscopies — not just by asking "how do you feel?"

## How Crohn's Can Change Over Time

At diagnosis, most people have inflammatory disease — the intestine is inflamed but otherwise structurally intact. Over time, if inflammation isn't controlled, it can progress:

### Inflammatory Stage
- Active inflammation in the bowel wall
- Symptoms: pain, diarrhea, fatigue
- **This is the stage where treatment is most effective.** Catching and controlling inflammation here can prevent progression.

### Stricturing Disease
- Chronic inflammation causes scarring (fibrosis)
- Scarring narrows the intestinal passageway (stricture)
- Symptoms: cramping after eating, bloating, nausea, vomiting (if food can't pass)
- Some strictures respond to medication. Others need dilation (stretching during colonoscopy) or surgery.
- **Dietary adjustments matter here** — low-residue foods that pass through narrow spaces more easily

### Fistulizing Disease
- Inflammation burrows through the bowel wall, creating abnormal tunnels (fistulas)
- Fistulas can connect the intestine to other organs, the skin, or the area around the anus
- Symptoms vary by location — drainage, pain, infection, sometimes passage of stool through abnormal openings
- Often requires a combination of biologics and surgery

### The Key Statistic
Within 10 years of diagnosis, about 50% of Crohn's patients will develop either stricturing or fistulizing disease if inflammation isn't adequately controlled. This is why your doctor pushes for treatment even when you feel okay.

## Why Early, Aggressive Treatment Matters

The old approach was "step-up" therapy — start with mild drugs, escalate only when they fail. The problem: while you're on milder drugs that aren't fully controlling the inflammation, damage accumulates silently.

The newer approach is "treat-to-target" — set a specific goal (usually endoscopic healing), start effective treatment early, and monitor closely. If you're not hitting the target, adjust quickly.

Biologics (like infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, risankizumab) used early can prevent progression to stricturing or fistulizing disease. Studies show that patients who achieve deep remission early have better long-term outcomes.

## What This Means for You

- Take your medications consistently, even when you feel good
- Don't skip monitoring appointments
- Track your symptoms so your doctor has real data, not just "I feel okay"
- Ask your doctor: "Am I in endoscopic remission, or just clinical remission?"

# Article 5: Life After Bowel Resection Surgery

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider.*

If you've had bowel resection surgery, this article is especially for you. Surgery is a big deal — physically and emotionally. Here's what's happening in your body and what to expect going forward.

## What Happened During Your Surgery

In a bowel resection, a surgeon removed a section of your intestine that was too damaged by Crohn's disease to function properly — usually due to strictures, fistulas, or inflammation that wasn't responding to medication. The healthy ends were then reconnected (called an anastomosis).

The most common resection for Crohn's is an **ileocecal resection** — removing the last part of your small intestine (ileum) and the first part of your large intestine (cecum), along with the ileocecal valve that sits between them.

## What Your Body Is Adjusting To

Your body is remarkably adaptable, but it needs time. Here's what's different now:

### If your ileum was removed:
- **B12 absorption is reduced.** The ileum is the ONLY place your body absorbs vitamin B12. If a significant portion was removed, you'll likely need B12 supplements or injections for life.
- **Bile acid absorption is disrupted.** Your ileum normally recycles bile acids (which help digest fat). Without it, excess bile acids reach your colon and cause watery diarrhea. This is called bile acid malabsorption, and it's very common after ileal resection.
- **Fat absorption may be reduced.** Without proper bile acid recycling, your body can't digest fat as efficiently. This can lead to fatty stools, diarrhea after high-fat meals, and deficiencies in fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).
- **Kidney stone risk increases.** Unabsorbed fat binds to calcium in your gut, leaving oxalate free to be absorbed and form kidney stones.

### If your ileocecal valve was removed:
- **Faster transit time.** The valve normally slows the movement of food from your small intestine to your large intestine. Without it, food moves through faster, giving your body less time to absorb nutrients.
- **Possible bacterial overgrowth.** The valve also prevents bacteria from the large intestine from creeping back into the small intestine. Without it, bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) can occur.

### If part of your colon was removed:
- **More frequent bowel movements.** Your colon absorbs water. Less colon = less water absorption = looser, more frequent stools.
- **Electrolyte imbalances.** The colon absorbs sodium and water. You may need to be more intentional about electrolyte intake.

## The Recovery Phases

Your diet after surgery follows a careful progression. The detailed guide is in the Post-Resection Dietary Guide section of this app, but here's the overview:

1. **Clear liquids** — immediately after surgery
2. **Full liquids** — as your gut wakes up
3. **Low-residue/low-fiber** — for the first month
4. **Gradual reintroduction** — one food at a time, over months
5. **Long-term maintenance** — your new normal

The most important rule: **go slow.** Your gut just went through major trauma. Reintroduce foods one at a time, keep a food diary (that's what this app is for), and give your body time.

## Will I Need Surgery Again?

Let's be honest about this. Crohn's disease is not cured by surgery — it can come back. The statistics:

- About 50% of patients who have surgery for Crohn's will need another surgery within 10 years if they're not on preventive medication
- With modern biologic medications started after surgery, the recurrence rate drops significantly
- Colonoscopy at 6-12 months after surgery is standard to check for early recurrence at the anastomosis site

This isn't meant to scare you. Many patients do very well after surgery, especially with proper medication and monitoring. The key is:
- Stay on your preventive medication
- Get your post-surgical colonoscopy on schedule
- Monitor your symptoms and labs
- Work with your GI doctor on a long-term plan

## Emotional Recovery

Nobody talks about this enough: surgery changes more than your body. It's completely normal to feel:

- **Grief** — for your old normal, for the body you had before
- **Anxiety** — about complications, recurrence, diet restrictions
- **Relief** — if you were very sick before surgery, you might feel better than you have in years
- **Frustration** — at the dietary restrictions, the recovery time, the unpredictability
- **Isolation** — feeling like nobody understands what you're going through

All of these feelings are valid. If you're struggling, talk to someone — a therapist who understands chronic illness, a support group, or even the community section of this app. You are not alone, and what you're feeling is normal.

# Article 6: Understanding Your Medications

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider before making changes to your medication.*

Crohn's medications can feel confusing — there are so many types, and they all work differently. Here's a straightforward guide.

## The Big Picture

Crohn's medications fall into two categories:
1. **Induction therapy** — to get a flare under control (put out the fire)
2. **Maintenance therapy** — to keep you in remission (prevent the fire from coming back)

Some medications do both. Your doctor will choose based on how severe your disease is, where it is, and how you've responded to treatments before.

## The Main Types

### Aminosalicylates (5-ASAs)
**Examples:** Mesalamine (Pentasa, Asacol, Lialda), Sulfasalazine

These are anti-inflammatory drugs that work directly on the lining of your gut. They're most commonly used for mild Crohn's, particularly in the colon. They're considered the mildest treatment option.

**Good to know:** Sulfasalazine can deplete folate — your doctor may recommend a folic acid supplement.

### Corticosteroids
**Examples:** Prednisone, Budesonide (Entocort)

Steroids are powerful anti-inflammatory drugs used for short-term flare control. They work fast — many patients feel better within days. But they have significant side effects with long-term use (bone loss, weight gain, mood changes, diabetes risk), so they're meant to be temporary bridges, not long-term solutions.

**Good to know:** Prednisone depletes calcium, vitamin D, potassium, and other nutrients. If you're on steroids, talk to your doctor about supplements.

### Immunomodulators
**Examples:** Azathioprine (Imuran), 6-Mercaptopurine, Methotrexate

These suppress your immune system more broadly. They take longer to work (2-3 months to reach full effect) but are effective for maintenance. Methotrexate depletes folate, so folic acid supplementation is standard.

**Good to know:** These require regular blood monitoring (CBC and liver function tests). If you've had bowel resection, injectable methotrexate may be preferred over oral — absorption of pills can be unpredictable after surgery.

### Biologics
This is where treatment has made the biggest advances. Biologics are lab-made proteins that target specific parts of your immune system.

**TNF Inhibitors:** Infliximab (Remicade), Adalimumab (Humira), Certolizumab (Cimzia)
- Block a protein called TNF-alpha that drives inflammation
- The first biologics approved for Crohn's, with the longest track record
- Many biosimilars now available (same drug, lower cost)

**Integrin Inhibitors:** Vedolizumab (Entyvio)
- Blocks immune cells from entering the gut specifically
- "Gut-selective" — fewer body-wide side effects
- Particularly good for patients who want to avoid systemic immunosuppression

**IL-12/23 Inhibitors:** Ustekinumab (Stelara), Risankizumab (Skyrizi)
- Block interleukins that drive the inflammatory cascade
- Risankizumab is one of the newest options, showing very strong results in clinical trials

**IL-23 Inhibitors:** Mirikizumab (Omvoh), Guselkumab (Tremfya)
- Even more targeted than IL-12/23 inhibitors
- The newest approved options (2025) with promising data

### JAK Inhibitors
**Example:** Upadacitinib (Rinvoq)

These are oral pills (not injections) that block enzymes called JAK that signal inflammation. They work fast and are taken daily by mouth. Newer option for Crohn's.

**Good to know:** These are oral medications — absorption may be affected by bowel resection. Discuss with your GI.

## Biosimilars — Are They Safe?

Yes. Biosimilars are essentially the same biological drug made by a different manufacturer — like generic versions of biologics. The FDA requires them to have the same safety and effectiveness as the original. They're often significantly cheaper. Many patients switch to biosimilars without any issues. If your insurance requires a biosimilar switch, it's generally safe.

## Why Adherence Matters

This is the hard truth: Crohn's medications only work if you take them. Skipping doses is one of the most common triggers for flares and disease progression. We understand it's frustrating — side effects are real, injections aren't fun, and when you feel good, it's tempting to stop.

But here's the thing: many Crohn's medications are preventing inflammation you can't feel yet. Stopping them often leads to a flare within weeks to months, and sometimes the medication doesn't work as well when you restart it (especially biologics, where your body can develop antibodies against the drug).

This app helps with medication reminders so you never accidentally miss a dose.

# Article 7: Nutrition Basics for Crohn's

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider or a registered dietitian.*

Let's start with the most important thing: **there is no single "Crohn's diet."** Anyone who tells you there's one diet that works for every Crohn's patient is oversimplifying a complex disease. What triggers a flare in one person may be perfectly fine for another.

That said, there ARE principles backed by research that can guide you. Here's what we know.

## Why Finding YOUR Trigger Foods Matters

Every Crohn's patient has a different set of foods that cause problems. Some people can eat dairy with no issues. Some can't tolerate eggs. Some are fine with salads in remission but react to raw vegetables during even mild symptoms.

The only way to figure out YOUR triggers is to track what you eat and how you feel afterward. Over time, patterns emerge. That's exactly what this app's food-symptom correlation feature does — it watches for connections between what you eat and how you feel, so you can make informed decisions instead of guessing.

## The Dietary Approaches — An Honest Overview

### Low-FODMAP Diet
FODMAPs are specific types of carbohydrates that are poorly absorbed and get fermented by gut bacteria, causing gas, bloating, and diarrhea. The low-FODMAP diet temporarily removes these, then reintroduces them one at a time to find your triggers.

**The evidence:** Strong for reducing IBS-like symptoms in IBD. Does NOT reduce intestinal inflammation — it's about symptom management, not disease control.

**Best for:** Patients with bloating, gas, and diarrhea that persists even when inflammation is controlled. Should be done with a FODMAP-trained dietitian.

### Crohn's Disease Exclusion Diet (CDED)
Developed specifically for Crohn's, the CDED is a whole-food diet that excludes specific foods thought to damage the gut microbiome and intestinal barrier. It has the strongest evidence of any dietary approach for actually reducing inflammation in Crohn's.

**The evidence:** Multiple clinical trials showing it can induce remission, sometimes as effectively as exclusive enteral nutrition (liquid diet). Particularly strong data in children and young adults.

**Best for:** Patients interested in using diet as part of their treatment strategy. Requires commitment but allows real food (unlike liquid diets).

### Specific Carbohydrate Diet (SCD)
Eliminates complex carbohydrates (grains, most sugars, starchy vegetables) based on the theory that they feed harmful gut bacteria. Allows simple carbohydrates (honey, fruit, certain vegetables).

**The evidence:** Some clinical evidence supporting benefit in IBD. Less robust than CDED data. Very restrictive — hard to sustain long-term.

**Best for:** Patients who want to try a structured elimination approach. Works better for some people than others.

### Mediterranean Diet
Rich in olive oil, fish, vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and lean proteins. Low in processed foods, red meat, and sugar. Naturally anti-inflammatory.

**The evidence:** Strong evidence for anti-inflammatory benefits across many conditions. Growing evidence specifically in IBD for maintaining remission. Less restrictive than other diets, making it more sustainable.

**Best for:** Long-term maintenance eating pattern, especially during remission. A sensible baseline that many IBD dietitians recommend.

## The Fiber Debate

For years, Crohn's patients were told to avoid fiber entirely. That advice is outdated — but it's nuanced:

- **Soluble fiber** (oats, psyllium, bananas, cooked carrots) dissolves in water and forms a gel. It generally slows things down and can help with loose stools. Most Crohn's patients tolerate it well.
- **Insoluble fiber** (wheat bran, raw vegetables, skins, seeds, nuts) doesn't dissolve. It adds bulk and can be irritating, especially during a flare or in areas with narrowing (strictures).

**The rule of thumb:** In remission, gradually increase fiber — especially soluble fiber. During a flare, reduce fiber, particularly insoluble. If you have a stricture, work with your doctor on specific fiber limits.

## Hydration: Why It Matters More for You

Crohn's patients lose more water than average through diarrhea. After bowel resection, this is even more pronounced — your body has less intestine to absorb water. Dehydration is one of the most common and underappreciated problems.

Plain water isn't always enough. When you have significant diarrhea, you also lose electrolytes (sodium, potassium). An oral rehydration solution (water + salt + sugar in specific ratios) is absorbed better than plain water because the sugar helps your body pull sodium and water into your cells.

Sip fluids throughout the day rather than drinking large amounts at once. Drink between meals rather than during meals (drinking during meals can speed transit time).

## Eating During a Flare vs. Remission

**During a flare:**
- Low-residue, low-fiber foods
- Well-cooked vegetables (no raw)
- White rice, white bread, simple carbs
- Lean proteins (chicken, fish, eggs)
- Small, frequent meals (5-6 per day)
- Extra fluids and electrolytes
- Avoid: raw vegetables, whole grains, spicy food, caffeine, alcohol, high-fat foods

**During remission:**
- Gradually expand your diet
- Experiment with new foods one at a time
- Prioritize anti-inflammatory foods (fish, olive oil, colorful vegetables)
- Include more fiber (especially soluble)
- Focus on nutrient-dense foods to replenish what you may have lost during flares

## The Psychology of Food Fear

After a bad flare, it's natural to develop anxiety around food. Some patients restrict their diet so severely that they become malnourished — not from Crohn's itself, but from fear of eating.

Here's what to remember:
- Not every stomach ache is a flare
- Your safe food list will grow over time as you learn your body
- Eating too little is its own health risk
- A registered dietitian who specializes in IBD can help you expand your diet safely
- Tracking food and symptoms (instead of just avoiding everything) puts you in control

You deserve to enjoy food. It might take time to rebuild that relationship, but it's possible.

# Article 8: The Low-FODMAP Diet Explained

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider or a FODMAP-trained dietitian.*

## What Are FODMAPs?

FODMAP stands for **F**ermentable **O**ligosaccharides, **D**isaccharides, **M**onosaccharides, **A**nd **P**olyols. These are types of short-chain carbohydrates that are poorly absorbed in your small intestine. When they reach your large intestine, gut bacteria ferment them, producing gas. They also draw water into your intestine through osmosis.

For people with a sensitive gut — which includes many Crohn's patients — this combination of gas and water causes bloating, cramping, diarrhea, and urgency.

In plain English: certain sugars and fibers in food aren't absorbed well. They end up in your colon where bacteria eat them and produce gas. Your gut overreacts.

## The Five FODMAP Groups

| Group | What It Is | Found In |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| **Fructose** (excess) | Fruit sugar | Apples, pears, honey, agave, high-fructose corn syrup, mango, watermelon |
| **Lactose** | Milk sugar | Milk, soft cheese, yogurt, ice cream, cream |
| **Fructans** | A type of fiber | Wheat, onions, garlic, artichokes, inulin/chicory root |
| **GOS** (Galacto-oligosaccharides) | A type of fiber | Beans, lentils, chickpeas, soybeans |
| **Polyols** | Sugar alcohols | Sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol (in stone fruits, mushrooms, sugar-free products) |

The tricky part: it's not usually ALL FODMAPs that bother you. You might be fine with lactose but react badly to fructans. That's why the reintroduction phase is so important — it helps you figure out YOUR specific triggers.

## The Three Phases

### Phase 1: Elimination (2-6 weeks)
Remove all high-FODMAP foods from your diet. This isn't permanent — it's a diagnostic tool. If your symptoms improve significantly during this phase, FODMAPs are likely contributing to your symptoms.

**Important:** This phase should not last more than 6 weeks. Long-term strict low-FODMAP can harm your gut bacteria diversity.

### Phase 2: Reintroduction (6-8 weeks)
Systematically test one FODMAP group at a time:
- Eat a small amount of a test food on day 1
- Increase the amount on day 2
- Eat a larger amount on day 3
- Monitor symptoms for 3 days after
- Move to the next FODMAP group

This tells you which groups are problematic and in what amounts. You may find you tolerate lactose perfectly but react to even small amounts of fructans.

### Phase 3: Personalization (Ongoing)
Based on what you learned in Phase 2, build YOUR personalized diet. You eat freely from low-FODMAP foods and your tolerated FODMAP groups, while limiting only the groups that actually caused symptoms.

Most people end up with a diet that's much less restrictive than the elimination phase. The goal was never to stay on strict low-FODMAP forever.

## FODMAP Stacking — The Hidden Trap

Even foods that are low-FODMAP individually can cause problems when you eat several of them in one meal. This is called "stacking."

Example: A half cup of blueberries is low-FODMAP. A small banana is low-FODMAP. A tablespoon of honey is moderate-FODMAP. But eat all three together in a smoothie, and the combined fructose load might push you over your threshold.

The rule: Watch your total FODMAP load per meal, not just individual foods.

## Why You Should Do This With a Dietitian

The low-FODMAP diet is more complex than it looks. A FODMAP-trained dietitian helps you:
- Make sure you're still getting adequate nutrition during elimination
- Design a proper reintroduction protocol
- Interpret your results correctly
- Avoid unnecessarily restricting foods that are actually fine for you
- Adapt the protocol for Crohn's-specific needs (which differ from general IBS)

Many IBD centers have dietitians on staff. The Crohn's and Colitis Dietitians network is another good resource.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Staying on elimination forever.** The strict phase is temporary. Long-term restriction harms gut bacteria.
2. **Thinking low-FODMAP = healthy.** Potato chips are low-FODMAP. That doesn't make them a health food.
3. **Ignoring serving sizes.** Most foods are only low-FODMAP up to a certain amount. A few almonds are fine; a handful may not be.
4. **Forgetting about garlic and onion.** These are in EVERYTHING — sauces, marinades, spice blends, broths. Read every label.
5. **Not testing enough foods.** Some people get so used to the elimination phase that they're afraid to reintroduce. The goal is to EXPAND your diet, not shrink it.
6. **Confusing FODMAP sensitivity with Crohn's inflammation.** FODMAPs cause functional symptoms (bloating, gas) but don't cause intestinal inflammation. If you're having a flare, that's a Crohn's issue, not a FODMAP issue.

## Crohn's-Specific FODMAP Considerations

- During a flare, many high-FODMAP foods are already on your avoid list (raw fruits, vegetables, beans). Low-FODMAP and flare-safe diets overlap significantly.
- After bowel resection, your FODMAP tolerance may change. The ileum plays a role in carbohydrate absorption, so you may be more sensitive to certain FODMAPs post-surgery.
- Some Crohn's medications (like antibiotics) can alter your gut bacteria, temporarily changing your FODMAP tolerance.

*Based on Monash University FODMAP research, King's College London diet studies, and Cox et al. (2020) randomized trial on FODMAP diet in IBD.*

# Article 9: Mental Health and Crohn's Disease

> *This information is for educational purposes only. If you are in crisis, contact the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline by calling or texting 988.*

## The Gut-Brain Connection Is Real

This isn't woo-woo science — the connection between your gut and your brain is one of the most active areas of medical research. Your gut has its own nervous system (the enteric nervous system) with over 100 million nerve cells. It communicates constantly with your brain through the vagus nerve, hormones, and immune signals.

When your gut is inflamed, it sends distress signals to your brain. When your brain is stressed, it sends signals that can affect gut function and inflammation. It's a two-way street.

This means your mental health and your Crohn's are not separate issues — they're connected. Treating one can help the other.

## Anxiety and Crohn's

Let's be real: living with Crohn's gives you plenty to be anxious about.

- Will I make it to the bathroom in time?
- What if I have a flare at work? On a date? On vacation?
- Is this pain just a bad food, or is something serious happening?
- Will I need another surgery?
- Can I eat this? What if it makes me sick?

This anxiety is a rational response to a genuinely unpredictable disease. You're not being dramatic. You're not overreacting. Your body has taught you that bad things can happen suddenly, and your brain is trying to protect you.

But when anxiety becomes constant and interfering with your daily life — avoiding social events, restricting your diet to almost nothing, checking for bathroom locations obsessively — it may be time to get support.

## Depression and IBD

Studies consistently show that depression is 2-3 times more common in people with IBD than in the general population. This isn't weakness. It's a combination of:

- **Biological factors:** Chronic inflammation produces cytokines that directly affect brain chemistry. Your disease is literally changing your brain's biochemistry.
- **The burden of chronic illness:** Managing medications, appointments, diet restrictions, pain, fatigue, and unpredictability is exhausting.
- **Social isolation:** Bathroom urgency, fatigue, and diet restrictions can make it hard to participate in normal social activities.
- **Body image:** Surgery scars, weight changes, ostomy bags, and medication side effects can impact how you feel about your body.
- **Loss of identity:** "I used to be someone who could eat anything, travel anywhere, do everything. Now I can't."

## Stress and Flares: What the Research Actually Shows

Stress does NOT cause Crohn's disease. But research does show that:

- Perceived stress is associated with an increased risk of flares
- Major life events (job loss, divorce, bereavement) can precede flares in some patients
- Chronic daily stress may be more damaging than acute stressful events
- The mechanism likely involves stress hormones (cortisol) affecting immune function and gut barrier integrity

This doesn't mean your flares are "in your head." It means your nervous system, immune system, and gut are all connected, and stress is one of many factors that can tip the balance.

## Practical Coping Strategies

### For Anxiety
- **Plan, don't avoid.** Instead of skipping the event, scope out the bathrooms. Pack an emergency kit. Having a plan reduces anxiety more than avoidance does.
- **Breathing exercises.** The 4-7-8 technique (inhale 4 seconds, hold 7, exhale 8) activates your vagus nerve and calms gut-brain signaling.
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).** The most evidence-based therapy for anxiety in chronic illness. It helps you identify and challenge catastrophic thinking patterns.

### For Depression
- **Movement.** Even gentle exercise (walking, yoga, stretching) has demonstrated antidepressant effects in IBD patients.
- **Connection.** Tell someone how you're feeling. A therapist, a support group, a trusted friend. Isolation makes depression worse.
- **Routine.** Depression makes everything feel pointless. A simple daily routine (wake up, eat, move, log your symptoms, go outside) provides structure.
- **Medication.** Antidepressants can be helpful and are safe to combine with most Crohn's medications. Talk to your doctor — this is not a sign of failure.

### For Stress Management
- **Mindfulness/meditation.** Even 5-10 minutes daily has shown measurable benefits in IBD studies.
- **Sleep hygiene.** Poor sleep is both a cause and consequence of stress. Prioritize it.
- **Set boundaries.** It's okay to cancel plans. It's okay to say "I need to rest." It's okay to not explain your disease to everyone.
- **Track your stress.** This app lets you log stress levels alongside symptoms. Over time, you may see patterns that help you prioritize stress management.

## When to Seek Professional Help

Consider reaching out to a mental health professional if:
- You're avoiding activities you used to enjoy because of Crohn's-related fear
- Anxiety or sadness is present most days for more than 2 weeks
- You're having trouble sleeping, eating, or functioning
- You're using alcohol or substances to cope
- You're having thoughts of self-harm

Look for therapists who specialize in chronic illness, health psychology, or medical psychology. Many IBD centers have psychologists on staff. Online therapy is also an option for days when leaving the house feels impossible.

## You Are Not Your Disease

Crohn's is something you have. It is not who you are. You are a person living with a challenging condition, and the fact that you're here — reading this, trying to manage your health, using this app — shows strength.

Bad days don't erase good days. Flares don't mean failure. Needing help isn't weakness.

*Based on research from the Journal of Crohn's and Colitis, Gastroenterology & Hepatology psychology reviews, and the Crohn's & Colitis Foundation mental health resources.*

# Article 10: When to Call Your Doctor

> *This information is for educational purposes only. When in doubt, call your doctor.*

## Red Flag Symptoms — Call Your Doctor Today

These need medical attention within 24 hours:

- **Fever over 101°F (38.3°C)** — especially if you're on immunosuppressants. Fever + immunosuppression = possible serious infection
- **Severe abdominal pain that's different from your usual Crohn's pain** — new location, new intensity, or sudden onset
- **Bloody stool that's more than your baseline** — especially bright red blood or large amounts
- **Inability to keep fluids down for more than 24 hours** — vomiting everything, can't drink
- **Signs of dehydration** — dark urine, dizziness when standing, dry mouth, no tears, rapid heartbeat
- **New lump or mass in your abdomen** — could be an abscess
- **Pus or drainage from your perianal area, a fistula, or a surgical site**
- **Night sweats combined with other symptoms**

## Signs of a Flare Starting

Contact your doctor if these persist for more than 3-5 days:

- Diarrhea increasing beyond your baseline
- Cramping or pain returning after a period of feeling good
- Fatigue that's worse than usual
- Loss of appetite or unintentional weight loss
- Waking at night with urgency or pain (this is almost always significant)
- Blood in stool when you normally don't have it
- Mouth sores
- Joint pain increasing

Catching a flare early gives you more treatment options. Don't wait until you're miserable.

## Signs of Bowel Obstruction — May Need ER

Go to the emergency room if you experience:

- **Severe cramping pain that comes in waves** (colicky pain)
- **Complete inability to pass stool or gas**
- **Vomiting, especially if it becomes dark green (bilious) or fecal-smelling**
- **Progressively worsening abdominal distension (bloating that keeps getting bigger)**
- **No bowel sounds** (your abdomen is completely silent when you listen)

Partial obstructions can sometimes resolve with bowel rest (no food, IV fluids). Complete obstructions may require emergency surgery.

## Signs of Infection (Especially on Immunosuppressants)

If you're taking biologics, immunomodulators, or steroids, your immune system is suppressed. Infections can be more serious. Seek care for:

- Fever over 100.4°F (38°C)
- Chills or rigors (uncontrollable shaking)
- Cough, shortness of breath, or difficulty breathing
- Painful, red, swollen skin (cellulitis)
- Pain or burning with urination
- Any wound that's red, swollen, warm, or draining pus
- Exposure to chickenpox, shingles, or tuberculosis

## When to Go to the ER vs. Call the Office

**Go to the ER:**
- Severe uncontrolled pain
- Signs of obstruction (above)
- Heavy rectal bleeding (soaking through pads)
- High fever with chills on immunosuppressants
- Signs of severe dehydration (confusion, fainting, no urine output)

**Call the office (urgent but not emergency):**
- Moderate increase in symptoms lasting more than 3 days
- Low-grade fever
- Mild-moderate increase in diarrhea or blood
- Questions about whether to adjust your medication
- New symptoms that concern you but aren't severe

## Template: What to Tell Your Doctor When You Call

Write this down before you call so you don't forget anything:

1. "My name is ___. I have Crohn's disease and I'm calling because ___."
2. "My symptoms started ___ days ago."
3. "Currently I'm having: [specific symptoms and severity]"
4. "This is [different from / similar to] my usual symptoms because ___."
5. "My current medications are: ___"
6. "I [have/have not] taken all my medications as prescribed."
7. "My last blood work / calprotectin was on [date] and results were ___."
8. "My last colonoscopy was on [date]."
9. "I [have/have not] had surgery. Most recent surgery was [date and type]."

# Article 11: Navigating Daily Life with Crohn's

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider.*

Living with Crohn's affects more than just your body. It touches every part of your daily life. Here are practical strategies from people who've been there.

## Bathroom Anxiety and Planning

The fear of not finding a bathroom in time is real and can become paralyzing. Here's how to manage it:

- **Scout ahead.** Before going somewhere new, check where bathrooms are. This app can help you build a mental map.
- **The "We Can't Wait" app** maps 50,000+ public restrooms in the US.
- **Carry an emergency kit:** wet wipes, change of underwear, plastic bag, hand sanitizer, odor spray. Having it means you almost never need it — but the confidence helps.
- **Request aisle seats** on planes, at events, in restaurants. Being near an exit reduces anxiety.
- **Talk to trusted people.** Telling a close friend "I might need to leave suddenly — it's a medical thing" removes the awkwardness.

## Travel Tips

Crohn's doesn't have to stop you from traveling. It just requires extra planning:

- **Carry medications in your carry-on.** Never check them. Bring extra in case of delays.
- **Get a doctor's letter** for injectable medications (biologics) so security doesn't question your needles.
- **Research healthcare options** at your destination — where's the nearest hospital?
- **Stick to safe foods** when traveling. This isn't the time to experiment with street food.
- **Stay hydrated.** Plane cabins are dehydrating, and foreign water may not be safe.
- **Consider travel insurance** that covers pre-existing conditions.
- **Time zone changes** matter for medications taken on a schedule. Plan ahead.

## Eating Out at Restaurants

This doesn't have to be stressful:

- **Look at the menu online first.** Identify 2-3 safe options before you arrive.
- **Don't be afraid to ask for modifications.** "Can I get that without the garlic sauce?" is completely normal.
- **Simple preparations are safest.** Grilled chicken, steamed vegetables, plain rice — most restaurants can do these.
- **Avoid buffets.** Ingredient cross-contamination is unpredictable.
- **Eat smaller portions.** You can always take the rest home.

## Work and Disclosure

Whether to tell your employer about your Crohn's is a personal decision.

**Arguments for disclosure:**
- Legal protection under the ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act) — but only if your employer knows
- Ability to request reasonable accommodations (bathroom proximity, flexible schedule, work from home during flares)
- Less stress from hiding your condition
- Colleagues understand if you need to leave suddenly

**Arguments against:**
- Stigma is real, even though it shouldn't be
- Not all managers are understanding
- You may not want to be defined by your illness at work

**Middle ground:** Disclose to HR (for legal protection) and your direct manager (for practical accommodation) without telling everyone. You control the narrative.

## Exercise and Crohn's

Exercise is one of the best things you can do for Crohn's — when your body allows it.

**Benefits:** Reduces inflammation, improves mood, strengthens bones (important if you've been on steroids), reduces fatigue, and supports gut motility.

**During remission:** Most exercise is safe. Walking, swimming, cycling, yoga, and strength training are all great options. Listen to your body and build gradually.

**During a flare:** Gentle movement only. Walking and stretching are usually manageable. High-intensity exercise can worsen symptoms. Rest when you need to.

**After surgery:** Follow your surgeon's guidance on when to resume exercise. Usually walking is encouraged early, and you'll gradually add intensity over 6-8 weeks.

## Intimacy and Relationships

Nobody talks about this, but they should. Crohn's can affect intimate relationships through:

- **Body image concerns** — scars, weight changes, ostomy bags
- **Fatigue** — when you're exhausted, intimacy feels impossible
- **Bathroom urgency** — fear of accidents during intimate moments
- **Pain** — perianal disease can make certain activities painful

**What helps:**
- Communication with your partner. Honesty reduces anxiety for both of you.
- Timing matters — you may feel better at certain times of day.
- A supportive partner understands that plans may change.
- If perianal disease is affecting intimacy, talk to your GI — treatments exist.

## Building Your Support System

You don't have to do this alone:

- **Your GI team** — gastroenterologist, IBD nurse, dietitian, and possibly a psychologist
- **Support groups** — in-person through Crohn's & Colitis Foundation, or online (Reddit r/CrohnsDisease has 80,000+ members)
- **Trusted friends and family** — even one person who really understands makes a difference
- **This app's community** — connecting with people who truly get it

# Article 12: Understanding Lab Results

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always discuss your lab results with your healthcare provider.*

When your doctor orders blood work and stool tests, the results can look like alphabet soup. Here's what the important ones mean and why they matter for Crohn's.

## Inflammation Markers

### CRP (C-Reactive Protein)
**What it measures:** A protein your liver makes when there's inflammation ANYWHERE in your body.

**Normal range:** Less than 5 mg/L (some labs say less than 10)

**What it means for you:** Higher CRP suggests active inflammation. But it's not specific to your gut — a cold, a skin infection, or even intense exercise can raise it. That's why doctors use it alongside other tests, not alone.

**Tip:** Track your CRP over time in this app. YOUR baseline matters more than the normal range. If your CRP is usually 2 and jumps to 15, that's significant — even though 15 might be "mildly elevated" on paper.

### ESR (Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate)
**What it measures:** How fast your red blood cells settle to the bottom of a tube. Inflammation makes them clump together and fall faster.

**Normal range:** 0-20 mm/hr (varies by age and sex)

**What it means for you:** Like CRP, it's a general inflammation marker. It changes more slowly than CRP — it might stay elevated for weeks after a flare resolves. Less useful for quick monitoring, more useful for tracking trends.

### Fecal Calprotectin
**What it measures:** A protein released by white blood cells in your gut. This is the most important monitoring test for IBD.

**Ranges:**
- Below 50: Very low gut inflammation (likely remission)
- 50-150: Mild elevation (may be okay or may warrant watching)
- 150-250: Moderate — suggests active gut inflammation
- Above 250: High — likely active disease

**Why it matters:** Unlike CRP and ESR, calprotectin is specific to your GUT. It doesn't go up from a common cold or a headache. It tells you what's actually happening inside your intestines. Many doctors now use it to decide whether you need a colonoscopy.

**Tip:** Getting a calprotectin test every 3-6 months (or when symptoms change) is one of the best ways to monitor your Crohn's between colonoscopies.

## Blood Count

### CBC (Complete Blood Count)
This panel gives a snapshot of your overall blood health:

- **Hemoglobin / Hematocrit:** Measures your red blood cells. Low = anemia. Very common in Crohn's due to chronic inflammation, blood loss, and iron/B12 deficiency. Fatigue is the main symptom.
- **White blood cells (WBC):** Measures immune cells. High can mean infection or active inflammation. Low can occur with certain medications (azathioprine, methotrexate) — that's why regular monitoring is required on these drugs.
- **Platelets:** Usually high during active inflammation (your body makes more as part of the inflammatory response). Very high platelets can increase clotting risk — Crohn's patients already have elevated clotting risk during flares.

## Nutritional Markers

### Iron Panel and Ferritin
**What it measures:** Your iron stores and how well you're transporting iron.

- **Ferritin** is the most useful number — it tells you how much iron you have stored. Below 30 is deficient in IBD patients (the standard cutoff of 12 is too low for Crohn's patients with chronic inflammation).
- **Iron (serum):** How much iron is floating in your blood right now. Can fluctuate day to day.
- **TIBC / Transferrin saturation:** How much your blood can carry iron. Helps distinguish between iron deficiency and anemia of chronic disease.

**Why it matters for Crohn's:** Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency in IBD. Your gut may not absorb iron well, and you may be losing blood. If oral iron supplements upset your stomach (they often do), IV iron is an effective alternative.

### Vitamin B12
**Normal range:** 200-900 pg/mL (but symptoms can appear below 400)

**Why it matters:** If your ileum is inflamed or has been removed, you may not absorb B12 properly. Deficiency causes fatigue, numbness/tingling in hands and feet, memory problems, and anemia. It can be subtle and build up over time.

**Test frequency:** Every 3-6 months for the first year after ileal resection, then yearly if stable.

### Folate
**Normal range:** Above 4 ng/mL

**Why it matters:** Folate deficiency can cause a type of anemia similar to B12 deficiency. Methotrexate and sulfasalazine both deplete folate, so supplementation is standard if you're on these drugs.

### Vitamin D
**Normal range:** 30-100 ng/mL. Below 20 is deficient. Below 30 is insufficient.

**Why it matters:** Vitamin D deficiency is extremely common in Crohn's — some studies show up to 70% of patients are deficient. Vitamin D is important for bone health (especially if you've ever been on steroids), immune function, and there's growing evidence it may help control Crohn's inflammation.

## Liver Function Tests

### ALT and AST
**What they measure:** Liver enzymes. Elevated levels can indicate liver stress.

**Why they matter for Crohn's:** Several Crohn's medications can affect the liver — particularly azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine, and methotrexate. Regular monitoring (every 1-3 months) is required while on these drugs.

**Normal range:** ALT 7-56 U/L, AST 10-40 U/L (varies by lab)

## Albumin
**Normal range:** 3.5-5.0 g/dL

**What it means:** Albumin is a protein that reflects your overall nutritional status. Low albumin suggests you're not absorbing enough protein, which is common during flares or after surgery. Very low albumin (below 3.0) is concerning and may affect wound healing.

## Why Trends Matter More Than Single Numbers

A single lab result is a snapshot. What matters more is the direction:

- CRP going from 2 → 8 → 15 over three months tells a story of increasing inflammation
- Ferritin going from 15 → 25 → 40 shows your iron stores are improving
- Calprotectin going from 300 → 150 → 80 shows your treatment is working

That's why this app lets you log lab values over time and visualize the trends. Bring these charts to your doctor appointments — it gives them a much richer picture than any single test.

*Based on ACG clinical guidelines, ECCO laboratory monitoring recommendations, and IBD nutritional assessment literature.*

# Article 13: Crohn's and Pregnancy

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always work closely with both your GI doctor and OB/GYN when planning or during pregnancy.*

If you have Crohn's and are thinking about having a baby — or already pregnant — the good news is that most women with Crohn's have successful pregnancies and healthy babies. The key is planning.

## Before Getting Pregnant: The Most Important Step

The single most important thing you can do is **get your Crohn's into remission BEFORE becoming pregnant.** Women who conceive during remission are much more likely to stay in remission throughout pregnancy. Women who conceive during active disease have a higher risk of flares, premature birth, and low birth weight.

Talk to your GI doctor about pregnancy planning. They can:
- Optimize your medications before conception
- Switch you off any medications that aren't pregnancy-safe
- Make sure your nutritional stores are adequate (folate, iron, B12, vitamin D)
- Time your pregnancy for when your disease is most stable

## Which Medications Are Safe?

This is the question everyone asks. Here's the general guidance — but ALWAYS confirm with your GI:

**Generally considered safe during pregnancy:**
- Aminosalicylates (mesalamine, sulfasalazine) — take extra folic acid with sulfasalazine
- Corticosteroids (prednisone, budesonide) — use at lowest effective dose, avoid in first trimester if possible
- Biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) — growing safety data. Many GIs continue these through pregnancy, sometimes stopping in the third trimester
- Azathioprine / 6-MP — generally continued if needed, despite earlier concerns

**Should be stopped before pregnancy:**
- **Methotrexate** — must be stopped at least 3-6 months before conception. Causes birth defects. This applies to both women AND men.
- **Tofacitinib (Xeljanz) and upadacitinib (Rinvoq)** — limited safety data in pregnancy. Stop before conception.
- **Ozanimod, etrasimod** — stop well before conception.

**Important rule:** The risk of uncontrolled Crohn's during pregnancy is almost always greater than the risk of continuing most medications. A flare during pregnancy is more dangerous to the baby than the medications used to prevent it. Do NOT stop your medications without talking to your GI.

## Nutritional Needs During Pregnancy with Crohn's

All the regular pregnancy nutrition requirements apply, PLUS your Crohn's-specific needs:

- **Folic acid:** 1 mg daily (higher than standard 0.4 mg) — start at least 1 month before conception
- **Iron:** Monitor closely. Pregnancy increases iron needs and Crohn's impairs absorption.
- **B12:** Especially important if you've had ileal resection. Monitor levels each trimester.
- **Vitamin D:** Most GIs recommend 2000-4000 IU daily during pregnancy with Crohn's.
- **Calcium:** 1000 mg daily for bone health of both you and baby.
- **DHA/Omega-3:** Important for fetal brain development. Aim for 200-300 mg DHA daily from fish or supplements.

## Delivery Considerations

Most women with Crohn's can deliver vaginally. However, a C-section may be recommended if you have:
- Active perianal disease (fistulas, abscesses)
- Active rectal disease
- A history of perianal surgery

Discuss delivery planning with both your GI and your obstetrician.

## Breastfeeding with Crohn's Medications

Most Crohn's medications are compatible with breastfeeding. Very little of most biologics passes into breast milk, and what does is likely broken down in the baby's stomach. Aminosalicylates and steroids at low doses are also generally safe.

Methotrexate is NOT safe during breastfeeding.

The benefits of breastfeeding (for both mother and baby) generally outweigh the theoretical risks of medication exposure through breast milk. But discuss your specific medications with your GI and pediatrician.

*Based on ECCO consensus statements on reproduction in IBD, ACG clinical guidelines, and Toronto consensus guidelines for IBD and pregnancy.*

# Article 14: New Research and Emerging Treatments

> *This information is for educational purposes only. Always consult your healthcare provider about treatment options.*

Crohn's disease research is more active than ever. Here's what's happening on the frontier — and what it might mean for you.

## What's New in Medications

### IL-23 Inhibitors — The Latest Class
The newest wave of biologics targets interleukin-23 (IL-23), a specific immune signal that drives Crohn's inflammation:

- **Risankizumab (Skyrizi):** Approved for Crohn's. Strong clinical trial results showing high remission rates. Dosed as injection every 8 weeks after initial IV induction.
- **Guselkumab (Tremfya):** Recently approved for Crohn's (2025). Another IL-23 inhibitor with promising data.
- **Mirikizumab (Omvoh):** Approved for Crohn's in 2025. Originally developed for ulcerative colitis, now expanding to Crohn's.

Why this matters: IL-23 inhibitors appear to be very effective with a favorable safety profile. They're becoming first-line biologic options for many patients.

### JAK Inhibitors — Pills Instead of Injections
- **Upadacitinib (Rinvoq):** Approved for Crohn's. The first oral (pill) advanced therapy for Crohn's. Works fast. Gives patients an alternative to injections and infusions.

### Biosimilars — More Options, Lower Cost
Multiple biosimilars for infliximab, adalimumab, and ustekinumab are now available. This competition is driving prices down and improving access. Seven ustekinumab biosimilars launched in the US in 2025 alone.

## The Microbiome Frontier

### Fecal Microbiota Transplant (FMT)
The idea is simple: transfer healthy gut bacteria from a donor to a Crohn's patient. The results are mixed:
- Strong evidence for treating C. difficile infection (a common complication of Crohn's treatment)
- Mixed evidence for treating Crohn's directly — some patients improve, many don't
- Research is ongoing with better donor selection and standardized protocols

### Defined Bacterial Consortia
Instead of transplanting an entire microbiome, researchers are developing specific combinations of beneficial bacterial strains. These "designer probiotics" can be manufactured consistently and dosed precisely. Several are in clinical trials.

### Phage Therapy
Bacteriophages are viruses that attack specific bacteria. Researchers are exploring whether they can selectively eliminate harmful gut bacteria while leaving beneficial ones intact. Very early stage but scientifically fascinating.

## Diet as Treatment

### CDED (Crohn's Disease Exclusion Diet)
The most exciting dietary research in Crohn's. Clinical trials have shown that CDED can induce remission in some patients, particularly children and young adults. It works by excluding foods that damage the gut barrier and microbiome (processed foods, certain additives, dairy) while including whole foods that support gut health.

The significance: This is one of the first times a dietary intervention has shown measurable reduction in inflammation markers (calprotectin, CRP), not just symptom improvement.

### Personalized Nutrition
Research is moving toward using a patient's individual microbiome profile to predict which foods will help or harm them. We're not there yet for clinical use, but it's the direction the field is heading.

## Personalized Medicine

### Pharmacogenomics
Genetic testing to predict which medications will work best for YOU — and which might cause side effects. Examples:
- TPMT testing before starting azathioprine (identifies patients at risk for dangerous bone marrow suppression)
- HLA-DQA1*05 testing to predict anti-drug antibody development to biologics

### Biomarker-Guided Treatment
Instead of trial-and-error, researchers are developing blood tests and genetic panels that predict which biologic class (anti-TNF vs. anti-integrin vs. anti-IL23) is most likely to work for a specific patient. This could save months of failed treatments.

## Clinical Trials: Should You Consider One?

Clinical trials are how new treatments become available. Participating means:
- Access to cutting-edge treatments before they're publicly available
- Close monitoring by research teams
- Contributing to knowledge that helps future patients
- All study-related care and medication is typically free

Considerations:
- You might receive a placebo (though most modern trials provide active treatment to all participants eventually)
- More frequent visits and tests
- Some risk of unknown side effects

If you're interested, ask your GI doctor about trials at your center, or visit clinicaltrials.gov and search for "Crohn's disease."

## How to Evaluate New Research

Not every headline means a breakthrough. Here's a quick filter:

1. **Where was it published?** Peer-reviewed journals (Lancet, NEJM, Gastroenterology, Gut) are more reliable than press releases or news articles.
2. **How many patients?** A study of 10 people is interesting. A study of 1,000 is meaningful.
3. **Was it randomized and controlled?** This is the gold standard. Observational studies (looking backward at data) are weaker evidence.
4. **Was it in humans?** "Cured Crohn's in mice" is very different from "cured Crohn's in people."
5. **Who funded it?** Drug company-funded research isn't necessarily bad, but know who's paying.

The research feed in this app curates studies from reputable journals and summarizes them in plain language, so you can stay informed without getting overwhelmed.
