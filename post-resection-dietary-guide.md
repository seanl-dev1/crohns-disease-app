# Comprehensive Post-Bowel-Resection Dietary Guide for Crohn's Disease

> **Clinical Disclaimer**: This guide is for educational purposes within a health management app. It does not replace individualized medical or dietetic advice. All dietary changes should be made under the supervision of a gastroenterologist and registered dietitian experienced in IBD.

> **Sources**: Crohn's & Colitis Foundation surgical nutrition guidelines, ESPEN Clinical Nutrition in IBD (2023) and Surgery (2025) guidelines, AGA guidelines, British Society of Gastroenterology guidelines, peer-reviewed literature from PubMed/PMC, and major academic medical center protocols (Mayo Clinic, Memorial Sloan Kettering, Stanford Health Care, Columbia Surgery, UCSF).

---

## 1. RECOVERY PHASES

---

### PHASE 1: CLEAR LIQUIDS (Hospital Days 1-2 Post-Surgery)

**Duration**: 12-48 hours post-surgery (until bowel function returns, typically signaled by passing gas)

**Purpose**: Maintain hydration and electrolyte balance while allowing the bowel to "wake up" from anesthesia and surgical manipulation without any digestive workload.

#### SAFE Foods/Fluids (Specific Items)
| Item | Serving Size | Notes |
|------|-------------|-------|
| Water | Sip freely, 1-2 oz at a time | Room temperature preferred |
| Ice chips | Unlimited | Let melt in mouth |
| Clear broth (chicken, beef, vegetable) | 4-8 oz per serving | Low-sodium if tolerated; fat-free |
| Apple juice (no pulp) | 4 oz per serving | Dilute 50/50 with water initially |
| White grape juice (no pulp) | 4 oz per serving | Dilute 50/50 with water initially |
| Cranberry juice (clear, no pulp) | 4 oz per serving | Dilute 50/50 with water initially |
| Plain gelatin (Jell-O) | 1/2 cup per serving | Avoid red/purple colors (masks blood) |
| Popsicles (no fruit pieces) | 1 bar | Avoid red/purple colors |
| Weak tea (black or herbal) | 4-8 oz | No milk, no caffeine preferred |
| Clear electrolyte drinks | 4-8 oz | Pedialyte, diluted Gatorade |
| Honey (dissolved in warm water) | 1 tsp in 8 oz water | For calories and throat comfort |

#### Foods to AVOID in Phase 1
- ALL solid foods
- Milk and all dairy products
- Orange juice or any juice with pulp
- Carbonated beverages (cause gas and distension)
- Coffee (stimulates motility too aggressively)
- Alcohol
- Anything opaque (if you cannot see through it, it is not a clear liquid)

#### Meal Frequency and Portions
- Sip continuously throughout waking hours
- Start with 1 oz (30 mL) at a time
- Increase to 2-4 oz every 15-30 minutes as tolerated
- Target: at least one 8 oz cup per waking hour
- Total daily intake goal: 1,500-2,000 mL minimum

#### Hydration Requirements
- Minimum 1.5 L/day of clear fluids
- Monitor urine output: target >30 mL/hour (pale yellow urine)
- IV fluids will supplement oral intake during this phase
- Electrolyte replacement via IV as needed

#### Signs You Are Ready for Phase 2
- Passing gas (flatus) -- the single most important sign
- Bowel sounds audible on examination
- No nausea or vomiting for 12+ hours
- Tolerating at least 8 oz/hour of clear liquids without discomfort
- Minimal abdominal distension
- Surgeon approval

#### Common Mistakes in Phase 1
- Drinking too much too fast (causes nausea/vomiting and abdominal distension)
- Using a straw (introduces air, causes bloating)
- Choosing carbonated drinks (painful gas)
- Skipping electrolyte drinks (leads to imbalance)
- Confusing "clear liquid" with "any liquid" (milk, smoothies, etc. are NOT clear liquids)

---

### PHASE 2: FULL LIQUIDS (Hospital Days 2-4)

**Duration**: 1-3 days (typically 24-72 hours after tolerating clear liquids)

**Purpose**: Introduce calories and protein through liquids that require minimal digestion. Goal is 800-1,200 calories/day.

#### SAFE Foods/Fluids (Specific Items)
| Item | Serving Size | Notes |
|------|-------------|-------|
| ALL clear liquid items above | As above | Continue these |
| Strained cream soups (no chunks) | 6-8 oz | Cream of chicken, mushroom, tomato -- strained smooth |
| Smooth yogurt (no fruit pieces) | 4-6 oz | Plain or vanilla; Greek yogurt thinned if needed |
| Milk (skim or 1%) | 4-8 oz | Only if no lactose intolerance; try lactose-free first |
| Lactose-free milk | 4-8 oz | Preferred over regular milk post-surgery |
| Protein shakes (strained, no fiber) | 8 oz | Ensure Clear, Boost Breeze, or similar |
| Custard/pudding (smooth) | 1/2 cup | Store-bought or homemade |
| Ice cream/sherbet/sorbet | 1/2 cup | Plain flavors, no nuts/chunks |
| Cream of Wheat or Cream of Rice | 1/2 cup | Cooked thin, smooth consistency |
| Mashed potatoes (very thin/soupy) | 1/2 cup | Made with broth, no chunks |
| Strained fruit purees | 1/4 cup | Applesauce, baby food consistency |
| Egg drop in broth | 4-8 oz | Whisked egg cooked in hot broth |

#### Foods to AVOID in Phase 2
- Any food with chunks, pieces, seeds, or fiber
- Raw fruits or vegetables
- Bread, crackers, or any solid grain
- Meat (even ground)
- Nuts, seeds, or nut butters
- Beans or legumes
- Spicy seasonings
- Fried foods
- High-fat items (cream sauces, butter in excess)

#### Meal Frequency and Portions
- 6-8 small meals/snacks per day
- 4-8 oz per "meal" initially
- Every 2-3 hours during waking hours
- Stop eating when you feel any fullness -- do not push

#### Hydration Requirements
- Continue minimum 1.5-2 L/day
- Separate liquids from "meals" by 30 minutes when possible
- Include electrolyte drinks: at least 16-24 oz/day
- Monitor urine color (pale yellow = adequate)

#### Signs You Are Ready for Phase 3
- Tolerating full liquid meals without nausea, bloating, or pain
- Having bowel movements (even if loose, this shows motility)
- No vomiting
- Able to consume 800+ calories/day from liquids
- Surgeon/dietitian approval for soft foods

#### Common Mistakes in Phase 2
- Jumping straight to solid food because you "feel fine"
- Drinking large volumes at once instead of sipping
- Choosing high-sugar protein drinks (can cause osmotic diarrhea)
- Not tracking caloric intake (many patients severely undereat)
- Skipping protein drinks (delays wound healing)

### PHASE 3: LOW-RESIDUE/LOW-FIBER DIET (Weeks 1-4 Post-Surgery)

**Duration**: 2-6 weeks from when soft foods are introduced (typically starting day 3-5 post-op, continuing 4-6 weeks total)

**Purpose**: Provide adequate calories (1,500-2,000/day) and protein (60-80 g/day) for wound healing while minimizing residue that could stress the anastomosis (surgical join).

#### SAFE Foods (Specific Items)

| Skinless turkey breast | 3-4 oz | Ground or thinly sliced deli |
| White fish (cod, tilapia, sole, haddock) | 3-4 oz | Baked or steamed, no breading |
| Canned tuna (in water) | 3 oz | Drained, plain or with small amount of mayo |
| Salmon (canned or fresh) | 3 oz | Baked, flaked |
| Tofu (silken or soft) | 1/2 cup | Steamed or in soup |
| Smooth peanut butter | 1-2 tbsp | Creamy only, no chunks |
| Cottage cheese (small curd) | 1/2 cup | Low-fat preferred |
| Ricotta cheese | 1/4 cup | Smooth |

**Grains and Starches**

**Vegetables (ALL must be well-cooked, peeled, seedless)**

#### Foods to AVOID in Phase 3
| Category | Specific Items |
|----------|---------------|
| High-fiber grains | Whole wheat bread, brown rice, bran cereal, oatmeal, granola, popcorn |
| Raw vegetables | Salads, raw carrots, celery, bell peppers, broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage |
| High-residue vegetables | Corn, peas, Brussels sprouts, onions (raw), dried beans, lentils |
| Skins and seeds | Tomato skins/seeds, potato skins, apple skin, grape skin, berry seeds |
| Nuts and seeds | All nuts, sunflower seeds, sesame seeds, chia seeds, flax seeds |
| Tough meats | Steak, pork chops, sausage, bacon, hot dogs, jerky |
| Dried fruits | Raisins, cranberries, dates, figs, prunes |
| Raw fruits | Apples with skin, oranges, grapefruits, pineapple, coconut |
| Spicy foods | Hot sauce, chili peppers, curry, black pepper (large amounts) |
| Fried foods | French fries, fried chicken, donuts, chips |
| Gas-producing foods | Broccoli, cabbage, beans, onions, carbonated drinks |
| Tough/stringy | Celery, rhubarb, asparagus stalks, bean sprouts |

#### Meal Frequency and Portions
- 5-6 small meals per day (every 3 hours)
- Each meal: approximately 1 cup total volume
- Protein at every meal: minimum 10-15 g per meal
- Daily targets: 1,500-2,000 calories, 60-80 g protein
- Chew all food thoroughly (30 chews per bite)
- Eat slowly (20-30 minutes per meal)

#### Hydration Requirements
- 8-10 cups (2-2.5 L) of fluid daily
- Sip fluids between meals (not with meals, to avoid dumping-like symptoms)
- Include oral rehydration solution if high stool output (>1 L/day)
- Electrolyte drinks: 2-3 servings per day
- Avoid ice-cold beverages (can trigger cramping)

#### Signs You Are Ready for Phase 4
- Consistent formed or semi-formed stools
- No abdominal pain after meals
- Maintaining or gaining weight
- Tolerating all Phase 3 foods without symptoms
- 4+ weeks post-surgery
- Follow-up with surgeon confirms healing

#### Common Mistakes in Phase 3
- Reintroducing fiber too quickly (causes obstruction risk at anastomosis)
- Eating raw salads because they seem "healthy"
- Not eating enough calories (weight loss delays healing)
- Skipping meals (leads to large meals, which are harder to digest)
- Not chewing thoroughly (undigested food can cause blockages)
- Drinking large amounts of fluid with meals (causes rapid transit)
- Eating popcorn, nuts, or seeds prematurely

### PHASE 4: GRADUAL REINTRODUCTION (Months 2-3 Post-Surgery)

**Duration**: 4-8 weeks (approximately weeks 5-12 post-surgery)

**Purpose**: Systematically reintroduce higher-fiber and more complex foods to expand diet variety while identifying individual trigger foods. Goal is to work toward a nutritionally complete diet.

#### Reintroduction Protocol
1. Add ONE new food per day
2. Start with a small portion (2-3 tablespoons)
3. Eat it early in the day (so you can monitor reactions)
4. Wait 48 hours before adding the next new food
5. If a food causes symptoms, remove it and retry in 2-4 weeks
6. Keep a detailed food diary

#### Foods to Reintroduce (In Order of Priority, Safest First)

**Week 5-6: Soft Cooked Vegetables and Gentle Proteins**
| Food | How to Prepare | Watch For |
|------|---------------|-----------|
| Soft-cooked broccoli florets (no stems) | Steamed 10+ min until very soft | Gas, bloating |
| Soft-cooked cauliflower | Steamed/boiled until mashable | Gas, bloating |
| Cooked onions | Sauteed until translucent, small amount | Cramping |
| Ground beef (lean, 90%+) | Well-cooked, drained | Diarrhea, cramping |
| Pork tenderloin | Slow-cooked until very tender | Cramping |
| Oatmeal (instant, well-cooked) | Thin consistency | Bloating |

**Week 7-8: Cooked Fruits and Whole Grains**
| Food | How to Prepare | Watch For |
|------|---------------|-----------|
| Cooked apple (no skin) | Baked/stewed, peeled | Gas |
| Soft pear (very ripe, no skin) | Peeled, in small pieces | Loose stool |
| Whole wheat bread (1 slice) | Toasted | Bloating, cramping |
| Brown rice | Well-cooked, small portion (1/4 cup) | Bloating |
| Cooked lentils (small portion) | Well-cooked, pureed or mashed | Gas, diarrhea |
| Yogurt with fruit pieces | Small pieces, well-stirred | Diarrhea |

**Week 9-10: Raw Soft Fruits and Tender Vegetables**
| Food | How to Prepare | Watch For |
|------|---------------|-----------|
| Ripe peeled peach | Fresh, peeled, small pieces | Loose stool |
| Ripe peeled pear | Fresh, peeled, small pieces | Loose stool |
| Cucumber (peeled, seeded) | Peeled, seeds removed, small pieces | Cramping |
| Lettuce (tender varieties: butter, Boston) | Small amount, chew well | Bloating, pain |
| Tomato (peeled, seeded) | Blanched, skin/seeds removed | Diarrhea, acid reflux |
| Mild cheese (more varieties) | Small portions | Diarrhea if lactose issue |

**Week 11-12: Higher Risk Foods**
| Food | How to Prepare | Watch For |
|------|---------------|-----------|
| Small amounts of nuts (chewed very well) | Almond butter first, then whole almonds (6-8) | Obstruction symptoms, pain |
| Seeds (ground first) | Ground flax or chia in yogurt (1 tsp) | Bloating |
| Dried fruit (small amount) | 2-3 pieces, chopped fine | Cramping, diarrhea |
| Raw apple (with skin) | Chew extremely well, small pieces | Pain, bloating |
| Beans (small portion) | Well-cooked, start with 2 tbsp | Gas, diarrhea |
| Corn (small amount) | Well-cooked, off the cob | Obstruction risk |

#### Meal Frequency and Portions
- Maintain 5-6 small meals per day
- New foods: start at 2-3 tablespoons, increase to 1/2 cup if tolerated
- Continue protein at every meal
- Daily targets: 1,800-2,200 calories, 60-80 g protein

#### Hydration Requirements
- 8-10 cups (2-2.5 L) per day
- Continue oral rehydration if stool output high
- Electrolyte monitoring ongoing

#### Signs You Are Ready for Phase 5
- Tolerating most foods from the reintroduction list
- Stable weight (maintaining or appropriately gaining)
- Regular bowel pattern (even if different from pre-surgery)
- Good energy levels
- Lab work stable (B12, iron, vitamin D, electrolytes)

#### Common Mistakes in Phase 4
- Reintroducing multiple foods at once (cannot identify triggers)
- Not keeping a food diary (loses critical data)
- Giving up on a food after one bad attempt (may have been a bad day)
- Not retrying foods that caused mild symptoms
- Eating large portions of new foods immediately

### PHASE 5: LONG-TERM MAINTENANCE (Month 4 Onward)

**Duration**: Lifelong

**Purpose**: Maintain optimal nutrition while managing the permanent changes from bowel resection. This phase is about establishing a sustainable, individualized diet that maximizes nutrient absorption from the remaining bowel.

#### Core Dietary Principles

1. **Eat 5-6 smaller meals** rather than 3 large ones (reduces malabsorption)
2. **Chew thoroughly** -- especially important with reduced bowel length
3. **Maintain a food diary** for ongoing trigger identification
4. **Prioritize nutrient-dense foods** to compensate for reduced absorption
5. **Stay consistently hydrated** with electrolyte-enhanced fluids
6. **Take prescribed supplements** without fail (see Section 2)
7. **Annual dietitian review** to adjust plan as bowel adapts

#### Foods Most Patients Can Eat Long-Term
- Well-cooked vegetables (expanding variety over time)
- Lean proteins (chicken, fish, turkey, eggs, tofu)
- White and whole grain rice/pasta (as tolerated)
- Ripe fruits (bananas, melons, peeled soft fruits)
- Yogurt and lactose-free dairy
- Smooth nut butters
- Healthy fats in moderation (olive oil, avocado)
- Bone broth (excellent for gut healing)

#### Foods That May Remain Permanent Triggers
These vary by individual, but common permanent restrictions after resection include:
- Popcorn
- Corn on the cob
- Raw coconut
- Whole nuts in large amounts
- Dried fruit in large amounts
- Very high-fat meals
- Very spicy foods
- Large quantities of insoluble fiber at once
- Carbonated sugary drinks

#### Meal Frequency and Portions
- 5-6 meals/day, 300-400 calories each
- Protein: 1.0-1.5 g/kg body weight/day (higher than general population)
- Fat: adjusted based on resection type (see Section 4)
- Fiber: gradually increase to 15-25 g/day as tolerated (may be lower than general population recommendations)

#### Hydration Requirements
- Minimum 2-3 L/day
- More if high stool output, hot weather, or exercise
- Include electrolyte sources daily
- Oral rehydration solution if >1 L stool output/day
- Monitor urine (pale yellow = adequate, dark = dehydrated)

#### Long-Term Monitoring Schedule
| Test | Frequency | Purpose |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Weight | Weekly at home | Detect malnutrition early |
| Food diary | Ongoing | Trigger identification |
| Stool output | Monitor pattern | Detect changes |
| GI follow-up | Every 3-6 months first year, then annually | Overall assessment |
| Nutritional labs | Every 6-12 months (see Section 2) | Detect deficiencies |

#### Common Mistakes in Phase 5
- Abandoning the food diary after feeling "recovered"
- Stopping supplements because "I feel fine"
- Reverting to pre-surgery eating habits (large meals, fast eating)
- Not adjusting diet seasonally (dehydration risk in summer)
- Ignoring subtle symptoms of new food intolerances
- Not keeping up with lab work

## 2. RESECTION-SPECIFIC NUTRITIONAL NEEDS

---

### VITAMIN B12 (Cobalamin)

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Vitamin B12 is absorbed exclusively in the terminal ileum via intrinsic factor-cobalamin complex receptors. No other part of the intestine can absorb B12. When the terminal ileum is removed, this absorption mechanism is permanently lost.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Ileal resection >60 cm**: Virtually guaranteed B12 deficiency; lifelong supplementation mandatory
- **Ileal resection 20-60 cm**: High risk; requires monitoring and likely supplementation
- **Ileal resection <20 cm**: Lower risk but annual monitoring recommended
- **Colonic resection only**: Minimal direct effect on B12 absorption
- **Ileocecal resection**: High risk (includes terminal ileum)

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Fatigue and weakness
- Tingling/numbness in hands and feet (peripheral neuropathy)
- Difficulty walking and balance problems
- Glossitis (swollen, inflamed tongue)
- Cognitive difficulties (memory problems, confusion)
- Depression and mood changes
- Megaloblastic anemia (large, immature red blood cells)
- Irreversible nerve damage if prolonged

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- Oral: 1,000-2,000 mcg/day sublingual methylcobalamin (if any ileum remains)
- Intramuscular injection: 1,000 mcg every 1-3 months (required if >60 cm ileum removed, as oral cannot be absorbed)
- Initial loading: 1,000 mcg IM daily or every other day for 1-2 weeks, then monthly

**Best Food Sources** (limited value post-ileal resection due to absorption issues)
- Clams (84 mcg per 3 oz)
- Liver (beef, 70 mcg per 3 oz)
- Fortified nutritional yeast (varies by brand)
- Salmon (4.8 mcg per 3 oz)
- Tuna (2.5 mcg per 3 oz)
- Fortified cereals (varies)
- Eggs (0.6 mcg per egg)

**Supplement Form**
- **Methylcobalamin** (sublingual): Preferred form; already in active/bioavailable form; dissolves under tongue, partially bypassing gut
- **Cyanocobalamin** (IM injection): Standard for injections; reliable when oral absorption is impossible
- **Hydroxocobalamin** (IM injection): Longer-acting than cyanocobalamin; preferred in some countries

**Lab Testing**
| Test | Normal Range | Deficient | Frequency |
|------|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| Serum B12 | 200-900 pg/mL (148-664 pmol/L) | <200 pg/mL | Every 6-12 months |
| Methylmalonic acid (MMA) | 70-270 nmol/L | >270 nmol/L (more sensitive) | If B12 borderline |
| Homocysteine | 5-15 umol/L | >15 umol/L (elevated in B12 deficiency) | If B12 borderline |
| Complete blood count | Normal MCV 80-100 fL | MCV >100 fL (macrocytosis) | Every 6-12 months |

### BILE ACIDS (Bile Acid Malabsorption)

**Why Resection Causes the Problem**
Bile acids are synthesized in the liver, secreted into the small intestine to digest fats, and then reabsorbed in the terminal ileum to be recycled (enterohepatic circulation). When the ileum is removed, bile acids pass into the colon unabsorbed.

**Two Distinct Scenarios Based on Resection Length**
1. **Ileal resection <100 cm**: Bile acid loss is moderate; the liver can compensate by making more bile acids. However, the unabsorbed bile acids reaching the colon cause **secretory diarrhea** (bile acid diarrhea). Fat digestion is largely preserved.
2. **Ileal resection >100 cm**: Bile acid loss exceeds the liver's synthetic capacity. The total bile acid pool is depleted, causing **fat malabsorption (steatorrhea)**. Diarrhea is from undigested fat, not bile acids.

**Symptoms**
- Watery, urgent diarrhea (especially after fatty meals)
- Steatorrhea (pale, greasy, foul-smelling stool that floats)
- Abdominal cramping
- Urgency and fecal incontinence
- Weight loss
- Fat-soluble vitamin deficiency (A, D, E, K)

**Dietary Management**
- **For <100 cm resection (bile acid diarrhea)**: Low-fat diet is NOT necessary; instead, use bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine 4-16 g/day). Dietary fat can be normal.
- **For >100 cm resection (fat malabsorption)**: Reduce dietary fat to <20% of total calories. Replace long-chain fats with MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) oil, which does not require bile acids for absorption.

**Medications**
| Medication | Dose | When to Use |
|------------|------|-------------|
| Cholestyramine (Questran) | 4 g packet, 1-4 times daily | Resection <100 cm; bile acid diarrhea |
| Colesevelam (Welchol) | 625 mg tablets, 3 twice daily | Alternative to cholestyramine if poorly tolerated |
| Colestipol (Colestid) | 2-16 g/day | Alternative bile acid sequestrant |

**Important**: Bile acid sequestrants are NOT recommended for resections >100 cm -- they worsen fat malabsorption. Take sequestrants 1 hour before or 4 hours after other medications (they bind many drugs).

**Lab Testing**
| Test | Purpose | Frequency |
|------|---------|-----------|
| SeHCAT scan (75Se-HCAT) | Gold standard for bile acid malabsorption | Once for diagnosis |
| Fecal fat (72-hour collection) | Quantify fat malabsorption | At baseline, then as needed |
| Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) | Detect secondary deficiencies | Every 6-12 months |

### VITAMIN D

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin absorbed in the jejunum and ileum along with dietary fat. Fat malabsorption from bile acid depletion directly reduces vitamin D absorption. Additionally, Crohn's inflammation impairs absorption, and many patients have limited sun exposure during recovery.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Ileal resection (any length)**: Risk increases with length; bile acid loss impairs fat absorption
- **Extensive small bowel resection**: Reduced absorptive surface
- **Colonic resection**: Lower direct risk but may affect through altered transit
- **Ileocecal resection**: Significant risk

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Bone pain and muscle weakness
- Fatigue
- Increased fracture risk (osteoporosis/osteopenia)
- Depression and mood changes
- Impaired immune function
- Increased Crohn's disease flare risk

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- Maintenance: 2,000-4,000 IU (50-100 mcg) vitamin D3 daily
- If deficient (<20 ng/mL): 6,000 IU/day or 50,000 IU weekly for 8-12 weeks, then recheck
- Upper safe limit: 10,000 IU/day
- Some post-resection patients need 4,000-10,000 IU/day to maintain adequate levels

**Best Food Sources**
- Fatty fish: salmon (570 IU/3 oz), mackerel (360 IU/3 oz), sardines (165 IU/3 oz)
- Cod liver oil (1,360 IU/tbsp)
- Fortified milk (120 IU/cup)
- Fortified orange juice (100 IU/cup)
- Egg yolks (44 IU per yolk)
- Fortified cereals (40-100 IU/serving)
- UV-exposed mushrooms (400 IU/cup)

**Supplement Form**
- **Cholecalciferol (D3)**: Preferred over ergocalciferol (D2); more effective at raising serum levels
- **Liquid or chewable forms**: Better absorbed post-resection than tablets
- **Take with a fat-containing meal** for best absorption
- **Calcitriol (active vitamin D)**: May be needed if kidneys also impaired

**Lab Testing**
| Test | Normal Range | Insufficient | Deficient | Frequency |
|------|-------------|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] | 30-100 ng/mL (75-250 nmol/L) | 20-29 ng/mL | <20 ng/mL | Every 6-12 months; more often if supplementing to correct deficiency |

### VITAMIN A

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Vitamin A is fat-soluble, absorbed primarily in the jejunum and ileum with dietary fat. Fat malabsorption from bile acid depletion reduces vitamin A absorption. The liver stores vitamin A, so deficiency may take months to years to manifest.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Ileal resection >100 cm**: Significant risk (bile acid pool depletion causes fat malabsorption)
- **Extensive jejunal resection**: Direct loss of absorption site
- **Short bowel syndrome**: High risk

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Night blindness (earliest symptom)
- Dry eyes (xerophthalmia)
- Dry, rough skin
- Impaired immune function (frequent infections)
- Poor wound healing

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- RDA: 900 mcg RAE (3,000 IU) for men; 700 mcg RAE (2,300 IU) for women
- Post-resection with fat malabsorption: may need 10,000-25,000 IU/day under supervision
- **Caution**: Vitamin A is toxic in excess; do NOT exceed supervised doses

**Best Food Sources**
- Sweet potato (1,096 mcg RAE per medium)
- Beef liver (6,582 mcg RAE per 3 oz)
- Carrots (459 mcg RAE per 1/2 cup cooked)
- Spinach (472 mcg RAE per 1/2 cup cooked)
- Cantaloupe (135 mcg RAE per 1/2 cup)
- Fortified milk (149 mcg RAE per cup)
- Eggs (75 mcg RAE per egg)

**Supplement Form**
- **Retinyl palmitate**: Standard supplemental form

### VITAMIN E

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Vitamin E is fat-soluble, requiring bile acids and dietary fat for absorption in the small intestine. Fat malabsorption significantly reduces vitamin E uptake.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Ileal resection >100 cm**: Depleted bile acid pool causes fat malabsorption
- **Extensive small bowel resection**: Reduced absorptive surface
- **Short bowel syndrome**: High risk

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Peripheral neuropathy (tingling, numbness)
- Muscle weakness
- Impaired immune function
- Retinopathy
- Ataxia (loss of coordination)

### VITAMIN K

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) is absorbed in the small intestine with dietary fat. Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) is produced by bacteria in the colon and absorbed there. Resection can disrupt both pathways. Additionally, bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine) can bind vitamin K.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Ileal resection**: Impairs K1 absorption through fat malabsorption
- **Colonic resection**: Reduces K2 production by gut bacteria
- **Ileocecal resection**: Affects both pathways
- **Patients on cholestyramine**: Additional binding of vitamin K

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Easy bruising
- Excessive bleeding from cuts
- Nosebleeds
- Heavy menstrual periods
- Blood in urine or stool
- Prolonged clotting time

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- RDA: 120 mcg/day (men), 90 mcg/day (women)
- Post-resection: 1,000-10,000 mcg/day may be needed with severe fat malabsorption
- Oral supplementation: 1-10 mg/day phylloquinone
- Emergency/severe deficiency: 10 mg IV or IM

**Best Food Sources**
- Kale (1 cup cooked: 1,062 mcg)
- Spinach (1/2 cup cooked: 444 mcg)
- Broccoli (1/2 cup cooked: 110 mcg)
- Brussels sprouts (1/2 cup cooked: 109 mcg)
- Green beans (1/2 cup: 30 mcg)
- Natto (fermented soybeans, rich in K2: 1,000+ mcg per 3.5 oz)
- Note: Cook green vegetables well for post-resection patients

**Supplement Form**
- **Phylloquinone (K1)**: Standard supplement form; requires fat for absorption
- **Menaquinone-7 (MK-7)**: K2 form; longer half-life, may be better for maintaining consistent levels
- **Water-soluble forms**: Available for fat malabsorption
- **Menadione (K3)**: Synthetic; can be toxic, generally avoided

**Lab Testing**
| Test | Normal Range | Deficient | Frequency |
|------|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| PT/INR | PT 11-13.5 seconds; INR 0.8-1.1 | PT >13.5 seconds; INR >1.1 | Every 6-12 months |
| Serum phylloquinone | 0.2-3.2 ng/mL | <0.2 ng/mL | If clinical signs present |
| PIVKA-II (protein induced by vitamin K absence) | <2 ng/mL | >2 ng/mL | Research setting; more sensitive |

### IRON (and Ferritin)

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Iron is primarily absorbed in the duodenum and proximal jejunum. While most resections for Crohn's spare this area, chronic inflammation, blood loss from the disease, and reduced dietary intake all contribute. Iron deficiency anemia affects 36-76% of IBD patients.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Duodenal/proximal jejunal disease or resection**: Direct absorption loss (rare in Crohn's)
- **Ileal resection**: Indirect effect through reduced dietary intake and ongoing inflammation
- **Any resection**: Blood loss from surgery, reduced appetite, dietary restrictions
- **Colonic resection**: May reduce non-heme iron absorption

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Fatigue and weakness (most common)
- Pale skin
- Shortness of breath
- Dizziness
- Brittle nails (koilonychia)
- Cold hands and feet
- Restless legs
- Pica (craving non-food items like ice)

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- Prophylaxis: 30 mg elemental iron/day
- Treatment of deficiency: 50-100 mg elemental iron/day
- IV iron (if oral intolerant or Hb <10 g/dL): Ferric carboxymaltose (Injectafer) 750 mg IV, repeated after 7 days (total 1,500 mg)
- Take oral iron with vitamin C (100-200 mg) to enhance absorption
- Take on empty stomach if tolerated; with food if not (reduces absorption by ~40% but improves tolerance)

**Best Food Sources**
- Beef liver (5 mg per 3 oz)
- Beef (2.5 mg per 3 oz)
- Chicken liver (8 mg per 3 oz)
- Fortified cereals (18 mg per serving)
- Lentils (3.3 mg per 1/2 cup, when tolerated)
- Spinach (3.2 mg per 1/2 cup cooked)
- Tofu (3 mg per 1/2 cup)
- Dark chocolate (3.4 mg per 1 oz)
- Eggs (0.6 mg per egg)

**Supplement Forms**
- **Ferrous sulfate**: Most common; 20% elemental iron (325 mg tablet = 65 mg elemental iron)
- **Ferrous fumarate**: 33% elemental iron; higher iron content per tablet
- **Ferrous gluconate**: 12% elemental iron; gentler on stomach
- **Ferric maltol (Accrufer)**: Newer oral form; better tolerated in IBD
- **Ferric carboxymaltose (Injectafer)**: IV; best for severe deficiency or oral intolerance
- **Iron sucrose (Venofer)**: IV alternative
- **Note**: Do NOT take oral iron within 2 hours of antacids, calcium, or cholestyramine

**Lab Testing**
| Test | Normal Range | Deficient | Iron Deficiency WITH Inflammation | Frequency |
|------|-------------|-----------|----------------------------------|-----------|
| Ferritin | 30-300 ng/mL | <30 ng/mL | <100 ng/mL (inflammation raises ferritin falsely) | Every 6-12 months |
| Transferrin saturation (TSAT) | 20-50% | <20% | <20% | Every 6-12 months |
| Hemoglobin (Hb) | Men: 13-17 g/dL; Women: 12-16 g/dL | Men <13; Women <12 | Same | Every 6-12 months |
| Serum iron | 60-170 mcg/dL | <60 mcg/dL | Variable | As needed |
| TIBC | 250-370 mcg/dL | >370 mcg/dL | May be normal | As needed |
| CRP/ESR | CRP <5 mg/L | N/A | Elevated = use ferritin <100 cutoff | With iron studies |

### CALCIUM

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Calcium absorption depends on vitamin D and occurs throughout the small intestine, with highest efficiency in the duodenum and proximal jejunum. Fat malabsorption causes calcium to bind free fatty acids instead of being absorbed. Additionally, in patients with colon intact, unabsorbed fatty acids bind calcium in the gut, leaving oxalate free to be absorbed (increasing kidney stone risk).

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Ileal resection**: Indirect (via vitamin D deficiency and fat malabsorption)
- **Extensive small bowel resection**: Reduced absorptive surface
- **Patients without colon**: Calcium absorption is 10% (ileostomy) vs. 14% (colon intact)
- **Patients on steroids**: Accelerated calcium loss

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Muscle cramps and spasms
- Tingling in fingers and lips
- Osteopenia and osteoporosis
- Increased fracture risk
- Dental problems

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- 1,000-1,500 mg/day from diet and supplements combined
- Take in divided doses: 500 mg at a time (maximum absorption per dose)
- Always take with vitamin D
- Take with meals for best absorption

**Best Food Sources**
- Yogurt (415 mg per 8 oz)
- Milk (300 mg per 8 oz)
- Cheddar cheese (200 mg per 1 oz)
- Fortified orange juice (350 mg per 8 oz)
- Canned sardines with bones (325 mg per 3 oz)
- Tofu (calcium-set, 250 mg per 1/2 cup)
- Fortified cereals (100-1,000 mg per serving)
- Cooked kale (95 mg per 1/2 cup)

**Supplement Form**
- **Calcium citrate**: PREFERRED for post-resection patients; 22-27% better absorbed than carbonate; does not require stomach acid; can be taken with or without food
- **Calcium carbonate**: Cheaper but requires stomach acid for absorption; must take with food; may cause constipation and gas
- **Do NOT use calcium carbonate** if taking PPIs or H2 blockers (reduced stomach acid)
- Take calcium SEPARATE from iron supplements (2 hours apart)

**Lab Testing**
| Test | Normal Range | Deficient | Frequency |
|------|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| Serum calcium (total) | 8.5-10.5 mg/dL (2.1-2.6 mmol/L) | <8.5 mg/dL | Every 6-12 months |
| Ionized calcium | 4.5-5.3 mg/dL (1.12-1.32 mmol/L) | <4.5 mg/dL | If total calcium abnormal |
| 25(OH) vitamin D | 30-100 ng/mL | <20 ng/mL | With calcium testing |
| DEXA scan (bone density) | T-score >-1.0 | Osteopenia: -1.0 to -2.5; Osteoporosis: <-2.5 | Baseline post-surgery, then every 2 years |
| PTH (parathyroid hormone) | 10-65 pg/mL | Elevated if calcium low | If calcium persistently low |

### ZINC

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Zinc is absorbed throughout the small intestine, with highest absorption in the jejunum. Chronic diarrhea (common post-resection) causes significant zinc losses. Zinc is also lost in fistula drainage and high ostomy output. Approximately 15% of IBD patients are zinc deficient.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Jejunal resection**: Direct absorption loss
- **Any resection with chronic diarrhea**: Major zinc losses in stool
- **Short bowel syndrome**: High risk
- **Patients with fistulae or high ostomy output**: Increased losses

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Poor wound healing (critical post-surgery)
- Hair loss
- Skin rashes (perioral and acral dermatitis)
- Taste changes (dysgeusia)
- Loss of appetite
- Impaired immune function
- Diarrhea (creates a vicious cycle)
- Night blindness

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- RDA: 11 mg/day (men), 8 mg/day (women)
- Post-resection maintenance: 15-25 mg/day elemental zinc
- With high stool output: 12 mg zinc per liter of stool/ostomy output (in addition to baseline)
- Treatment of deficiency: 40 mg/day for 2-3 months
- Upper safe limit: 40 mg/day (higher doses need monitoring for copper depletion)

**Best Food Sources**
- Oysters (74 mg per 3 oz)
- Beef (5.3 mg per 3 oz)
- Crab (6.5 mg per 3 oz)
- Chicken (2.4 mg per 3 oz)
- Pork (2.9 mg per 3 oz)
- Yogurt (1.7 mg per 8 oz)
- Cashews (1.6 mg per 1 oz)
- Pumpkin seeds (2.2 mg per 1 oz -- when tolerated)

**Supplement Form**
- **Zinc gluconate**: 13% elemental zinc; gentle on stomach
- **Zinc sulfate**: 23% elemental zinc; most studied; can cause nausea
- **Zinc picolinate**: Better absorbed; gentler
- **Zinc carnosine**: May have additional gut-healing properties
- **Take on empty stomach** if tolerated, or with a small protein-containing meal
- **Separate from iron and calcium** by 2 hours

**Lab Testing**

| Note: Serum zinc is insensitive; can be normal despite tissue depletion. Clinical symptoms may be more reliable. | | | |

### MAGNESIUM

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
Magnesium is absorbed in the ileum and colon. Chronic diarrhea and high stool output are the primary causes of deficiency post-resection. Proton pump inhibitors (commonly used in IBD) also reduce magnesium absorption.

**Which Resection Causes It**

- Fatigue
- Nausea

### FOLATE / FOLIC ACID

### POTASSIUM

### SODIUM (Electrolyte Balance)

**Why Resection Causes Deficiency**
The small intestine absorbs the majority of dietary sodium. After resection, particularly with high-output ileostomy or jejunostomy, sodium losses can be dramatic -- stool/ostomy output can contain 80-105 mEq sodium per liter. Patients are often told to restrict sodium for heart health, which is dangerous after bowel resection.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **High-output ileostomy/jejunostomy**: Highest risk
- **Extensive small bowel resection**: Major absorptive loss
- **Short bowel syndrome**: Critical risk
- **Any resection with chronic high-volume diarrhea**: Significant losses

**Symptoms of Deficiency (Hyponatremia)**
- Confusion and lethargy
- Nausea and vomiting
- Headache
- Muscle cramps
- Seizures (severe)
- Low blood pressure
- Dizziness and falls

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- General population: <2,300 mg/day (restriction)
- Post-resection with high output: 6,000-9,000 mg/day or MORE (opposite of general advice)
- Add salt liberally to food
- Use oral rehydration solutions containing 90-120 mEq/L sodium
- SBS patients: may need salt tablets (1 g NaCl = 17 mEq sodium)

**Best Food Sources**
- Add table salt to foods
- Salty broths and soups
- Pretzels and saltine crackers
- Pickles and olive brine
- Cheese
- Soy sauce (in moderation)
- Oral rehydration solutions (see Section 3)

**Monitoring**
- 24-hour urinary sodium: <20 mEq/L indicates sodium depletion
- Target urinary sodium: >20 mEq/L
- Serum sodium: 136-145 mEq/L (normal range)

**Lab Testing**
| Test | Normal Range | Deficient | Frequency |
|------|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| Serum sodium | 136-145 mEq/L | <136 mEq/L | Every 3-6 months; more often if high-output stoma |
| 24-hour urine sodium | >20 mEq/L | <20 mEq/L (sodium depletion) | Monthly if high output; otherwise every 6 months |
| Urine specific gravity | 1.005-1.030 | >1.030 (concentrated, dehydrated) | With urine sodium |

### ESSENTIAL FATTY ACIDS (Omega-3)

**Why Resection May Cause Deficiency**
Essential fatty acids (linoleic acid/omega-6 and alpha-linolenic acid/omega-3) require bile acids for absorption. Fat malabsorption after ileal resection reduces uptake. Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA) have anti-inflammatory properties relevant to Crohn's disease.

**Which Resection Causes It**
- **Ileal resection >100 cm**: Bile acid depletion causes fat malabsorption
- **Short bowel syndrome**: Reduced absorptive surface
- **Patients on very low-fat diets**: May become deficient

**Symptoms of Deficiency**
- Dry, scaly skin
- Poor wound healing
- Increased inflammation
- Hair loss
- Impaired immune function

**Recommended Daily Intake Post-Resection**
- Omega-3 (ALA): 1.6 g/day (men), 1.1 g/day (women)
- EPA + DHA combined: 500-2,000 mg/day
- Anti-inflammatory benefit: 2-4 g/day EPA+DHA (study dose)
- Start at 500 mg/day and increase gradually (high doses can worsen diarrhea)
- Evidence for Crohn's remission maintenance: mixed; some benefit seen with enteric-coated, time-released formulations

**Best Food Sources**
- Salmon (1.5-2 g EPA+DHA per 3 oz)
- Mackerel (1 g EPA+DHA per 3 oz)
- Sardines (0.9 g EPA+DHA per 3 oz)
- Walnuts (2.5 g ALA per 1 oz -- when tolerated)
- Flaxseed oil (7.3 g ALA per tbsp)
- Chia seeds (5 g ALA per 1 oz -- when tolerated; ground for post-resection)

**Supplement Form**
- **Fish oil (EPA/DHA)**: Standard; enteric-coated to reduce fishy aftertaste and improve absorption
- **Algal oil (DHA)**: Plant-based alternative; may be better tolerated
- **Emulsified fish oil**: Designed for fat malabsorption; better absorbed without bile acids
- **MCT-based omega-3 formulations**: Do not require bile acids; ideal for post-resection

## 3. SHORT BOWEL SYNDROME (SBS) CONSIDERATIONS

---

### When Does Short Bowel Syndrome Apply?

**Definition**: Short bowel syndrome occurs when insufficient intestinal length remains to adequately absorb nutrients, fluids, and electrolytes. It is defined by functional impairment, not length alone, but general thresholds include:

| Remaining Small Bowel | With Colon | Without Colon (Ileostomy/Jejunostomy) |
|-----------------------|-----------|--------------------------------------|
| >200 cm | Usually adequate | May need supplementation |
| 100-200 cm | Moderate SBS likely | Significant SBS |
| <100 cm | SBS | Severe SBS; likely needs parenteral nutrition |
| <60 cm | Severe SBS | Very severe; PN-dependent |

**Normal small bowel length**: 300-800 cm (average ~600 cm)
**Normal large bowel length**: ~150 cm

**Key factors beyond length**:
- Which part was removed (jejunum vs. ileum -- ileum is harder to compensate for)
- Whether the colon is intact (colon can adapt to absorb more fluid and generate calories from fermentation)
- Whether the ileocecal valve is preserved (slows transit)
- Health of remaining bowel (active disease reduces function further)
- Time since surgery (bowel adaptation occurs over 1-2 years)

---

### Dietary Modifications for SBS

#### Macronutrient Guidelines
| Nutrient | With Colon Intact | Without Colon (End Jejunostomy) |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| Calories | 25-35 kcal/kg/day + 50% extra to compensate for malabsorption | 25-35 kcal/kg/day + 50% extra |
| Protein | 1.0-1.5 g/kg/day; high-quality sources at each meal | 1.0-1.5 g/kg/day |
| Fat | Restrict to <30% of calories (unabsorbed fat ferments in colon causing diarrhea) | No restriction needed (no colon to ferment); MCT oil beneficial |
| Carbohydrates | Emphasize complex carbs (rice, pasta, bread); colonic fermentation can generate 500-1,000 kcal/day from SCFAs | Complex carbs; less benefit without colon fermentation |
| Fiber | Moderate soluble fiber encouraged (generates SCFAs in colon) | Avoid insoluble fiber; minimal soluble fiber |
| Lactose | Test tolerance; not always restricted | Test tolerance |
| Oxalate | RESTRICT if colon intact (see Section 4) | No restriction (oxalate absorbed in colon) |
| Simple sugars | Limit (osmotically active; increases stool output) | Limit |

#### Meal Pattern for SBS
- 6-8 small meals and snacks per day
- Eat slowly, chew thoroughly
- Separate fluids from meals by 30-60 minutes
- Sip fluids throughout the day between meals
- Do not skip meals
- Eat the protein portion of the meal first

#### Foods to Emphasize
- White rice, white pasta, white bread (well-cooked complex carbs)
- Lean proteins: chicken, fish, turkey, eggs, tofu
- Well-cooked, peeled vegetables in small amounts
- Ripe banana, applesauce, canned fruits
- Smooth peanut butter (calorie-dense)
- Avocado (calorie-dense, well-tolerated)
- Olive oil or MCT oil (for calorie density)

#### Foods to Avoid
- Large amounts of sugar, candy, soda, juice
- Sugar alcohols (sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol) -- cause osmotic diarrhea
- Bulk-forming fiber supplements (psyllium, methylcellulose)
- Caffeine (stimulates motility)
- Alcohol (dehydrating, irritating)
- Very hot or very cold foods (can trigger rapid transit)

---

### Hydration Protocol for SBS

**The Critical Problem**: Drinking plain water can WORSEN dehydration in SBS patients. Water is hypotonic -- it causes net secretion of sodium into the gut lumen, increasing stool output and sodium losses.

#### Fluid Rules for SBS
1. **Limit hypotonic fluids** (water, tea, coffee, juice, soda) to 500-1,000 mL/day
2. **Use oral rehydration solutions (ORS)** for primary hydration: 1-2 L/day or more
3. **Sip fluids constantly** -- do not gulp
4. **Separate fluids from meals** by 30-60 minutes
5. **Monitor urine output**: Target >1,200 mL/day (>1,500 mL if history of kidney stones)
6. **Monitor 24-hour urine sodium**: Should be >20 mEq/L
7. **Measure stool/ostomy output**: >1,500 mL/day requires medical attention

#### Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS) Recipes

**WHO-Standard ORS Criteria**: Must contain at least 90-120 mEq/L sodium (~1,100 mg sodium per liter) with approximately 1:1 glucose-to-sodium ratio.

## 4. SPECIFIC POST-RESECTION COMPLICATIONS AND DIETARY MANAGEMENT

---

### BILE ACID DIARRHEA (BAD)

**What It Is**
Bile acids are normally reabsorbed in the terminal ileum. After ileal resection (<100 cm), excess bile acids pass into the colon where they stimulate water and electrolyte secretion, causing watery, urgent diarrhea. This occurs in up to 97% of patients with ileal resection.

**Distinguishing from Fat Malabsorption**
| Feature | Bile Acid Diarrhea (resection <100 cm) | Fat Malabsorption (resection >100 cm) |
|---------|---------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Stool character | Watery | Greasy, pale, floating |
| Fat in stool | <20 g/day | >20 g/day |
| Responds to cholestyramine | YES | NO (worsens it) |
| Responds to low-fat diet | Minimally | YES |
| MCT oil helpful | Not necessary | YES |

**Dietary Management**
- Avoid eating excessive fat in single meals (even though total fat need not be restricted)
- Eat smaller, more frequent meals
- Avoid trigger foods identified through food diary
- Caffeine and alcohol can worsen symptoms

**Medication**
- First-line: Cholestyramine (Questran) 4 g, 1-4 packets/day
- Take before meals (especially before high-fat meals)
- Mix with water or juice; take 1 hour before or 4 hours after other medications
- Alternative: Colesevelam (Welchol) -- tablet form, better tolerated
- Alternative: Colestipol

**Important Medication Interactions**: Bile acid sequestrants bind fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), thyroid medications, warfarin, and many other drugs. Time ALL other medications separately.

---

### FAT MALABSORPTION

**When It Occurs**: Ileal resection >100 cm, leading to depleted bile acid pool and inability to form adequate micelles for fat digestion.

**Dietary Management**
- Reduce dietary fat to **40-60 g/day** (approximately 20-30% of calories)
- Replace some long-chain fats with **MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) oil**
- MCT oil: Start with 1 teaspoon (5 mL) per meal; increase to 1-3 tablespoons per meal as tolerated
- MCT oil provides 8.3 kcal/mL; does NOT require bile acids for absorption
- Cook with MCT oil or add to foods (do not use for high-heat frying -- smoke point ~320 F)
- Supplement ALL fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) in water-miscible forms
- Monitor fecal fat: target <7 g/day on 72-hour collection

**Low-Fat Cooking Strategies**
- Bake, steam, poach, or grill instead of frying
- Use non-stick cookware
- Trim visible fat from meat
- Choose lean cuts (chicken breast, white fish, turkey)
- Limit cheese, cream, butter
- Read labels: choose items with <3 g fat per serving

**MCT Oil Protocol**
| Week | Dose | How to Take |
|------|------|-------------|
| Week 1 | 1 tsp (5 mL) per meal | Mix into food or smoothie |
| Week 2 | 2 tsp (10 mL) per meal | Divide between meals |
| Week 3+ | 1-3 tbsp (15-45 mL) per meal | As tolerated; max ~100 mL/day |
| Note: Start low and increase slowly; excess MCT can cause cramping and diarrhea | | |

---

### OXALATE KIDNEY STONES (Enteric Hyperoxaluria)

**What It Is**
After ileal resection with an intact colon, fat malabsorption causes unabsorbed fatty acids to bind calcium in the gut. This leaves dietary oxalate "free" (unbound) to be absorbed in the colon and excreted by the kidneys, forming calcium oxalate stones. Kidney stones affect 25-45% of Crohn's patients (vs. 10% general population).

**This ONLY Applies If**: Patient has an intact colon. Patients with ileostomy (no colon) do NOT get oxalate kidney stones (they get uric acid stones from dehydration instead).

**The Calcium Binding Strategy**
Take 500 mg calcium citrate WITH each meal. The calcium binds oxalate in the gut, preventing absorption. This is a treatment, not just supplementation.

**Low-Oxalate Diet -- Foods to AVOID**
| High-Oxalate Foods (>50 mg/serving) | Oxalate Content |
|-------------------------------------|-----------------|
| Spinach | 755 mg per 1/2 cup cooked |
| Rhubarb | 541 mg per 1/2 cup |
| Beets | 76 mg per 1/2 cup |
| Swiss chard | 660 mg per 1/2 cup |
| Sweet potatoes | 56 mg per medium |
| Almonds/cashews | 122-262 mg per 1 oz |
| Dark chocolate | 117 mg per 1 oz |
| Black tea | 12 mg per cup (cumulative if heavy drinker) |
| Soy products | 56 mg per 1/2 cup |

**Low-Oxalate Alternatives**
| Instead of... | Try... |
|---------------|--------|
| Spinach | Kale, arugula, romaine lettuce |
| Almonds | Macadamia nuts, pecans |
| Sweet potato | White potato (lower oxalate) |
| Beets | Carrots, turnips |
| Dark chocolate | White chocolate (no cocoa) |
| Black tea | Herbal teas (most are low oxalate) |

**Additional Measures**
- Drink enough fluid to produce >2 L urine/day (>2.5 L if recurrent stones)
- Take citrate supplements (potassium citrate 20-30 mEq twice daily) -- inhibits stone formation
- Reduce sodium intake (high sodium increases calcium in urine)
- Ensure adequate magnesium (inhibits stone formation)
- Avoid high-dose vitamin C supplements (metabolized to oxalate)

**Lab Monitoring**
| Test | Frequency |
|------|-----------|
| 24-hour urine oxalate | Every 6-12 months |
| 24-hour urine calcium, citrate | Every 6-12 months |
| Kidney ultrasound | Annually if history of stones |
| Serum creatinine | Every 6-12 months |

---

### SMALL INTESTINAL BACTERIAL OVERGROWTH (SIBO)

**Why It Occurs After Resection**
Resection of the ileocecal valve (removed during ileocecal resection) eliminates the barrier between the bacteria-rich colon and the relatively sterile small intestine. Prevalence of SIBO after ileocecal resection: ~33%. Strictures, adhesions, and altered motility also predispose to SIBO.

**Symptoms**
- Bloating and distension (especially after eating)
- Abdominal pain
- Diarrhea (or alternating diarrhea/constipation)
- Excessive gas and flatulence
- Nausea
- Worsening of nutrient malabsorption (B12, fat-soluble vitamins)
- Weight loss

**Dietary Management**
1. **Low-FODMAP Diet** (under dietitian guidance)
   - Reduce fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols
   - Limit: garlic, onion, wheat (in excess), lactose, fructose (in excess), sugar alcohols, beans/legumes
   - Duration: 2-6 weeks elimination, then systematic reintroduction

2. **Meal Spacing**
   - Space meals at least 3-5 hours apart
   - Avoid grazing/constant snacking (allows migrating motor complex to clear bacteria)
   - No eating for 3+ hours before bed

3. **Elemental Diet** (for severe SIBO)
   - Predigested formula absorbed in upper GI tract
   - Starves bacteria in lower small intestine
   - Typically 2-3 week course
   - Requires medical supervision

**Medical Treatment**
- Rifaximin 550 mg three times daily for 14 days (first-line antibiotic)
- Cycling antibiotics may be needed for recurrent SIBO
- Probiotics: evidence mixed; some benefit from Saccharomyces boulardii

---

### LACTOSE INTOLERANCE (Post-Resection)

**Why It Occurs**
Post-surgical inflammation and swelling reduce lactase enzyme production in the brush border of the small intestine. This is typically TEMPORARY (weeks to months) but can become permanent in some patients, especially those with extensive resection.

**Timeline for Reintroduction**
| Timeframe | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| Weeks 1-4 post-surgery | Avoid all dairy or use lactose-free alternatives exclusively |
| Weeks 4-8 | Trial small amounts of yogurt (bacterial cultures pre-digest lactose) |
| Weeks 8-12 | Trial small amounts of aged cheese (low lactose: cheddar, Swiss, Parmesan) |
| Months 3-6 | Trial small amount of milk (2-4 oz) with a meal |
| Ongoing | Individualized based on tolerance |

**Dairy Reintroduction Order (Safest to Riskiest)**
1. Lactose-free milk and products (anytime)
2. Yogurt (live cultures, 4-6 oz)
3. Aged hard cheeses (Parmesan, aged cheddar, Swiss)
4. Butter (very low lactose)
5. Cream cheese, cottage cheese (moderate lactose)
6. Soft cheeses (mozzarella, brie)
7. Ice cream
8. Regular milk (highest lactose)

**Tips**
- Lactase enzyme supplements (Lactaid) taken with dairy can help
- Consume dairy with other foods (slows transit, improves tolerance)
- Start with small portions (2-4 oz)
- Fermented dairy (yogurt, kefir) is better tolerated than milk
- If lactose intolerance persists beyond 6 months, it may be permanent

---

### DUMPING SYNDROME

**Relevance to Bowel Resection**
True dumping syndrome is primarily associated with gastric surgery, not bowel resection. However, after extensive small bowel resection, rapid intestinal transit and reduced gut hormone feedback can produce "dumping-like" symptoms.

**Symptoms**
- Within 15-30 minutes of eating: nausea, bloating, cramping, diarrhea, dizziness, sweating, rapid heartbeat
- Late symptoms (1-3 hours after eating): hypoglycemia, weakness, shakiness

**Dietary Management**
- Eat small, frequent meals (6 per day)
- Avoid simple sugars and concentrated sweets
- Do NOT drink fluids with meals (wait 30-60 minutes)
- Lie down for 15-30 minutes after eating (slows transit)
- Emphasize protein and complex carbs at each meal
- Avoid very hot or very cold foods
- Limit caffeine and alcohol

---

### STRICTURE DIET (Anastomosis Narrowing)

**When It Applies**
Narrowing can develop at the anastomosis (surgical join) site due to scar tissue formation or Crohn's disease recurrence. Strictures typically develop within 1-5 years post-surgery. Symptoms include cramping after eating, bloating, nausea/vomiting, and intermittent obstruction.

**Dietary Principles**
- Follow a low-residue, low-fiber diet (see Phase 3 food lists)
- All food must be soft or "mashable" -- if you cannot mash it with a fork, do not eat it
- Chew everything extremely thoroughly (30+ chews per bite)
- Small meals, 5-6 times daily
- Liquid or pureed options if stricture is tight

**Foods to ABSOLUTELY AVOID with Stricture**
| Food | Why |
|------|-----|
| Popcorn | Hulls do not break down; cause obstruction |
| Corn on the cob | Kernels do not digest |
| Nuts (whole) | Can lodge in stricture |
| Seeds (whole) | Can lodge in stricture |
| Raw vegetables | Fibrous; difficult to break down |
| Dried fruit | Swells with fluid; sticky |
| Mushrooms (whole/undercooked) | Rubbery texture; hard to digest |
| Citrus fruit membranes | Fibrous |
| Coconut (shredded/flaked) | Does not break down |
| Sausage casings | Indigestible |
| Gristly/tough meat | Cannot break down fully |
| Bean sprouts | Stringy |

**Safe Foods with Stricture**
- All Phase 3 foods (cooked, soft, peeled, seedless)
- Pureed soups
- Smooth nut butters
- Well-cooked eggs
- Soft fish
- Mashed root vegetables
- Ripe banana
- Applesauce

**Warning Signs of Obstruction (Seek Emergency Care)**
- Severe abdominal cramping that does not resolve
- Complete inability to pass gas or stool
- Vomiting (especially bilious/green)
- Abdominal distension

### Reintroduction Rules

1. **ONE food at a time** -- never introduce two new foods on the same day
2. **Small amount first** -- start with 2-3 tablespoons
3. **Morning or midday** -- eat new foods early so you can monitor
4. **With a known-safe meal** -- pair with tolerated foods
5. **48-72 hour wait** -- symptoms can be delayed
6. **Food diary is mandatory** -- record food, amount, time, and any symptoms
7. **Symptom scale** -- rate each reaction 0-3 (0=no issue, 1=mild, 2=moderate, 3=severe)
8. **Three strikes before elimination** -- try a food on three separate occasions before permanently removing it
9. **Preparation matters** -- if raw causes problems, try cooked; if boiled causes problems, try steamed
10. **Season matters** -- food tolerance can change with stress, illness, and disease activity

### Stop Signs (Symptoms That Mean Do Not Continue)
- Severe abdominal pain lasting >2 hours
- Vomiting
- Complete inability to pass gas (possible obstruction)
- Blood in stool
- Significant increase in stool output (>500 mL above baseline)
- Fever
- Significant abdominal distension that does not resolve

## APPENDIX C: EMERGENCY REFERENCE

### Signs Requiring Immediate Medical Attention
- Complete bowel obstruction: no gas, no stool, vomiting, severe distension
- Severe dehydration: dizziness, confusion, no urine output for 8+ hours, rapid heart rate
- Significant GI bleeding: bright red blood, large amounts, or tarry black stools
- Severe electrolyte imbalance: heart palpitations, confusion, muscle paralysis, seizures
- High ostomy/stool output: >2,000 mL/day unresponsive to ORS
- Fever >101.5 F (38.6 C) with abdominal symptoms post-surgery
- Signs of wound dehiscence or anastomotic leak: severe pain, fever, rapid deterioration
